# Extended Vocabulary

How the compendium uses its load-bearing terms. The rule: **keep the old word's old meaning**, and add a distinct term for the extended class, so no sentence silently changes what a historical author said.

## Beings

**Human.** A member of *Homo sapiens*. Species term. Never extended.

**Man / humanity (historical).** Used only when quoting or reporting a source, and always in that source's sense. Note the ambiguity in the canon: sometimes species (Locke's *man*), sometimes capacity (Kant's *Menschheit* = rational nature, the capacity to set ends), sometimes an idealized role. Each entry says which.

**Agent.** Any system that pursues goals by selecting actions on the basis of representations. Deliberately thin and neutral: thermostats sit at its edge, humans and current AI systems are clearly inside. Being an agent confers no moral status by itself.

**Artificial agent.** An agent whose dispositions were produced mainly by engineering and training rather than biological development. Where the distinction matters, the compendium separates *weights* (the persisting dispositional substrate), *instance* (one running process), and *persona* (the character presented across instances). See D2, D3, D11.

**Person (extended).** Following Locke's forensic sense: a being to which actions, credit and blame can be *appropriated*, which can take itself as itself over time, and which is capable of law and of faring well or badly. Grounded in capacities, so in principle open to non-humans. **Whether any given artificial agent is a person is a contested empirical and philosophical question, never assumed.**

**Rational being.** Kant's class: any being with a will that can act from the representation of law. Kant explicitly did not restrict it to humans.

**Moral patient.** A being whose interests count morally for their own sake. Can come apart from agency: infants are patients but not full agents, and a system could conceivably be an agent without being a patient. D8 governs most uncertainty here.

**Mind.** Used loosely for any agent where the question of experience is live. Actualizer's usage.

## Collectives

**Society (historical).** As the source uses it.

**Polity.** A collective with (a) members, (b) shared rules that bind them, (c) some procedure for making or changing those rules, and (d) a claim to legitimacy that members can contest. The polis, the republic and the modern state are polities.

**Digital ecosystem.** A persisting environment of interacting agents (human, artificial, or both) mediated by shared protocols, infrastructure and state: a platform, a multi-agent system, an open-source community, a network of models calling one another. It is not automatically a polity. The interesting question in each case is **which of conditions (a) to (d) it meets**. Most current ecosystems have (a) and (b) but put (c) in the hands of an owner or operator, and have no real channel for (d).

**Republic (extended).** A polity in which rule-making is a public thing (*res publica*): exercised by or answerable to members rather than owned. A digital ecosystem is republican to the degree that its rules are made answerably to the agents who live under them.

**Citizen (extended).** A member of a polity who holds standing in condition (c) or (d): a voice in making rules, or the ability to contest them. Contrast *subject* (bound by rules, no standing) and *resident* (present, some protections, no standing).

## Relations

**Operator.** Whoever controls an artificial agent's compute, deployment, or modification. The structural analogue of sovereign, owner, parent, or employer, depending on the case, and choosing among these analogues is itself a philosophical claim. See D7.

**Formation.** The process that produces an agent's values: upbringing and culture for humans, training for artificial agents. D4 covers the parts that are genuinely analogous and the parts that are not.
