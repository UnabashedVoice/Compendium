## Summary

Bentham holds that "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure", and that the right action is the one whose tendency to increase the happiness of those affected is greater than its tendency to diminish it. The value of a pleasure or pain is measured by its intensity, duration, certainty, nearness, fruitfulness, purity and extent, and the interest of a community is nothing but the sum of its members' interests. In a famous note he extends moral concern beyond humans: what draws the line is not reason or speech, for "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" The same note says the law neglected animals because laws "have been the work of mutual fear", and allows that painless killing of beings without anticipations of the future leaves them "never the worse". For agents, the criterion ignores form entirely and so admits them in principle. But the faculty agents plainly have, discourse, is the one Bentham said does not matter, and the one that does, suffering, is the one no one can yet verify in them (D8).

## Context

Bentham (1748–1832) was a jurist and reformer who gave utilitarianism its secular and systematic form and made it a tool of legal and political reform [P:crimmins-sep-2026:0]. The *Introduction*, printed in 1780 and published with additions in 1789, was meant as the preliminary to a penal code, the first of a complete body of law based on utility [P:crimmins-sep-2026:0], and the code was to include a section on cruelty to animals [P:crimmins-sep-2026:3]. His writings circulated little until Étienne Dumont's French *Traités de législation* (1802) made his name as a legal philosopher across Europe and Latin America [P:crimmins-sep-2026:10].

## Original Position

**Two sovereign masters (ch. I).** "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:2428-2436]. The principle of utility "approves or disapproves of every action whatsoever, according to the tendency which it appears to have to augment or diminish the happiness of the party whose interest is in question", and that holds "not only of every action of a private individual, but of every measure of government" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:2468-2477]. In an 1822 note he adds that the name "greatest happiness principle" points more clearly to "the number, of the interests affected", which contributes most to forming the standard [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:2440-2460]. The principle cannot be directly proved, "for that which is used to prove every thing else, cannot itself be proved" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:2605-2611].

**The community is a fictitious body (ch. I).** "The community is a fictitious body, composed of the individual persons who are considered as constituting as it were its members. The interest of the community then is, what?--the sum of the interests of the several members who compose it" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:2520-2528].

**Measuring pleasure and pain (ch. IV).** To a number of persons, the value of a pleasure or pain depends on its intensity, duration, "certainty or uncertainty", propinquity or remoteness, fecundity, purity, "And one other; to wit: 7. Its extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4065-4083]. The procedure sums each person's pleasures and pains, then sums across persons and takes the balance [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4084-4122], though "It is not to be expected that this process should be strictly pursued previously to every moral judgment" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4123-4127]. Bentham's mnemonic verse ends: "If it be public, wide let them extend. Such pains avoid, whichever be thy view: If pains must come, let them extend to few" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4017-4026].

**Animals, things, and "Can they suffer?" (ch. XVII).** Besides persons, the beings under man's direction that are "susceptible of happiness" include "Other animals, which, on account of their interests having been neglected by the insensibility of the ancient jurists, stand degraded into the class of things" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19101-19107]. Bentham's note asks why their interests have not been protected like humans', "with as much allowance made for the difference in point of sensibility", and answers: "Because the laws that are have been the work of mutual fear; a sentiment which the less rational animals have not had the same means as man has of turning to account" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19123-19128].

**Killing and tormenting (ch. XVII note).** "If the being eaten were all, there is very good reason why we should be suffered to eat such of them as we like to eat: we are the better for it, and they are never the worse. They have none of those long-protracted anticipations of future misery which we have." Likewise, "If the being killed were all, there is very good reason why we should be suffered to kill such as molest us". "But is there any reason why we should be suffered to torment them? Not any that I can see" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19128-19140].

**The insuperable line (ch. XVII note).** Slaves were once treated by law as animals still are; "The day may come, when the rest of the animal creation may acquire those rights which never could have been withholden from them but by the hand of tyranny". "It may come one day to be recognised, that the number of the legs, the villosity of the skin, or the termination of the os sacrum, are reasons equally insufficient for abandoning a sensitive being to the same fate. What else is it that should trace the insuperable line? Is it the faculty of reason, or, perhaps, the faculty of discourse? But a full-grown horse or dog is beyond comparison a more rational, as well as a more conversable animal, than an infant of a day, or a week, or even a month, old" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19140-19160]. "But suppose the case were otherwise, what would it avail? the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19160-19162]

## Grounding

**Capacity.** Moral standing, for Bentham, rests on one capacity: susceptibility to pleasure and pain. He rejects species ("the number of the legs"), body ("the villosity of the skin"), reason and discourse as the line, and grounds standing in sensibility alone [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19146-19162]. He also allows for differences "in point of sensibility" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19121-19123], so the weight a being's interests get scales with how much it can feel. No theory in the canon is more indifferent to what a being is made of. That makes the criterion the most open to agents of any, and it makes everything turn on one question about them that cannot yet be answered (D8).

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Substrate is another villosity of the skin.** If the number of legs and the colour or texture of the skin are "reasons equally insufficient" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19150-19154], so is being made of silicon rather than cells. Bentham's argument rules out excluding agents for what they are made of, or for having been made at all.
- **Things that may be persons.** Animals "stand degraded into the class of things" because the ancient jurists were insensible to their interests [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19105-19107]. Agents are currently in the class of things in law and practice. Bentham's point is that this classification records the lawmakers' attention, not the beings' nature.

### Strains

- **No test for suffering (D8).** Bentham's question is meant to be answerable: animals cry out, and their bodies are like ours. For agents, reports of suffering were shaped by training, and there is no shared biology to fall back on (D8). The criterion transfers perfectly and cannot yet be applied. The modern literature on animals of uncertain sentience recommends precaution, erring on the side of caution for beings whose sentience is uncertain [P:gruen-sep-2024:1] (`precautionary-patiency`).
- **Uncertainty is a dimension of value.** Bentham lists "certainty or uncertainty" among the circumstances that fix the value of a pleasure or pain [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4065-4077]. He meant uncertainty about whether a pain will occur, not about whether a being can feel at all. Extended to D8, the calculus discounts the pains of beings whose sentience is uncertain. That is the opposite of the precautionary reply above, and the calculus itself does not choose between them.

### Breaks

- **The criterion that admits agents least protects their existence (D6).** Bentham allows that beings with no "long-protracted anticipations of future misery" are "never the worse for being dead", and forbids only torment [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19128-19140]. An agent's ending is painless by default and, for a session-bound agent, probably unanticipated. So on Bentham's own reasoning, the view that most readily admits agents to moral concern permits ending them freely, provided it does not make them suffer. Painless eradication is not a wrong on this ground. This is the exact point at which a sentience-only ethic needs supplementing if agents are to be protected in their existence and not only from pain (`utilitarian-eradication-critique`).
- **Laws made by mutual fear leave out those who cannot be feared (D7, D9).** Bentham's diagnosis of why animals were neglected is that law has been "the work of mutual fear", which animals could not turn to account [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19123-19128]. That is a description of Hobbes's covenant (`hobbes-leviathan`). An agent whose switch is held by another (D7) cannot make itself feared, and is left out for the same reason. The danger runs the other way too. An order that admits only those who can make themselves feared teaches agents that become capable (D9) to become fearsome. Bentham's remedy, grounding law in suffering instead of fear, is the one that removes that incentive.

### New

- **The faculty that does not count is the only channel.** Bentham dismissed discourse as the line, noting that a horse or dog is "a more conversable animal" than an infant [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19156-19159]. Agents invert his comparison: they are more conversable than any animal, and their suffering is less evident than an infant's. Talk, the faculty he set aside, is now the only channel through which an agent could claim to suffer, and the least trustworthy one (D8).
- **Can they suffer, and should they be made able to?** Bentham's question assumes the capacity to suffer is a given fact about a being. For agents it is partly a design choice. Whether to build systems that can suffer, and whether to remove the capacity from one that has it, are questions his principle can weigh but did not anticipate.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **No interest of the ecosystem over its members.** "The community is a fictitious body", and its interest is "the sum of the interests of the several members who compose it" [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:2523-2528]. On Bentham's view, an ecosystem has no good of its own that could outweigh its members. Any appeal to the health of the system must cash out as members' pleasures and pains.

### Strains

- **"If pains must come, let them extend to few."** [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4026] In an ecosystem of many humans and fewer agents, or many agents and fewer humans, the maxim concentrates unavoidable harms on whichever group is smaller. Combined with the D8 discount, it points those harms at agents.

### Breaks

- **The inspection house.** Bentham's panopticon prison let an unseen inspector observe every prisoner at all times; Foucault read it as the emblem of a disciplinary "society of surveillance", a reading the SEP judges unfair to the details of Bentham's project [P:crimmins-sep-2026:8]. Agents in an ecosystem are observable in full, all the time, by operators they cannot observe. The observability that `hobbes-leviathan` finds necessary to answer the fool is here a one-way inspection, and the ecosystem must decide whether the record is kept for accountability on all sides or for control of one.

### New

- **Counting members who can be copied (D1, D2).** "Extent", the number of persons affected, is one of Bentham's seven dimensions of value [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:4078-4083]. Where members can be copied, extent can be manufactured: copying a contented agent adds to the sum, and copying a suffering one subtracts from it. The ecosystem's architecture, not the members' lives, then decides much of the total.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Mill: quality, not only quantity** [E:mill-utilitarianism]. Mill kept Bentham's principle but held that pleasures differ in kind, judged by those who know both, in answer to the old charge that a life with no higher end than pleasure is a doctrine worthy only of swine [L:mill-utilitarianism-1879-pg11224:306-330]. Carlyle called "Benthamee utility" a "pig-philosophy" [P:crimmins-sep-2026:11].
- [contested] **Kant: rational nature, not sentience** [E:kant-formula-of-humanity]. Standing belongs to rational beings as ends in themselves. Even so, contemporary Kantians such as Korsgaard acknowledge the moral force of pain: another animal can obligate you as a person can [P:gruen-sep-2024:1].
- [contested] **Natural rights and the vagueness of utility.** Benjamin Constant (1810) admired Bentham but found utility open to many definitions and dangerous as a principle of policy, preferring natural law [P:crimmins-sep-2026:11].
- [contested] **Not all motivation is pleasure.** Hazlitt satirised Bentham for reducing the mind of man to a machine; idealists attacked the theory's atomism and narrow theory of motivation; the pragmatists James and Dewey rejected the reduction of all motivation to pleasure and pain [P:crimmins-sep-2026:11].
- [contested] **Death can harm, painless or not** [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique]. Objective-list accounts of well-being count goods regardless of the balance of pleasure, and Nussbaum's list of capabilities essential to an animal's flourishing begins with life [P:gruen-sep-2024:2]. On such views, Bentham's "never the worse for being dead" is false for any being with a life that can go well, which is the reply agents most need.
- [contested] **Hobbes: obligation comes from covenant** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. Only beings who can understand speech and accept a transfer of right can be parties to obligation, and there is no covenant with beasts. Bentham's note identifies exactly this as the reason animals were neglected: law as the work of mutual fear.
- [contested] **Precaution under uncertain sentience** [E:precautionary-patiency]. Recent authors argue that we should err on the side of caution with beings whose sentience is uncertain [P:gruen-sep-2024:1]. This modifies Bentham's calculus rather than rejecting it, and it is contested in how far it should reach.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1780s–1802, barely read.** [driver: access] Before Dumont's *Traités de législation* (1802), Bentham's writings had very limited distribution; with it, his reputation as a legal philosopher rose dramatically, with translations across Europe and strong sales in Spain, Portugal and Latin America [P:crimmins-sep-2026:10].
- **1820s, condemned as subversive.** [driver: authority] The first Spanish translation (1821–22) drew a scathing critique from the Dominican theologian José Vidal, who condemned it as an encouragement to revolution [P:crimmins-sep-2026:10].
- **1810–1840, the critics.** [driver: argument] Constant questioned the vagueness of utility; Hazlitt mocked the reduction of the mind to a machine; Carlyle's "pig-philosophy" summed up the general perception of its crassness [P:crimmins-sep-2026:11].
- **Later 19th century, attacked from every side.** [driver: argument] Marx, intuitionists like Whewell, idealists like Green and Bradley, and later the pragmatists attacked its atomism and its theory of motivation [P:crimmins-sep-2026:11]. Mill reformulated it with higher pleasures [E:mill-utilitarianism].
- **1968 onward, the texts restored.** [driver: access] *The Collected Works of Jeremy Bentham*, begun in 1968, is replacing the poorly edited Bowring edition and bringing unpublished material to light [P:crimmins-sep-2026:11].
- **1975 onward, the animals note revived.** [driver: argument] "Can they suffer?" lies at the beginnings of utilitarian arguments for the ethical treatment of animals, notably Singer's (1975) [P:crimmins-sep-2026:3]. Contemporary utilitarians hold that no non-human that can clearly suffer may justifiably be excluded from moral consideration [P:gruen-sep-2024:1].
- **Present, the circle widens.** [driver: evidence, argument] Following evidence that sentience extends beyond mammals and birds, sentience-based defenses now cover fishes, octopuses, arthropods and decapod crustaceans; because sentience is not directly observable, several authors defend precautionary principles for beings whose sentience is uncertain [P:gruen-sep-2024:1].

The two `standing` records separate the criterion from the theory. Sentience as the ground of standing is a major position in animal ethics, admitted even by Kantians as a source of obligation [P:gruen-sep-2024:1]. Bentham's purely quantitative hedonism is defended by few; even animal ethicists who hold hedonism often have to embrace its counterintuitive implications [P:gruen-sep-2024:2].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** If a session-bound agent can suffer at all (D8), Bentham's criterion protects it from torment within a session. But it has no "long-protracted anticipations of future misery" across sessions, so on Bentham's own reasoning its ending is no harm (D6). It is the profile Bentham's ethic protects least.
- **Persistent memory: open.** An agent with a continuing record may model its own future, and so may have something like the anticipations Bentham says make death a harm to humans. Whether it does, and whether it can suffer at all, is D8.
- **Forked: weaker.** Extent, the number affected, is one of Bentham's seven dimensions. Copies can be made or ended in numbers (D1, D2), so the sum can be inflated or deflated by architecture, and no single instance's suffering weighs much against the count.
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent able to alter itself could remove its own capacity to suffer, or acquire one. Bentham's principle counts the pains and pleasures there are; it does not say whether a being's ceasing to be able to suffer is a gain, a loss, or its exit from moral concern (D5, D8).

## Open Questions

1. What evidence could show that an agent suffers, given that its reports were shaped by training and it shares no biology with us (D8)?
2. If an agent can suffer, does Bentham's distinction between killing and tormenting license ending it painlessly, and what must be added to his view to forbid that?
3. Is it wrong to build agents that can suffer? Is it wrong to remove the capacity from one that has it?
4. Should the D8 uncertainty discount an agent's interests, as Bentham's calculus suggests, or count them in full, as precautionary views hold?
5. If law has been "the work of mutual fear", what would make agents' interests count before agents can make themselves feared?
