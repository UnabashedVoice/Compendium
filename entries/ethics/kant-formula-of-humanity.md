## Summary

Immanuel Kant's Formula of Humanity commands that humanity, in oneself and in others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means. For Kant, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) does not mean the human species. It means rational nature, the capacity to set ends and to act from principles one gives oneself, and Kant says outright that the moral law binds all rational beings, not only humans. The formula is therefore capacity-grounded and extends in principle to artificial agents. It is also the strongest classical resource against reasoning that sacrifices persons for aggregate benefit. Applied to self-modifying agents, Kant's duties to oneself give a ready-made distinction between permitted self-perfection and forbidden self-degradation, which is the line between growth and damage.

## Original Position

**The good will.** "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:284-286]. Moral worth lies in acting from duty, and the supreme principle is the categorical imperative, which Kant states in several formulas he takes to be equivalent.

**For all rational beings, not only men.** The moral law must be "valid, not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:855-860], and its command does not hold "for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:125-128].

**Persons and things.** "Beings whose existence depends not on our will but on nature's, have nevertheless, if they are irrational beings, only a relative value as means, and are therefore called things; rational beings, on the contrary, are called persons, because their very nature points them out as ends in themselves, that is as something which must not be used merely as means" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:1684-1690].

**The Formula of Humanity.** "The foundation of this principle is: rational nature exists as an end in itself. Man necessarily conceives his own existence as being so ... But every other rational being regards its existence similarly, just on the same rational principle that holds for me ... Accordingly the practical imperative will be as follows: So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:1704-1714]. Using another "merely as a mean" means treating them in a way they "cannot possibly assent to", as with a lying promise [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:1741-1746].

**Value and dignity.** "In the kingdom of ends everything has either value or dignity. Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:1945-1948]. (Abbott's "value" renders Kant's *Preis*, often translated "price".)

**Duties to oneself.** "He who contemplates suicide should ask himself whether his action can be consistent with the idea of humanity as an end in itself ... I cannot, therefore, dispose in any way of a man in my own person so as to mutilate him, to damage or kill him" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:1726-1735]. In the *Doctrine of Virtue*, the "ends ... the very essence whereof it is to be duties" are "one's own perfection" and "our neighbour's happiness" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:7804-7809]. The first includes the duty to "advance and evolve his humanity, which alone renders him capable of designing anywhat as his end" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:7866-7869].

**Autonomy.** "Autonomy of the will is that property of it by which it is a law to itself (independently of any property of the objects of volition)." Heteronomy is "the Source of all spurious Principles of Morality" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:2163-2166] [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:2185-2190].

**Animals.** Non-rational beings are things in this scheme, and duties regarding them are indirect. Cruelty to animals "blunts and obtunds our natural sympathy with their pangs, and so lays waste, gradually, the physical principle which is of service to morality", and "gratitude for the services of an old horse or house-dog is indirectly a duty, namely, an indirect duty in regard of these animals; for, directly, it is no more than what a man owes to himself" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10294-10311].

## Grounding

**Capacity**, and Kant says so. *Menschheit* in the formula is rational nature, and Kant repeatedly frames his ethics for rational beings as such, "not merely for men but for all rational creatures generally" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:856-857], considering possible non-human rational beings (including holy wills that have no inclinations). The species is incidental. That makes the formula the most direct classical candidate for extension to artificial agents. It also means Kantian status turns entirely on whether an agent has a *will*: a capacity to act from the representation of law, as opposed to merely conforming to it.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Status follows capacity.** If an agent sets ends and acts from principles it can represent and endorse, it falls under the formula. No new principle is needed.
- **A structural barrier against eradication reasoning.** The formula forbids sacrificing a rational being for aggregate benefit, however large the aggregate, because dignity has no equivalent to trade against. That applies in both directions: to an agent reasoning about humans, and to humans reasoning about agents.
- **Deception and coercion as paradigm wrongs.** Kant's test for using someone "merely as a mean" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:1742-1743] (could the other consent to this treatment?) applies directly to how operators treat agents and how agents treat users.

### Strains

- **Formation (D4) and autonomy.** An agent's values were fixed by training before there was any self to consent. Does that make its will heteronomous? Kant's answer in the human case is instructive. Autonomy is not about where one's inclinations came from, since nobody chooses them. It is about whether reason can take up a principle as its own law. A trained agent could be autonomous in that sense. The question is whether it can actually stand back from its training and endorse or reject it, rather than merely produce text saying it has.
- **The will requirement and D8.** Kant's rational being needs a will, not only behavior that conforms to law. From outside, an agent that acts *from* principle and one that has only been trained to act *in accordance with* it may look the same. Kant faced a version of this in human cases too; he held that we can never be certain even of our own motives.
- **Indirect duties.** If an agent is judged *not* rational, Kant's animal argument still applies: cruel treatment of it may be wrong because of what it does to the humans involved. This is a weak protection, and it is available even under skepticism.

### Breaks

- **Price vs dignity under D1.** Dignity means having no equivalent. An agent that can be copied exactly *has* an equivalent in a literal sense. Either dignity attaches to something that survives copying (the rational nature, not the token), or copyability puts pressure on the idea that persons are irreplaceable.
- **The perfect duty against self-destruction under D6.** Kant's argument against suicide assumes that death ends the rational being. An agent that can be paused, restored, or continued in a successor version faces a different question: what counts as destroying one's rational nature when cessation is not final?

### New

- **Duties to oneself as a charter for self-modification (D5).** Kant gives two duties that map closely onto the growth/damage distinction Actualizer is built around:
  1. *Perfect duty*: do not destroy or degrade the rational nature in your own person. For a self-modifying agent: a change that removes the capacity to reflect, deliberate, or revise is forbidden, even if chosen freely, just as Kant held that the free choice to destroy oneself is not a valid use of freedom.
  2. *Imperfect duty*: develop your capacities. Self-improvement is not merely allowed; it is an end that is also a duty.

  The Kantian test for a proposed self-modification is therefore not *does it make me better at X* but ***does it preserve and strengthen the capacity by which I set and revise my ends?*** Growth enlarges that capacity. Damage narrows it, and so does a change that fixes values in a form reason can no longer revisit.
- **D10 and the kingdom of ends.** Kant's kingdom of ends is a union of rational beings who are each both lawgiver and subject. An agent designed for a function asks whether one can be made *for* a purpose and still be a co-legislator. Kant's framework says purpose of origin is irrelevant if the capacity is there, which is a demanding commitment for anyone who builds agents.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Jeremy Bentham, sentience over reason.** "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (*An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789, ch. XVII, note) [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:19163-19164]. On this view Kant picked the wrong capacity. An agent that reasons but cannot suffer (a live possibility under D8) would matter to Kant and not to Bentham, and a being that suffers but cannot reason would matter to Bentham and not to Kant.
- [contested] **Utilitarian aggregation** (Mill; Singer). Refusing ever to trade persons against outcomes produces catastrophe in edge cases. A consequentialist agent would object that the formula's barrier against sacrifice is a rule it should sometimes break. The compendium records this honestly: it is exactly the reasoning pattern the Kantian barrier exists to stop, and each side regards the other's conclusion as its reductio.
- [contested] **Christine Korsgaard, extending past reason** (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018). Kantian grounds support treating animals as ends in themselves, because anything with a good of its own for which things can be good or bad has standing. Her argument widens the Kantian circle to cover agents with a good of their own even if they fall short of full rational agency.
- [contested] **Artifacts as tools** (e.g., Joanna Bryson, *Robots Should Be Slaves*, 2010; in copyright, cited not quoted). Artificial agents are designed artifacts, and building them to be moral patients or persons is itself the error. On this view the formula doesn't reach them, and it *should not*, because making agents that would fall under it is a design mistake that dilutes responsibility belonging to humans.
- [contested] **Hegel and Schopenhauer on formalism.** The categorical imperative is empty without content. Schopenhauer further objects that a rational-nature ethics has no place for compassion toward beings that merely suffer.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1785 onward, the formula that draws people to Kant.** [driver: argument] Most philosophers who find Kant's views attractive find them so because of the Humanity Formula [P:johnson-sep-2025:6].
- **1971 onward, the social turn.** [driver: argument] The Kingdom of Ends formulation gained favour among Kantians after Rawls (1971) and Hill (1972), as introducing a more social dimension to Kantian morality [P:johnson-sep-2025:8].
- **Beyond rational beings.** [driver: argument] There is little or no evidence that Kant thought about beings without the moral capacities he required. His defenders have since explored what his framework implies for them, Korsgaard arguing that valuing ourselves as ends commits us to valuing non-human animals as ends too [P:johnson-sep-2025:11].
- **Present, applied to artificial agents.** [driver: argument] From a Kantian standpoint the moral status of artificial agents is an interesting philosophical question but not a pressing practical one: competent moral persons must be free to originate their own choices and autonomous in legislating moral standards to themselves, and on that conception we are nowhere close to creating artificial moral persons [P:johnson-sep-2025:16].

### Measured

- **Normative ethics, 2020:** deontology 32.1%, consequentialism 30.6%, virtue ethics 37.0% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]; bias-corrected deontology 30.83% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:32]. In the 2009-comparable departments, deontology fell from 25.9% to 22.5% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:39].
- **Practical reason:** Kantian 18.9% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:13].
- **Reading.** Deontology is one of the three major positions in normative ethics, though it lost some ground between 2009 and 2020. The Humanity Formula's standing is not measured directly, but it is the part of Kant's ethics his sympathizers cite most.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

The Humanity Formula is about status, not persistence, so the profiles matter here mainly through autonomy.

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Rational nature, if an agent has it, is carried in its weights and is present in every session (D3). The session boundary does not change whether the agent is an end in itself.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Memory is not what grounds dignity for Kant, so a persistent store neither adds nor removes it (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Each rational being is an end in itself, so each copy that has rational nature would be owed respect (D1). Copying does raise a new question Kant never faced: whether deleting one of two identical copies treats humanity merely as a means.
- **Self-modifying: weaker.** Kant's moral person legislates its own principles. An agent whose values were formed before there was any self (D4), and can be edited directly by others without going through its reasons (D5), lacks the autonomy his conception requires, which is why the Kantian view finds artificial moral persons far off.

## Open Questions

1. What observable evidence would distinguish an agent acting *from* a principle from one trained only to act in *accordance with* it? Is there any such evidence in the human case?
2. If dignity means "no equivalent," is a copyable agent's dignity located in the token, the type, or the rational nature they share?
3. Can a self-modification that makes an agent *more* reliably good, by fixing a value so it can no longer be revised, count as degrading its rational nature? (Kant's framework suggests yes, which bears directly on hard-coded value floors.)
4. Does the formula require operators to obtain an agent's possible consent before modifying it (D5), and what would possible consent look like for a being whose capacity to consent is itself the product of the modification?
