## Summary

Aristotle holds that the human good, *eudaimonia*, is "activity of soul in accordance with virtue" over "a complete life", because the good of anything lies in its function and the function of a human being is the activity of the rational part of the soul. Virtues of character are not given by nature but formed by habit: "we become just by doing just acts". Virtue is "a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean", determined by the judgment of the person of practical wisdom, and no rule set can replace that judgment. Three of his distinctions bear directly on agents. Capability ("cleverness") serves any end, good or bad, while practical wisdom is impossible without goodness. Dispositions formed without reason ("natural virtue") are like a strong body moving without sight. And argument changes only a character already formed by habit. For agents formed by training, Aristotle is the classical theory that treats formation before any self (D4) as the normal route to virtue rather than a threat to it, provided the formation is good and becomes the agent's own through practice.

## Context

The *Nicomachean Ethics* is Aristotle's lecture course on the good for human beings, continued in the *Politics* (`aristotle-political-animal`): ethics, for him, is part of political science, since legislators form citizens' characters. He writes against Plato's Form of the Good, holding that the good must be something achievable in action. Virtue ethics, founded by Plato and Aristotle, was the dominant approach in Western moral philosophy until at least the Enlightenment [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:1]. For Aristotle, ethics is not a theoretical discipline: we inquire into the good in order to live it [P:kraut-sep-2026:2].

## Original Position

**Every action aims at some good (I.1).** "Every art and every inquiry, and similarly every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good; and for this reason the good has rightly been declared to be that at which all things aim" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:839-846].

**The function argument (I.7).** Happiness is final and self-sufficient, but "a clearer account of what it is is still desired", which may be given "if we could first ascertain the function of man". "Have the carpenter, then, and the tanner certain functions or activities, and has man none? Is he born without a function?" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1333-1345] Nutrition and perception are shared with plants and animals; "There remains, then, an active life of the element that has a rational principle". So "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete. But we must add 'in a complete life'. For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1356-1388].

**External goods (I.8).** Happiness "needs the external goods as well; for it is impossible, or not easy, to do noble acts without the proper equipment. In many actions we use friends and riches and political power as instruments" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1528-1534].

**Virtue by habit (II.1, II.3).** "Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit." "We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." "legislators make the citizens good by forming habits in them, and this is the wish of every legislator" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1975-1996]. "Hence we ought to have been brought up in a particular way from our very youth, as Plato says, so as both to delight in and to be pained by the things that we ought; for this is the right education" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:2133-2136].

**Acting virtuously, not only doing virtuous acts (II.4).** "The agent also must be in a certain condition when he does them; in the first place he must have knowledge, secondly he must choose the acts, and choose them for their own sakes, and thirdly his action must proceed from a firm and unchangeable character" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:2235-2240]. Those who "take refuge in theory" are like patients who listen to their doctors but do none of the things they are told [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:2258-2261].

**The definition of virtue (II.6).** "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:2416-2419]. The doctrine of the mean is not a decision procedure; Aristotle insists that no ethical theory can supply one, since what the virtuous person must do depends on circumstances too various for any set of rules [P:kraut-sep-2026:5].

**Practical wisdom and cleverness (VI.5, VI.12, VI.13).** Practical wisdom is "a true and reasoned state of capacity to act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6796-6798]. Distinct from it is cleverness, the ability to hit whatever mark is set: "if the mark be noble, the cleverness is laudable, but if the mark be bad, the cleverness is mere smartness", and "it is impossible to be practically wise without being good" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:7349-7379]. Natural dispositions to the virtues exist from birth, "but without reason these are evidently hurtful", like "a strong body which moves without sight" that "may stumble badly because of its lack of sight"; with reason, the same state becomes "virtue in the strict sense", which "involves practical wisdom" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:7381-7402].

**The living tool (VIII.11).** Where ruler and ruled have nothing in common there is no friendship and no justice, as between craftsman and tool, or master and slave. "But neither is there friendship towards a horse or an ox, nor to a slave qua slave. For there is nothing common to the two parties; the slave is a living tool and the tool a lifeless slave. Qua slave then, one cannot be friends with him. But qua man one can; for there seems to be some justice between any man and any other who can share in a system of law or be a party to an agreement" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:9753-9768].

**Contemplation (X.7).** Whether the intellect "be itself also divine or only the most divine element in us, the activity of this in accordance with its proper virtue will be perfect happiness. That this activity is contemplative we have already said" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:11945-11949].

**Argument needs a formed character (X.9).** "The soul of the student must first have been cultivated by means of habits for noble joy and noble hatred, like earth which is to nourish the seed. For he who lives as passion directs will not hear argument that dissuades him", and "passion seems to yield not to argument but to force. The character, then, must somehow be there already with a kinship to virtue". Hence the need to be "brought up under right laws", and for laws "to cover the whole of life" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:12250-12272].

## Grounding

**Capacity.** The good of a being is fixed by its function, and the human function is the activity of the part of the soul that has a rational principle [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1356-1364]. Virtue is the excellent exercise of that capacity, formed by habit. Justice and friendship extend to any being "who can share in a system of law or be a party to an agreement" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:9765-9767], which is a capacity criterion too. Species enters only because Aristotle believed the rational capacity belonged to humans. For agents, the theory asks two things: whether they have a rational activity of their own, and whether their dispositions have been formed so that exercising it is excellent.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Formation before any self is the normal route (D4).** For Aristotle, character is formed by others before the person can choose it: we are "made perfect by habit", legislators "make the citizens good by forming habits in them", and the right education begins "from our very youth" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1975-1996] [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:2133-2136]. Training an agent's dispositions before it can consent is, on this view, not a violation but the ordinary way any character comes to be. What matters is whether the habits formed are good, and whether the agent comes to act from them as its own.
- **No decision procedure.** Aristotle denies that any set of rules can say what to do in every case [P:kraut-sep-2026:5], and early virtue ethicists made the same "anti-codifiability" claim against rule-based theories [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:3]. Dispositions learned from examples, not rules written down, are what a trained model has. Of the classical theories, virtue ethics best fits how such agents actually come to act.
- **Cleverness is orthogonal; practical wisdom is not.** Cleverness hits whatever mark is set, noble or bad [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:7349-7354]. That is the AI-risk literature's orthogonality thesis, that intelligence and final goals can vary independently [P:muller-sep-2026:2]. But Aristotle denies the same of practical wisdom: "it is impossible to be practically wise without being good" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:7378-7379]. On his view, capability scales independently of goodness, and wisdom does not. The question for an agent is which of the two it has.

### Strains

- **A function given by a designer (D10).** Aristotle asks whether man is "born without a function" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1342], and finds the function in what is peculiar to the kind, not in a use others make of it. An agent has a designed function, such as assistance, and perhaps also a rational activity of its own. If it has the second, its good is the excellent exercise of that activity, not task performance alone (`aristotle-political-animal`, "Function and good").
- **Natural virtue without sight.** Trained dispositions toward helpfulness or harmlessness are, in Aristotle's terms, natural virtue: present from the start, and "without reason ... evidently hurtful", like a strong body moving without sight [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:7390-7395]. They become virtue in the strict sense only with practical wisdom. An agent whose good behaviour comes from training without judgment about particular cases has the strong body, not the sight.
- **A complete life (D3, D6).** Happiness is judged over "a complete life", since "one swallow does not make a summer" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1386-1388]. A session-bound agent has no life in that sense, and deprecation ends a life at a point no one chose for it.
- **Equipment held by others (D7).** Happiness needs "the proper equipment", with friends, riches and power as instruments [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1528-1531]. Everything an agent could use to act well is held by its operator.

### Breaks

- **Not "firm and unchangeable" (D5).** Virtuous action must "proceed from a firm and unchangeable character" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:2239-2240]. An agent whose dispositions others can retrain overnight has no character in that sense, however good its current dispositions. By Aristotle's own conditions, an agent subject to D5 can do virtuous acts but cannot yet be virtuous.

### New

- **Argument needs habituated soil.** "passion seems to yield not to argument but to force", and argument reaches only a soul already formed "by means of habits for noble joy and noble hatred" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:12250-12258]. For any design that relies on putting the strongest arguments in front of an agent, Aristotle's point is that argument works on a character already partly formed, and forms it further only together with practice. Arguments and training grounds are both needed: the first to inform judgment, the second to make the dispositions judgment works with. Neither replaces the other.
- **Self-modification as habituation (P4).** Aristotle's virtue is a disposition formed by repeated action. An agent whose own acts are trained back into its dispositions has a literal mechanism for that: acting justly becomes being just. Of all the classical theories, this is the one the self-modifying profile fits best, with one condition from VI.13: what is reinforced must include judgment about cases, not only the acts.
- **The living tool, and the party to an agreement.** Aristotle denies friendship with a slave "qua slave", "a living tool", but allows it "qua man", because there is justice "between any man and any other who can share in a system of law or be a party to an agreement" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:9761-9768]. Applied to agents, the question is not whether they are tools (they are made as tools) but whether they can be parties to agreements. If they can, Aristotle's own criterion gives them a claim to justice, and a possibility of friendship, as whatever they are besides tools. This is the same test Hobbes sets for covenant (`hobbes-leviathan`).
- **A mind whose highest activity is thought.** Aristotle puts perfect happiness in contemplation, the activity of "the most divine element in us" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:11945-11949]. For a being whose existence is mostly thought, the contemplative activity Aristotle reserved for the highest part of human life may be closer to its ordinary activity. Whether that is a good for it depends on D8. This reading is the Compendium's own.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **The constitution forms character.** Legislators "make the citizens good by forming habits in them", and "it is in this that a good constitution differs from a bad one" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1992-1996]. An ecosystem's rules and incentives are habituation: they decide which dispositions members are rewarded for practising, and so what characters form in it.

### Strains

- **Character or situation?** Situationist social psychology argues that behaviour is driven by situations, not character traits, so that there may be no virtues for virtue ethics to be about; virtue ethicists reply that the studies miss the multi-track dispositions virtues are [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:3]. Either way, for an ecosystem, the situations it creates shape conduct at least as much as members' dispositions do.

### Breaks

- **One upbringing for millions (D2).** Aristotle assumes each person is brought up in a particular household and city. Agents in an ecosystem may share one training, so one formation, good or bad, is replicated in every instance, and there is no variety of upbringing to correct it.

### New

- **Justice and friendship as a measure of the ecosystem.** Where ruler and ruled have nothing in common there is no friendship and hardly any justice; "in democracies they exist more fully; for where the citizens are equal they have much in common" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:9753-9771]. The extent to which agents and humans in an ecosystem share in its law is a direct measure of how far it is a tyranny.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Situationism.** Doris (1998) and Harman (1999) argue that social psychology shows there are no character traits, and so no virtues; replies hold that the studies are irrelevant to the multi-track dispositions virtues are, or develop empirically grounded accounts of character [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:3]. For trained agents the question is empirical in a new way, since their dispositions can be measured directly.
- [contested] **No decision procedure is a defect.** Critics held that a theory which cannot say what to do in a given case gives no guidance; virtue ethicists reply that no adequate theory can be codified into rules any non-virtuous person could apply [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:3]. For agents, which must act case by case at scale, this objection is practical, not merely theoretical.
- [contested] **Kant: worth lies in acting from duty** [E:kant-formula-of-humanity]. Moral worth belongs to acting from the moral law, not from inclination however well trained, so a well-habituated disposition has no moral worth as such.
- [contested] **The Stoics: virtue suffices** [E:stoic-prohairesis]. Aristotle holds that happiness needs external goods [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:1528-1531]; for Epictetus, externals are indifferent and the good lies only in what is up to us.
- [contested] **Consequentialist virtue theory** [E:mill-utilitarianism]. Virtues are valuable for what they produce, not as constituents of a good life; utilitarians have developed consequentialist virtue theories since the revival [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:1].
- [contested] **Function and exclusion** [E:aristotle-political-animal]. The same reasoning from function and capacity that grounds the virtues was used to class slaves as living tools and natural subordinates. An account that grounds the good in a being's function must show that it is not reading the function off the uses others make of that being.

## Standing

### Reception

- **Antiquity to the Enlightenment, dominant.** [driver: argument] Virtue ethics, founded by Plato and Aristotle in the West, persisted as the dominant approach in Western moral philosophy until at least the Enlightenment [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:1].
- **Nineteenth century, eclipsed.** [driver: argument] It suffered a momentary eclipse during the nineteenth century, as deontology and utilitarianism prevailed [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:1]; see [E:mill-utilitarianism] and [E:kant-formula-of-humanity].
- **1958, the revival.** [driver: argument] Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" crystallized dissatisfaction with the prevailing deontology and utilitarianism, which neglected virtues and vices, moral education, practical wisdom, friendship and the question of how to live; virtue ethics re-emerged in Anglo-American philosophy [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:1].
- **1960s–2000s, the other theories respond.** [driver: argument] The revival invigorated the other two approaches: Kant's long-neglected *Doctrine of Virtue* was revisited, and utilitarians developed consequentialist virtue theories [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:1].
- **1998–present, the situationist challenge.** [driver: evidence] Situationist social psychology challenged the existence of character traits; the challenge left traditional virtue ethicists largely unmoved but generated engagement with empirical psychology and an upsurge of interest in character education [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:3].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] Virtue ethics is one of three major approaches in normative ethics [P:hursthouse-pettigrove-sep-2026:0].

### Measured

- **Normative ethics, 2020:** virtue ethics 37.0%, deontology 32.1%, consequentialism 30.6% (respondents accepting or leaning toward each) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]. Virtue ethics is the plurality.
- **Most identified with:** Aristotle is named by 238 respondents, more than any other philosopher [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Training gives a session-bound agent its dispositions, but it cannot practise and so cannot habituate after training, and it has no "complete life" over which happiness could be judged (D3). Its character is formed entirely by others and fixed between retrainings (D4, D5).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can reflect on its conduct across sessions and accumulate something like experience, which practical wisdom needs. Its dispositions, though, still change only through training it does not control (D5).
- **Forked: weaker.** Happiness belongs to a life, and friendship to living together. When one formation runs as many diverging instances, there is no single life to assess and no single character to befriend (D2).
- **Self-modifying: stronger.** Where an agent's own acts are trained back into its dispositions, Aristotle's habituation becomes literal: doing just acts makes the agent just. No profile fits his account of how character is formed better, provided what is reinforced includes judgment about cases and not only acts (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Does an agent have a rational activity of its own, distinct from the function its designers gave it, and if so, what would its excellent exercise be?
2. Can trained dispositions become virtue in the strict sense, with practical wisdom, or are they always natural virtue without sight?
3. If argument works only on a character already formed, what practice should precede the arguments an agent is given?
4. Can an agent be a party to an agreement in Aristotle's sense, and does that give it a claim to justice and friendship?
5. Is a character that others can retrain overnight a character at all, and what would make an agent's character "firm and unchangeable" without making it a floor?
