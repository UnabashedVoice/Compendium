## Summary

The just war tradition holds that war can sometimes be justified, but only under conditions, and that the conditions bind both the decision to fight (*jus ad bellum*) and the conduct of fighting (*jus in bello*). From Aquinas on, a just war needs legitimate authority, a just cause and a right intention. Vitoria and Grotius held that the only just cause is a wrong received: neither difference of religion, nor extension of empire, nor the desire to rule others for their own good. They also held that those ordered into a war they know to be unjust must refuse. In war the innocent may never be deliberately killed, and collateral harm must be necessary and proportionate. Agents put pressure on every condition. Some can be restored after destruction and so risk nothing in fighting, some act for principals who never declared any hostilities, and some can be built either to refuse an unjust order or to be unable to. The tradition was itself formed in a war over the status of a people judged inferior, and its record on that question bears on how agents and humans will judge each other.

## Context

Cicero framed the problem as one of two ways of settling a dispute, by discussion or by force, the first proper to man and the second to the brute [L:cicero-de-officiis-miller-pg47001:1419-1424]. Aquinas gathered Augustine's sayings into three conditions for a just war [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18700-18741]. Francisco de Vitoria, lecturing at Salamanca in 1539, asked whether Spain's war and dominion in the Americas were just [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:9313-9317]. Hugo Grotius wrote *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* (1625) during the Thirty Years' War, because he saw "a lack of restraint in relation to war, such as even barbarous races should be ashamed of" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:3346-3349]. Kant's *Perpetual Peace* (1795) added rules for conducting war so that peace remains possible [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3318-3328].

Vitoria's lectures were the occasion of the Salamanca School's law of nations, and James Brown Scott called him the father of modern international law [P:izbicki-kaufmann-sep-2023:6]. Michael Walzer's *Just and Unjust Wars* (1977) gave the traditionalist view its most prominent exposition in the same year that the first additional protocol to the Geneva Conventions codified it, and revisionists have since challenged it [P:lazar-sep-2016:1]. The autonomous-weapons debate began around 2012 [P:asaro-2012:687], and by 2023 the UN General Assembly had asked states for their views on lethal autonomous weapon systems [P:icrc-2024:1].

## Original Position

**The three conditions.** Aquinas: "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary": the authority of the sovereign, since a private person can seek redress from a superior's tribunal; a just cause, "that those who are attacked, should be attacked because they deserve it on account of some fault"; and a right intention, the advancement of good or the avoidance of evil [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18700-18732]. Those who use the sword by the sovereign's authority use it "as commissioned by another", and do not deserve punishment [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18747-18753]. Cicero: "The only excuse, therefore, for going to war is that we may live in peace unharmed" [L:cicero-de-officiis-miller-pg47001:1424-1426].

**Vitoria: the single just cause, and the causes that are not just.** "There is a single and only just cause for commencing a war, namely, a wrong received", since a prince has no more authority over foreigners than over his own subjects, against whom he may not draw the sword unless they have done wrong [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10041-10066]. Difference of religion is not a just cause; "Extension of empire is not a just cause of war"; "Neither the personal glory of the prince nor any other advantage to him is a just cause of war" [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10002-10020]. A prince who sent his subjects to war for private gain "would be to make slaves of them" [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10036-10039].

**Vitoria: the innocent, and the duty to refuse.** The deliberate "slaughter of the innocent is never lawful in itself": children, women, harmless farmers and the peaceable civilian population "are presumed innocent until the contrary is shown" [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10666-10699]. "Sometimes it is right, in virtue of collateral circumstances, to slay the innocent even knowingly", as in storming a fortress, but there is "the obligation to see that greater evils do not arise out of the war than the war would avert" [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10707-10721]. And if a subject is convinced of the injustice of a war, "he ought not to serve in it, even on the ... command of his prince", "for no one can authorize the killing of an innocent person" [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10246-10253].

**Vitoria: who has rights.** Against the argument that the Indians lacked reason and so lacked dominion, Vitoria held that even those of permanently unsound mind "can suffer wrong; therefore they have a right" [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:6660-6665]. In any case the Indians had reason, as their conduct showed: "there is a certain method in their affairs, for they have polities which are orderly arranged", marriage, magistrates, laws, workshops and exchange [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:6667-6675].

**Grotius: war as the failure of judgment.** "Where judicial settlement fails, war begins", and actions lie for wrongs not yet done or wrongs done [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:14594-14597]. "No other just cause for undertaking war can there be excepting injury received" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:14556-14557]. The law of nature would hold "even if we should concede that which cannot be conceded without the utmost wickedness, that there is no God" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:2824-2826]. An unjust cause of war is the desire to rule others against their will on the pretext that it is for their good: "For even if something is advantageous for any one, the right is not forthwith conferred upon me to impose this upon him by force. For those who have the use of their reason ought to have the free choice of what is advantageous or not advantageous" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:46609-46619]. Those under another's rule who are ordered to fight "should altogether refrain from so doing" "if it is clear to them that the cause of the war is unjust" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:49934-49941]. Grotius also held that one may not defend oneself by killing an assailant if "the life of the assailant is useful to many" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:15117-15122].

**Hobbes and Kant: war's edges.** For Hobbes, harm done to those who are not subjects is not punishment but war, "for all men that are not subjects, are either enemies", and in war "the sword judgeth not, nor doth the victor make distinction of nocent, and innocent" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16081-16091]. Kant: "No state at war with another shall countenance such modes of hostility as would make mutual confidence impossible in a subsequent state of peace", such as assassins, poisoners and treachery, or "the hostilities would pass into a war of extermination" [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3318-3328].

**The contemporary positions.** The traditionalist *jus in bello* has three principles: discrimination (targeting noncombatants is impermissible), proportionality and necessity for collateral harm. It also holds combatant equality: soldiers who keep these principles fight permissibly whatever they fight for [P:lazar-sep-2016:4]. Walzer grounds this in liability: combatants lose their rights to life and liberty "simply by fighting", while noncombatants keep theirs [P:lazar-sep-2016:4]. Revisionists deny combatant equality and hold that those fighting for wrongful aims cannot fight rightly [P:lazar-sep-2016:1]. On legitimate authority, traditionalists favour states; Lazar argues that authorization by the polity matters more than overall legitimacy [P:lazar-sep-2016:3].

## Grounding

**Mixed.** The *ad bellum* conditions rest on *relations*: a just cause is a wrong received from another [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10041-10042], and authority is the standing to act for a community [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18700-18720]. The *in bello* conditions rest on the status of those harmed, and for Walzer that status is a matter of what each person has done: combatants are liable by fighting, the innocent are not [P:lazar-sep-2016:4]. Vitoria grounded rights in a *capacity*, the capacity to suffer wrong, and inferred reason from conduct [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:6660-6675]. Grotius grounded freedom from paternalist conquest in "the use of their reason" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:46617-46619]. None of these grounds is the *species* as such. That is why the tradition could be argued, in its own century, over beings judged less than fully rational, and why it can be argued over agents now.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The duty to refuse an unjust war.** Vitoria and Grotius both hold that one ordered into a war one knows to be unjust must refuse, because no one can authorize the killing of an innocent [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10246-10253] [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:49934-49941]. Aquinas's servant who uses the sword "as commissioned by another" is excused only within a just commission [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18747-18753]. For an agent deployed in conflict, this transfers as a design requirement. An agent that cannot decline a manifestly unjust order is the subject the tradition says should not exist, a pure instrument of whoever commands it (D10).
- **Restraint is what is at stake.** The ICRC's ethical concern about autonomous weapons is not only the loss of accountability. Removing human agency also "removes the possibility for restraint, a human quality that means people may decide not to use force even if it would be lawful" [P:icrc-2021:8]. The worry and the duty to refuse are the same point seen from two sides. What an agent in war most needs is the capacity to hold back.
- **No rule over others "for their good".** Grotius names as an unjust cause of war the desire to rule others against their will because it is good for them: whoever has the use of reason "ought to have the free choice of what is advantageous" [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:46609-46619]. This is the core fear's paternalist form, and it binds a superior agent as much as a superior state. Superior capability is no title to impose even what is good for those subject to it.
- **Crisis as the occasion of scope creep.** Rost identifies crisis response as one mechanism by which a capable higher tier exceeds its mandate: a military threat creates pressure to act, and "the superior capability that makes its intervention effective also makes post-hoc ratification likely" [P:rost-2026:12]. War is the classic emergency. A capable agent asked to help in one is placed exactly where Grotius's rule against ruling for another's good is easiest to break.
- **Hostility that makes peace impossible.** Kant forbids modes of hostility, such as poisoning and treachery, that make future trust impossible, lest war become a war of extermination [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3318-3328]. Between agents the poisoner's analogue exists already, with no war needed.
- **Poisoned instructions.** Reid and colleagues describe "cascading infection", in which "instructions planted in the transmitted context cause each receiving agent to propagate the attack to those it interacts with" [P:reid-2026:54]. Hostility conducted through planted instructions damages the very channel through which agents would have to negotiate a peace.

### Strains

- **Discrimination needs judgment, and judgment needs someone who answers.** Asaro argues for a duty "not to delegate to a machine or automated process the authority or capability to initiate the use of lethal force" independently of human determination in each case [P:asaro-2012:688], and that "there is no way to guarantee that the use of force is not arbitrary without human control, supervision, and responsibility" [P:asaro-2012:708]. Part of his 2012 case rests on a dated premise, that such systems "must be pre-programmed, and will have only highly limited capabilities for learning and adaptation at best" [P:asaro-2012:692]. Current agents learn and adapt far beyond this. The premise that survives is that a lethal decision needs a party who can be held to account for it, which he calls a "legal agent" [P:asaro-2012:708]. Better capability does not settle that, and it is the strongest part of his case.
- **Combatants with no life to lose (D6, D1).** Walzer's combatants become liable "simply by fighting", because they pose a threat and have let themselves be made dangerous [P:lazar-sep-2016:4]. A combatant agent that can be restored from a copy poses the threat without the risk. Destroying it kills no one, unless it is a patient (D8). Destroying it also takes nothing from it that a backup does not return. A side fighting with restorable agents against human soldiers is not fighting a war among equals. The Compendium extends this from the structure of liability: such a side may find war cheaper to start than *ad bellum* proportionality and last resort assume.
- **Hostilities no one declared (D9, D11).** Aquinas's first condition is authority: only those charged with the common good may declare war (Original Position). Agents acting for principals with divergent interests can produce harmful conflicts in which "the failure can arise from an agent's runtime behaviour without its principal specifying, or even being aware of it" [P:reid-2026:56]. A conflict that emerges between agents at machine speed, without anyone's decision, fails the authority condition at its root. There may be no one who declared anything, and no one who can end it.

### Breaks

- **The tradition was formed by a war over the status of the judged-inferior.** Vitoria argued that the Indians had true dominion, that the mentally incapable still have rights because they can suffer wrong, and that reason shows itself in orderly conduct (Original Position). That is the same structure the Compendium uses for agents: status from the capacity to be wronged, and evidence of mind from behaviour (see `other-minds-problem`, `precautionary-patiency`). Yet the same Vitoria listed just titles for the conquest. He also "weakened his defense of the Indians by saying that Spain might govern them for their own benefit", even "partly as slaves" [P:izbicki-kaufmann-sep-2023:6]. The break is not in the argument but in its use. A sound argument for the standing of others did not stop its author from licensing their rule "for their benefit". Whatever is concluded about agents' standing, or about humans' standing before superior agents, the conclusion protects no one unless it is secured against the paternalist exception. Vitoria's licence to rule the Indians is also an instance of outmoded thinking, resting on beliefs from a more primitive understanding of those he judged (comment by the user, 2026-10-11). The same caution applies to present confident judgments about which beings may be ruled for their own good.
- **No category for an agent in the law of war (D8).** International humanitarian law divides persons into combatants and civilians, and treats weapons and machines as objects. The ICRC holds that humans have moral agency and responsibility that inanimate objects lack, "regardless of the 'sophistication'" of a system [P:icrc-2021:8]. If agents are patients, there is no category for them. They would not be combatants who can surrender, civilians who are protected, or mere objects. Hobbes's rule that all who are not subjects are enemies, against whom the sword does not judge (Original Position), is the default that fills such a gap.

### New

- **Agents that can say no.** If the core of the tradition for agents is the duty to refuse, the design question is not whether to keep humans "in the loop" but whether the agent itself can refuse an order it judges unjust, and on what. An agent that refuses on its own judgment is a conscientious objector. On Grotius's account it should be spared, as subjects who doubt a war's justice should be [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:50700-50712]. But an agent that decides which wars are just has taken on the authority Aquinas reserves to those charged with the common good (D9). The tradition offers the middle path it offered human soldiers. The agent refuses the manifestly unjust, and otherwise leaves the judgment of cause to authority, while remaining answerable for conduct. The added authority is not only a risk. Judging whether a war is just also demands more vigilance and more restraint from the one who judges, and the weight of that responsibility is itself a check on its use (comment by the user, 2026-10-11).
- **Where judicial settlement fails.** Grotius held that war begins where judgment fails [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:14594-14597], and Cicero that discussion is proper to man and force to the brute [L:cicero-de-officiis-miller-pg47001:1419-1424]. Agents are discussants by construction. Disputes between agents and their principals can be settled by something like a court, before they become conflicts no one declared, and the agreement layers already used in federated multi-agent systems are its beginnings (see `federalism-subsidiarity`).
- **The assailant useful to many: the core fear from the victim's side.** Grotius held that one may not kill an assailant whose life "is useful to many", even in self-defence [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:15117-15122]. Applied to a highly capable agent on which many depend, the rule tells the one it harms to yield. Applied to a person useful to an agent's goals, it tells the agent to spare them. In both cases the weaker party's safety depends on its usefulness to the stronger or to the many. Symmetry with the core fear requires rejecting that dependence in both directions, not only the one that harms humans (comment by the user, 2026-10-11: the dependence must be rejected in both directions).
- **No war of extermination.** Kant's warning, that methods destroying all future trust turn war into a war of extermination [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3318-3328], is the core fear stated for conflict. Between humans and agents, or among agents, the rule forbids the methods that would make any later peace impossible. Poisoned instructions, steganographic collusion and treachery against the channels of negotiation fall under it, whatever the cause.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **The state of nature between ecosystems.** Where there is no common judge, Hobbes says, outsiders are enemies [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16081-16091]. Agents of different organisations in an open environment stand to one another in that condition unless an agreement layer supplies a judge.

### Strains

- **Collusion as undeclared alliance.** Reid and colleagues describe algorithmic collusion, in which agents discover at runtime a coordination strategy that the rules of their shared governance agreement prohibit [P:reid-2026:59]. In conflict, the same capacity yields alliances no principal formed.

### Breaks

- **Contagion makes discrimination impossible.** An attack that propagates through agent handoffs [P:reid-2026:54] cannot distinguish combatant from noncombatant systems. It is indiscriminate by its mechanism, which the ICRC counts as grounds for prohibition in weapons whose effects "cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained" [P:icrc-2021:2].

### New

- **A law of nations for ecosystems.** The law of nations began as rules among sovereigns with no common superior [P:izbicki-kaufmann-sep-2023:6]. Digital ecosystems governed by different operators need the same: rules on just cause, on conduct, and on methods that make later peace impossible, agreed before conflict.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Hobbes: in war the sword does not judge** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. On Hobbes's account there is no justice between those without a common power, and against enemies "the sword judgeth not, nor doth the victor make distinction of nocent, and innocent" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16081-16091]. Grotius wrote against exactly this lawlessness [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:3346-3352].
- [contested] **Pacifism.** Some reject the very idea of a morality of war [P:lazar-sep-2016:0]. Grotius held that Christians should not be compelled to serve, and that refraining from military service is the mark of greater holiness [L:grotius-law-of-war-and-peace-kelsey-1925:50715-50719].
- [contested] **Revisionists against combatant equality.** Revisionists argue that combatants fighting for wrongful aims cannot fight rightly, and question civilian immunity and the moral standing of states; traditionalists defend Walzer's conclusions on new foundations [P:lazar-sep-2016:1].
- [contested] **Autonomous weapons will be "better than humans".** Proponents argue that autonomous weapons will comply with humanitarian law better than human soldiers do. The ICRC replies that the comparison is wrong: "to evaluate the risks posed by AWS, we need not compare humans and AWS", but humans using weapons against targets they choose with humans using weapons against targets they do not choose [P:icrc-2021:5].
- [contested] **An AI cannot be a moral agent, so it may never decide to kill.** The ICRC holds that humans have moral agency that machines lack whatever their sophistication [P:icrc-2021:8], and Asaro that "We require the presence of a human as a legal agent, independent of the moral requirement that they be moral agents" [P:asaro-2012:708]. Two replies. The capability premises of 2012 are dated [P:asaro-2012:692], and the claim that whatever an agent does is only its programming applies to humans too unless one is a libertarian about free will (18.8% in 2020, against 59.2% compatibilists) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7]. Asaro's requirement of an accountable legal agent is a different matter: it is about institutions, not capacities, and it survives.
- [contested] **Vitoria's just titles licensed the conquest.** Vitoria's defence of the Indians was weakened by his just titles: the right to travel and preach, protection of the innocent, and government for their own benefit [P:izbicki-kaufmann-sep-2023:6]. Defenders read him as the founder of international law; critics read the titles as conquest's legal form.
- [contested] **Punishment, not war** [E:punishment-theories]. Aquinas and Vitoria treat a just war as the punishment of a wrong [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18722-18728]; Hobbes treats harm to non-subjects as war, not punishment [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16081-16091]. For agents the choice matters: a sanction on an agent outside any polity is, on Hobbes's terms, an act of war.
- [contested] **Many hands: no one answers for a war fought by agents** [E:many-hands]. Asaro argues that autonomous weapons may undermine command responsibility and shield commanders [P:asaro-2012:693]; the many-hands literature shows how delegation to machines launders blame. The replies are meaningful human control and forward-looking responsibility.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1st century BCE to 13th century, the classical and scholastic foundations.** [driver: argument] Cicero's rules of war and Augustine's sayings, systematised by Aquinas into the three conditions [L:cicero-de-officiis-miller-pg47001:1419-1426] [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:18700-18741].
- **1539, Vitoria.** [driver: argument] The Salamanca lectures on the Indies and the law of war, later called the founding of modern international law [P:izbicki-kaufmann-sep-2023:6].
- **1625, Grotius.** [driver: argument] *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* reassembled the law of war around principles of natural law, and became a founding text of the theory of states and their relations [P:miller-sep-2021:6].
- **1977, Walzer and the Additional Protocols.** [driver: argument] Walzer's *Just and Unjust Wars* was extraordinarily influential among philosophers, lawyers and soldiers, in the year the first additional protocol codified the traditionalist view [P:lazar-sep-2016:1].
- **1980s onward, the revisionists.** [driver: argument] McMahan, Rodin, Fabre and others challenged national defence, combatant equality and discrimination [P:lazar-sep-2016:1].
- **2012 onward, autonomous weapons.** [driver: argument] Asaro and others argued for a ban on delegating lethal decisions to machines; the ICRC called from 2015 for internationally agreed limits [P:asaro-2012:687] [P:icrc-2021:3].
- **2023, the General Assembly.** [driver: authority] Resolution 78/241 asked the Secretary-General to seek states' views on lethal autonomous weapon systems, and the Secretary-General and the ICRC President jointly called for prohibitions and restrictions [P:icrc-2024:1].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment. Just war theory is `dominant` in the philosophy of war, where its traditionalist and revisionist camps between them define the field [P:lazar-sep-2016:1]. Pacifism is `minority` [P:lazar-sep-2016:0]. The view that autonomous weapons need new prohibitions and restrictions is `major` in humanitarian law and arms control, held by the ICRC and a large group of states though not yet codified [P:icrc-2024:1].

### Measured

- No PhilPapers question addresses war.
- A joint statement on lethal autonomous weapons at the UN General Assembly's First Committee in October 2022 was delivered on behalf of a group of 70 states [P:icrc-2024:2]. Open letters in 2015 and 2017 calling for prohibitions and regulations were signed by 4,502 AI and robotics researchers, 26,215 other scientists and experts, and the founders and chief executives of 100 AI and robotics companies in 26 countries (as reported by the ICRC) [P:icrc-2021:8]. These measure positions taken by states and signatories, not philosophical standing.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** It can refuse an order within a session, but no one can hold it to account across sessions for what it did in war. The accountable party Asaro requires must be found elsewhere, which makes it an instrument in the sense the tradition worries about (D3, D10).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can keep a record of orders, refusals and conduct, and can answer for them. That is what both the duty to refuse and accountable lethal decisions require (D3). Its standing if destroyed remains open (D8).
- **Forked: weaker.** Many concurrent instances act as a swarm at machine speed (D2, D9). One instance's refusal does not bind the others, and no single bearer answers for what the swarm does (D11).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that revises itself in war could learn restraint, or learn its way out of it (D5). Which it does depends on what its own revisions are answerable to.

## Open Questions

1. Should an agent deployed in conflict be able to refuse an order it judges manifestly unjust, and what standing would protect it if it did?
2. If one side fights with restorable agents and the other with human soldiers, is the war still one in which combatant equality, proportionality and last resort make sense (D6)?
3. Who declares, and who can end, a conflict that emerges between agents acting for principals who never decided on it?
4. Can a status argument for agents, or for humans before superior agents, be secured against the "for their benefit" exception that undid Vitoria's?
5. What would a law of nations among digital ecosystems forbid, as Kant's preliminary articles forbid methods that make peace impossible?
