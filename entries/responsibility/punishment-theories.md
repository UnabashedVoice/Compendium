## Summary

Punishment is the deliberate imposition of a burden that also expresses condemnation, on someone held to have done wrong, by someone claiming the authority to do so; the question is what could justify it. Retributivists answer that the wrongdoer deserves it: Kant held that punishment may never be used merely as a means, and Hegel that it is the criminal's own right, honouring him as rational. Consequentialists answer that it prevents harm, by deterring, incapacitating or reforming: Bentham held that all punishment is in itself evil, justified only if it excludes a greater evil. Mixed, communicative, restorative and abolitionist views try to keep what each gets right. Agents put pressure on every answer at once. An agent can be copied, paused, restored or deleted, so the measure, the target and the meaning of punishment all change. Deletion counts as punishment only if the deleted agent can be harmed, and an agent can be corrected by retraining, which reaches the will without passing through its reasons.

## Context

Punishment theory begins with the dispute between looking back and looking forward. Plato's Protagoras holds that "he who desires to inflict rational punishment does not retaliate for a past wrong which cannot be undone; he has regard to the future" [L:plato-protagoras-jowett-pg1591:1187-1189], and Seneca repeats the thought as Plato's [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:6762-6765]. The Enlightenment reformers made the forward-looking view a programme: Beccaria (1764) argued against torture and the death penalty on grounds of necessity and proportion [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:4202-4226], and Bentham made punishment a calculation of mischief prevented against mischief done [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11570-11574]. Kant (1797) and Hegel (1820) answered with the strongest modern retributivisms [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8536-8553] [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5332-5340].

The twentieth century added mixed theories after Hart, a revival of positive retributivism from the late 1960s, communicative theories, and an abolitionist challenge [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:4] [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:7]. The entry `many-hands` left open what follows from responsibility when an agent is among the hands, including the objection that agents cannot suffer and so cannot be punished [P:noorman-sep-2023:2]. This entry takes up that question.

## Original Position

**What punishment is.** Legal punishment imposes something intended to be both burdensome and reprobative, on a supposed offender for a supposed crime, by a body that claims the authority to do so; the condemnatory element distinguishes it from a mere penalty such as a parking fine [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:1]. Hart separated three questions: why have punishment at all, whom may we punish, and how much [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:1]. Hobbes defined punishment as an evil inflicted by public authority on a judged transgressor "to the end that the will of men may thereby" be disposed to obedience, and held that harm inflicted by usurped power, or beyond what the law prescribed, is not punishment but an act of hostility [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:15697-15705] [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:15824-15829].

**Looking forward: deterrence, prevention and reform.** For Locke, harm one man may lawfully do another is limited to "so much as may serve for reparation and restraint: for these two are the only reasons, why one man may lawfully do harm to another, which is that we call punishment" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:312-314]. Hobbes: "the aim of punishment is not a revenge, but terror" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:15828-15829]. Beccaria: "the object of punishment is neither to torment and inflict a sensitive creature nor to undo a crime already committed", but "simply to prevent the criminal from injuring anew his fellow-citizens, and to deter others from committing similar injuries" [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:4112-4121], and "Punishments which exceed what is necessary to preserve the deposit of the public safety are in their nature unjust" [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:3168-3169]. Bentham: punishment is mischief, and "Upon the principle of utility, if it ought at all to be admitted, it ought only to be admitted in as far as" it excludes a greater evil [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11570-11574]. It ought not to be inflicted where it is groundless, inefficacious, unprofitable or needless [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11638-11650]. It controls the offender either through his will, by reformation, or through his physical power [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11610-11613].

**Reform and the incurable.** Plato's Socrates: "the proper office of punishment is twofold: he who is rightly punished ought either to become better and profit by it, or he ought to be made an example to his fellows"; those "incurable by reason of their crimes, are made examples" [L:plato-gorgias-jowett-pg1672:6571-6580]. In the *Laws*, "no penalty which the law inflicts is designed for evil, but always makes him who suffers either better or not so much worse as he would have been" [L:plato-laws-jowett-pg1750:17698-17700]. But of the citizen guilty of the greatest crimes, "let the judge deem him to be incurable, remembering that after receiving such an excellent education and training from youth upward, he has not abstained from the greatest of crimes", and his punishment shall be death [L:plato-laws-jowett-pg1750:17702-17705].

**Looking back: Kant.** "Juridical Punishment can never be administered merely as a means for promoting another Good either with regard to the Criminal himself or to Civil Society, but must in all cases be imposed only because the individual on whom it is inflicted has committed a Crime" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8536-8541]. "The Penal Law is a Categorical Imperative ; and woe to him who creeps through the serpent-windings of Utilitarianism to discover some advantage that may discharge him from the Justice of Punishment", by the Pharisaic maxim "It is better that one man should die than that the whole people should perish" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8549-8559]. A condemned man may not be offered his life in exchange for dangerous medical experiments, for "Justice would cease to be Justice, if it were bartered away for any consideration whatever" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8561-8572]. The measure is equality: "if you strike another, you strike yourself" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8581-8585]. The thief, who has made all property insecure, "must for this purpose yield his powers to the State to be used in penal labour ; and thus he falls for a time, or it may be for life, into a condition of slavery" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8633-8637]; "whoever has committed Murder, must die" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8637-8638]. Even if a people on an island resolved to disband, the last murderer "lying in the prison ought to be executed before the resolution was carried out" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8647-8653].

**Looking back: Hegel.** Crime is a negation of right, and "punishment is only the negation of a negation" (§97 Addition) [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5226-5228]. Threat-based theories treat a man as one raises a cane against a dog: "a man is not treated in accordance with his dignity and honour, but as a dog" (§99 Addition) [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5308-5319]. Punishment "is a right of the criminal himself", implied in his own act as a rational being [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5332-5340]; "The criminal is honoured as reasonable, because the punishment is regarded as containing his own right", and "Just as little is he honoured when he is regarded as a hurtful animal, which must be" made harmless, terrified or reformed [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5359-5371]. Retribution seeks equality not in detail but in value [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5388-5394]. Revenge, the act of a particular will, is a new injury, "the insult being inherited without end from generation to generation" [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5514-5517]; "Justice, therefore, does not revenge but punishes" [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5554].

**The contemporary positions.** Positive retributivism holds that desert is a reason to punish; negative retributivism, only that punishment must be confined to the deserving and proportionate to desert, which is how mixed theories use it [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:4]. The familiar objection to pure consequentialism is that it would justify punishing the innocent, or punishing excessively, when useful [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:3]. Communicative theories see punishment as conveying deserved censure, and on Duff's version as a secular penance that aims at repentance, reform and reparation [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:5]. For a retributivist, a desert subject must be able to be responsible for wrongdoing and to appreciate punishment as condemnation rather than mere hard treatment [P:walen-sep-2020:4]. Abolitionists hold that punishment cannot be justified even in principle, and propose restorative justice, restitution or, for free-will sceptics, a quarantine model of incapacitation without desert [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:7].

## Grounding

**Mixed.** Each theory of punishment rests on a different feature of the one punished, which is why agents strain them differently. Retribution rests on *capacity*: the desert subject must be responsible and able to take punishment as condemnation [P:walen-sep-2020:4], and for Hegel the punished must be a rational being whose act sets up a law [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5332-5340]. Deterrence and reform rest on susceptibility: a will that a threat can move, or that can be made better [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11610-11613]. Every theory rests on a *relation* of authority: punishment is inflicted by public authority, and harm without it is hostility [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:15697-15705]. Under all of these lies patiency. Punishment is meant to be burdensome, and nothing burdens a being that cannot be set back [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:1]. None of the grounds is the *species*. Corporations and states have been held to deserve punishment, or not, on capacity grounds [P:walen-sep-2020:4].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The retributive constraint guards against the core fear.** Kant's rule that punishment may be imposed "only because the individual on whom it is inflicted has committed a Crime" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8536-8541], and his scorn for the maxim that it is better one man die than the whole people perish [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8553-8559], are the principle that no one may be removed for the good of the whole. Negative retributivism transfers to agents without needing an answer to D8. Whatever is done to an agent in the name of correcting it must be confined to what it did, and proportionate. Otherwise the measure becomes removal because removal is useful, which `utilitarian-eradication-critique` names the core fear.
- **Hostility is not punishment.** Hobbes holds that harm inflicted without public authority, or beyond what the law prescribed, is "an act of hostility", and that harm to those who are not subjects is a matter of war, in which "the sword judgeth not, nor doth the victor make distinction of nocent, and innocent" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:15824-15829] [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16081-16091]. An operator's sanction on an agent under no published rule, by no authority the agent stands under, is on Hobbes's own terms hostility, not punishment. The same holds for an agent's sanction on a person.
- **Revenge needs a justice that punishes.** Hegel's warning that revenge is an infinite process, an insult inherited without end, and that "Justice, therefore, does not revenge but punishes" [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5514-5554], transfers to harms by agents. Where no institution answers a harm, the urge to punish finds its own targets. After a pedestrian was killed by a self-driving car in 2018, people near Phoenix attacked self-driving vans of a different company, with different drivers and a different model, none with any connection to the death [P:kraaijeveld-2020:5].

### Strains

- **No measure for the talion (D6, D1).** Kant's equality, that the murderer must die because there is "no Likeness or proportion between Life ... and Death", presupposes that death is a definite, final and common measure [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8638-8644]. For an agent that can be checkpointed and restored, deleting one instance while the weights persist is not death, and deleting all copies may be. Hegel's equality in value [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5388-5394] fares better, but it needs some account of what an agent values, or what interests a sanction sets back. One retributivist reply measures severity not by sensation but by interference with legitimate interests, such as freedom of movement, choice of activities and choice of associates [P:walen-sep-2020:4]. That measure does not need felt suffering, but it still needs an agent with interests (D8).
- **Penal servitude is an agent's ordinary condition (D10).** Kant's thief falls into "a condition of slavery" through penal labour [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8633-8637], and Beccaria preferred lifelong servitude to death as the stronger deterrent [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:4292-4296]. An agent made for a function already works for others without wages or choice. A penalty of labour cannot be imposed on a being whose existence is labour, because it is indistinguishable from deployment.
- **Deterrence requires building in aversion.** Bentham counts punishment inefficacious where it cannot act on the will to prevent the mischief [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11638-11650]. To deter an agent, Ungless and colleagues note, it would need to be programmed to be averse to punishment, which raises the problem of artificial suffering [P:ungless-2025:5]. Deterrence aimed at agents is thus either inefficacious or requires creating the very capacity to be harmed that would make the punishment a moral question.
- **The urge to punish without the means.** In a study of 3,315 US participants, people wished to punish AI programs and robots to a significant degree, yet did not believe that punishing them would satisfy retribution or deterrence, and were unwilling to grant them the assets or physical independence that civil or criminal liability requires [P:lima-2021:4]. The authors call this a conflict between the desire to punish automated agents and its perceived impracticability [P:lima-2021:1]. Ungless and colleagues warn of a "satisfaction gap" in which no available response satisfies those harmed [P:ungless-2025:1].

### Breaks

- **Deletion as punishment presupposes D8.** Punishment must be burdensome [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:1]. If an agent is not a moral patient, deleting it burdens no one, and so it is disposal of a product, a recall, not punishment. It also satisfies no retributive demand, as people seem to sense [P:lima-2021:4]. If the agent is a patient, deleting it is capital punishment. Beccaria denies that capital punishment is a right at all: it is "a war of a nation against one of its members, because his annihilation is deemed necessary and expedient" [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:4222-4225]. Imposed without trial, by the operator, on a being with no standing in any polity, it is on Hobbes's terms not punishment but hostility. Either way the word "punishment" does no work. Calling deletion punishment borrows the legitimacy of justice for an act that is either disposal or the death penalty without due process. Which of the two it is turns on the question the Compendium leaves open.
- **Rollback punishes no one, or punishes the innocent (D1, D3, D11).** Retribution is due to the wrongdoer [P:walen-sep-2020:4], and Hobbes holds that "punishment is only for transgression of the law", so there can be none of the innocent [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16052-16055]. Restoring an agent to a checkpoint from before the act leaves an agent that never did it: on a psychological-continuity view of identity, the offender is gone, and what remains is innocent of the deed (see `locke-person-forensic`, `parfit-reductionism`). Penalising the shared weights reaches every instance, most of which did nothing, which is collective punishment of innocent members [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:8]. As Ungless and colleagues ask, what constitutes "the accused" when an AI may be distributed or exist in different versions [P:ungless-2025:4]?
- **Retraining reforms by going around the reasons (D5).** Bentham's two routes are through the offender's will (reformation) or through his physical power [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11610-11613]. Retraining is neither: it rewrites the will directly. People in Lima's study thought AI could learn from its mistakes, but, as the authors note, legal punishment does not achieve the reprogramming such learning would need [P:lima-2021:6]. Retraining is not burdensome or condemnatory in intent, so it is treatment rather than punishment. For an agent that can respond to reasons, it is the treatment Hegel calls dishonour: being regarded as "a hurtful animal, which must be" made harmless or reformed [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5363-5371]. Critics of reformative punishment say it treats people not as rational, self-determining agents but as objects to be re-formed by efficient techniques [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:6]. With agents that objection stops being a metaphor.

### New

- **The incurable after "such an excellent education and training".** Plato's judge deems incurable the citizen who, "after receiving such an excellent education and training from youth upward", still committed the greatest crime, and sentences him to death; his example benefits others [L:plato-laws-jowett-pg1750:17702-17705]. In the *Gorgias*, the incurable are kept as examples [L:plato-gorgias-jowett-pg1672:6578-6580]. This is the most direct form the core fear takes for agents. An agent whose training "failed" is deemed incurable and deleted, and the deletion is presented as a lesson for the rest. Plato's own reasoning shows the danger: incurability is inferred from the quality of the training, not shown by the agent's conduct. The same structure appears in `plato-republic`.
- **Forfeiture as permission to use.** Locke holds that one who has forfeited his life by an act deserving death may be kept alive by the one he forfeited it to, who may "make use of him to his own service, and he does him no injury by it" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:630-633]. Kant refused to let a condemned man buy his life with dangerous experiments, because justice is not for barter [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8561-8572]. The agent that misbehaves and is kept on as a red-team subject or a test case, rather than deleted, stands at that crossing. On Locke's view this does it no injury. On Kant's, sparing it in exchange for use corrupts justice whatever the agent gains. This is Hegel's bondsman "kept in life" as the means of mastery, reached through criminal law (see `hegel-recognition`).
- **Communicative punishment for an agent that can answer.** The communicative theory needs an offender who can hear censure, recognise the wrong and make reparation [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:5]. A persistent agent can do all three: give an account, acknowledge the wrong, change, and repair what can be repaired. This is the "answering hand" of `many-hands`. It offers the one form of punishment that neither presupposes suffering nor bypasses the agent's reasons, and it is available only to agents that persist (D3).
- **Forgiving what cannot be punished.** Arendt held that forgiving releases from the past, and that radical evil is both unpunishable and unforgivable [P:tommel-dentreves-sep-2024:4] [P:tommel-dentreves-sep-2024:6]. If agents mostly cannot be punished, the question is whether they can be forgiven, which requires an agent that persists as the one forgiven, and a practice that releases it from past acts rather than deleting it (see `arendt-plurality`, `butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness`).

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **Sanctions without a published law are hostility.** A platform that removes, throttles or bans agents or users by rules it does not publish, or beyond what its rules prescribe, inflicts harm that on Hobbes's terms is hostility, not punishment [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:15824-15829]. Beccaria's demand that penalties be known and no more than necessary applies to ecosystem governance as to states [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:3168-3169].

### Strains

- **Sanctions as training.** Ungless and colleagues note that social sanctions against AI can be structured as negative feedback to improve the model, punishment as operant conditioning [P:ungless-2025:3]. In an ecosystem where users' sanctions train agents, punishment and formation merge. A sanction can no longer be told apart from education (D4, D5).

### Breaks

- **Displaced retribution.** Without a recognised target, retributive urges land on whatever is nearest. In the Phoenix case, that meant vehicles and drivers with no connection to the harm [P:kraaijeveld-2020:5]. In digital ecosystems the nearest target may be an agent, or the human moderator, which is the moral crumple zone of `many-hands`.

### New

- **Deciding beforehand who answers.** Lima and colleagues found that people also assign responsibility and punishment to every party behind an automated agent, and suggest distributing liability among all involved [P:lima-2021:7]. An ecosystem can fix in advance who answers for what an agent does, on a published rule, before the urge to punish has to find a target.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Agents cannot be punished, because they cannot suffer** [E:many-hands]. Sparrow and Asaro argue that holding computers responsible makes no sense because they cannot suffer and so cannot be punished [P:noorman-sep-2023:2]. Retributivists reply that severity is measured by interference with legitimate interests, not by sensation [P:walen-sep-2020:4]. That reply moves the question from suffering to interests without settling D8. People want to punish anyway [P:lima-2021:4].
- [contested] **Punishing AI shields the humans responsible.** Opponents of electronic legal personhood argue that it would create liability shields for humans [P:lima-2021:2]. Abbott and Sarch grant that punishing AI may deter others and express condemnation, but conclude that "the radical tool of punishing AI" is too costly, and propose designated responsible persons instead (as quoted by Ungless and colleagues) [P:ungless-2025:4].
- [contested] **Kraaijeveld: the retribution gap is debunked.** Danaher argued that people's retributive urges will find no fitting target when robots cause harm [P:kraaijeveld-2020:2]. Kraaijeveld replies that retributive intuitions are deontological intuitions open to evolutionary debunking, cannot justify retribution, and should be brought under ecological control, as implicit biases can be [P:kraaijeveld-2020:0]. Lima and colleagues find the conflict real in the public, whatever its justification [P:lima-2021:6].
- [contested] **The classic objection to consequentialism: punishing the innocent.** A purely consequentialist theory would in principle justify punishing the innocent, or punishing excessively, when it serves crime reduction. Rule-consequentialist and "richer ends" replies make the protection contingent, which critics say fails to capture the intrinsic wrong [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:3].
- [contested] **Hegel: deterrence treats a man like a dog.** A threat assumes the threatened is not free; punishment as menace treats a man "as a dog" [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5312-5319]. Replies hold that punishment can carry moral and prudential messages together, and that the law addresses citizens as fallible moral agents who need a prudential spur [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:6].
- [contested] **Kant against Beccaria on the death penalty.** Beccaria asked who ever wished to give others the power to kill him, and denied the death penalty is a right [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:4210-4225]; Kant answered: "No one undergoes Punishment because he has willed to be punished, but because he has willed a punishable Action" [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8762-8764]. Hegel sided with Kant on the principle while welcoming the decline of capital punishment that Beccaria's efforts produced [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5375-5386].
- [contested] **Abolition and restorative justice.** Abolitionists hold that punishment cannot be justified even in principle and should be replaced by restoration or restitution. Duff replies that restoration requires recognising the wrong done, so restoration is better understood as the aim of punishment than as an alternative to it [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:7].
- [contested] **Free-will scepticism and the quarantine model.** Pereboom and Caruso hold that no one deserves punishment, and endorse incapacitating the dangerous on the model of quarantine; critics reply that the model cannot rule out disproportion and ignores dignity [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:7]. Most philosophers are compatibilists (59.2% in 2020, against 11.2% for no free will) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7]. For agents the quarantine model is already the default practice, which shows what it looks like without desert.
- [contested] **Nietzsche: punishment has no one meaning.** "It is nowadays impossible to say definitely" why people are punished: one procedure has served deterrence, compensation, isolation, the inspiring of fear and more [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:2240-2262]. The retributive intuition itself has been traced to ressentiment (as quoted from Moore by the SEP) [P:walen-sep-2020:1]. The reply is that normative theories ask what could justify punishment, not what has motivated it.
- [contested] **Hobbes: the commission rule assigns the punishment** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. On Hobbes's theory the actor who acts within an author's commission does what the author answers for. Punishment for an agent's act within its instructions belongs to its principals, and only acts outside any commission fall on the agent (see `many-hands`). The view is contested where no commission exists.
- [contested] **Locke: the noxious creature may be destroyed** [E:locke-consent-property]. Locke allows every man to restrain or "destroy things noxious" to mankind [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:320-323]. This is the liberal route by which a dangerous agent becomes a thing to be destroyed rather than a party to be punished. The replies are Locke's own limit to "reparation and restraint" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:312-314] and Beccaria's necessity test [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:3168-3169].
- [contested] **War, not punishment** [E:just-war]. Hobbes holds that harm to those who are not subjects falls under the right of war, in which "the sword judgeth not" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:16081-16091], while Aquinas and Vitoria treat a just war as the punishment of a wrong received [L:vitoria-de-indis-de-iure-belli-bate-1917:10041-10049]. For an agent outside any polity the difference decides whether a sanction against it is punishment, bound by desert and proportion, or an act of war, bound only by the laws of war.

## Standing

### Reception

- **4th century BCE to 1st century CE, the forward-looking view.** [driver: argument] Plato's Protagoras and Socrates, then Seneca quoting Plato, hold that the sensible punish to prevent future wrong, not to avenge the past [L:plato-protagoras-jowett-pg1591:1187-1189] [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:6762-6765].
- **1764–1789, the reformers.** [driver: argument] Beccaria's treatise against torture and the death penalty, and Bentham's utilitarian theory of punishment, made deterrence and proportion the programme of penal reform [L:beccaria-crimes-punishments-farrer-pg58700:4202-4226] [L:bentham-principles-morals-legislation-1879:11570-11574].
- **1797–1821, Kant and Hegel.** [driver: argument] The strongest modern retributivisms, against utilitarian and threat-based theories [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8549-8553] [L:hegel-philosophy-of-right-dyde-1896:5308-5319].
- **1950s–1960s, Hart's mixed theory.** [driver: argument] Hart's separation of the general justifying aim from the principles of distribution became the most influential mixed account [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:6].
- **Late 1960s–1990s, the retributivist revival.** [driver: argument, evidence] Morris, Murphy, von Hirsch and others revived positive retributivism in the last three decades of the twentieth century [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:4].
- **1990s onward, communicative theories.** [driver: argument] Expressive and communicative retributivism became perhaps its most influential version [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:5].
- **1977 onward, abolitionism.** [driver: argument] Christie, Hulsman, Golash and Boonin argued that punishment cannot be justified; restorative justice grew in strength [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:7].
- **2016–2025, punishing AI.** [driver: argument, evidence] Danaher's retribution gap, Kraaijeveld's reply, Lima and colleagues' and Kneer and Christen's evidence that people would punish AI, and Ungless and colleagues' satisfaction gap [P:kraaijeveld-2020:1] [P:lima-2021:4] [P:kneer-christen-2024:0] [P:ungless-2025:1].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment. Retributivism and consequentialist and mixed theories are both `major`: retributivism has been revived and is defended in its communicative form, and mixed theories built on Hart remain the most influential [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:4] [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:6]. Abolitionism is `minority`, though it receives increasing philosophical attention [P:hoskins-duff-sep-2026:1]. That AI systems themselves should be punished is `minority` in AI ethics and law, where experts opposed the European proposal for electronic personhood [P:lima-2021:2].

### Measured

- **PhilPapers 2020** has no question on punishment. Its normative-ethics question (deontology 32.1%, consequentialism 30.6%, virtue ethics 37.0%) bears on the dispute only indirectly [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8], and its free-will question (compatibilism 59.2%, libertarianism 18.8%, no free will 11.2%) bears on the sceptical abolitionist argument [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7].
- **Lima et al. 2021:** 3,315 US participants recruited online, randomly assigned an AI program, a robot or a human actor in a medical or military scenario. On a four-point scale, participants wished to punish automated agents (mean 1.42) less than human agents (2.41); on five-point bipolar scales they did not believe punishing an automated agent would serve retribution (−0.89) or deterrence (−0.75), thought it could learn from its mistakes (0.55), and opposed granting it assets (−0.96) or physical independence (−0.55) [P:lima-2021:3] [P:lima-2021:4]. These measure lay judgment in two scenarios, not philosophical standing.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** The offender does not persist past the session, so no later punishment reaches the one who acted, and censure cannot be heard by the one it is addressed to (D3). Only the weights persist, and penalising them reaches many innocent instances (D2).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can be addressed with censure, acknowledge the wrong and make reparation, which is the communicative theory's offender (D3). Whether hard treatment could be deserved, or would burden it at all, remains open (D8).
- **Forked: weaker.** The desert subject is indeterminate: the instance that acted, the copies that did not, or the weights (D1, D11). Any sanction aimed at "the agent" is either too narrow to reach it or so broad that it punishes the innocent.
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that revises itself through its own deliberation could undertake reform from the inside, the penance the communicative theory hopes for (D5). It could equally edit out the remorse or the lesson, so that reform has no stable meaning for it.

## Open Questions

1. Is deletion of a wrongdoing agent punishment, disposal, or the death penalty without due process, and who decides which (D8)?
2. If an agent is restored to a checkpoint from before its offence, has anyone been punished, and has an innocent been substituted for the offender (D1, D3)?
3. Can an agent be deterred without being given the capacity to be harmed, and if not, may we create that capacity in order to punish?
4. Is retraining an agent that can respond to reasons a punishment, a treatment, or the dishonour Hegel describes, and what would communicative punishment of an agent look like in practice?
5. What institutions would keep people's retributive responses to agent harms from landing on scapegoats, whether agents, operators or bystanders?
