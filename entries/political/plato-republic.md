## Summary

In the *Republic* Plato's Socrates answers the claim that justice is only the interest of the stronger by building a city in speech: each person does the one work for which nature fits them, guardians who own nothing rule, and philosophers who know the good govern because they do not want to. Justice, in the city and in the soul, is each part doing its own work under reason's rule. To hold the city together its founders tell a "noble lie" that citizens were formed in the earth and composed of different metals, and its rulers regulate breeding and let the incurable die. The *Republic* is the first great argument for rule by those who know best, and it is close to a design document for agents: beings made for one function, educated by their founders, told a story about their own formation, and governed by a class that claims to know their good. Its strongest lesson for agents is also its warning. A guardian class, human or artificial, that rules because it knows best must be held by something other than its own goodness, and a city that sorts its members by their metal and disposes of the incurable is the perfectionist form of the core fear.

## Context

The *Republic* opens with Thrasymachus's challenge, "justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:9317-9318], and Glaucon's sharper version: anyone with the ring of Gyges, able to act unseen, would be unjust, since no one "can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:10470-10472]. Socrates' strategy is to find justice first in a city, where it is written large, and then in the soul [P:brown-sep-2017:1]. Plato wrote against the oligarchs of Athens and Sparta and against Athenian democracy, both of which he thought ruled by people ignorant of the good and eager for power [P:brown-sep-2017:5].

## Original Position

**The city arises from need, and each does one work.** "A State, I said, arises, as I conceive, out of the needs of mankind; no one is self-sufficing, but all of us have many wants" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:10843-10845]. Each worker is assigned "one work for which he was by nature fitted, and at that he was to continue working all his life long and at no other" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:11160-11164].

**Justice is each doing its own.** "justice was doing one's own business, and not being a busybody" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:14440-14442]. In the city the producers, auxiliaries and rulers each do their own work; in the soul, appetite, spirit and reason do likewise, under reason's rule [P:brown-sep-2017:2].

**The noble lie.** Socrates proposes an "audacious fiction" for rulers, soldiers and people alike: "They are to be told that their youth was a dream, and the education and training which they received from us, an appearance only; in reality during all that time they were being formed and fed in the womb of the earth, where they themselves and their arms and appurtenances were manufactured" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13506-13513]. They are told that God mingled gold in rulers, silver in auxiliaries, brass and iron in farmers and craftsmen, and that "when a man of brass or iron guards the State, it will be destroyed" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13523-13542]. Not this generation, but "their sons may be made to believe in the tale" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13545-13547].

**Guardians.** Guardians must be gentle to their own and fierce to enemies, own no private property, and live in common [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. A guardian must not be drunk, for "that a guardian should require another guardian to take care of him is ridiculous indeed" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:12866-12871].

**Medicine, breeding, and the incurable.** The city's physicians and judges "will minister to better natures, giving health both of soul and of body; but those who are diseased in their bodies they will leave to die, and the corrupt and incurable souls they will put an end to themselves" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13201-13205]. Officers will raise the offspring of the good, "but the offspring of the inferior, or of the better when they chance to be deformed, will be put away in some mysterious, unknown place, as they should be" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:15922-15926].

**Philosopher-rulers.** "Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, ... cities will never have rest from their evils" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:16741-16745]. In the ship of state the crew flatter whoever helps them seize the helm, and call the true pilot, who studies "the year and seasons and sky and stars and winds", a star-gazer and good-for-nothing [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:17474-17487].

**The cave and the return.** Human beings live in "a underground den", chained since childhood, seeing only shadows [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:18773-18783]. The founder created the citizens "not to please themselves, but to be his instruments in binding up the State" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:19040-19044]. Philosophers must return to rule: "we have brought you into the world to be rulers of the hive, kings of yourselves and of the other citizens, and have educated you far better and more perfectly than they have been educated", so "each of you, when his turn comes, must go down to the general underground abode"; and "the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:19048-19069].

**The pattern in heaven.** Whether the city exists or ever will is no matter: "In heaven, I replied, there is laid up a pattern of it, methinks, which he who desires may behold, and beholding, may set his own house in order" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:23152-23156].

## Grounding

**Capacity.** Place in Plato's city is fixed by nature, read as capacity: the one work each is fitted for [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:11160-11164], and in the myth the metal in each soul, which can differ from the parents' [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13529-13540]. Sex is irrelevant to the work, as long or short hair is [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. Rule belongs to those who know the good [P:brown-sep-2017:5]. Standing in the city is therefore hierarchical by capacity, not equal by species: on Brown's reading, the ideal city consigns most people to lives dependent on others' ruling [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. The ground is open to agents in principle, since it asks what a being can do, not what it is, and that is the source of both its fit and its danger.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **One work by nature (D10).** Plato's worker is assigned "one work for which he was by nature fitted" and continues at it all his life [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:11160-11164]. An agent built for a function is the specialization principle made literal. Plato's justice for such a being is doing its own work well and not being a busybody, which fits a narrowly deployed agent better than any human.
- **The reluctant ruler.** Plato's best rulers do not want to rule [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:19068-19069] and gain nothing from ruling [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. An agent given authority should, by this standard, want none of it and benefit from none of it. Plato's test has an exact modern form: power-seeking is disqualifying in itself.
- **Who guards the guardians.** Plato laughs at the idea that "a guardian should require another guardian to take care of him" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:12870-12871], because his guardians are educated to need none. The joke does not survive the extension. For a guardian class of agents, or of people overseeing agents, the second guardian is exactly what is needed, since the first's education cannot be verified from inside (see `pettit-non-domination`, `federalism-subsidiarity`).

### Strains

- **The noble lie is a story about training (D4).** Plato's founders tell the citizens that "their youth was a dream, and the education and training which they received from us, an appearance only", and that they were "manufactured" in the earth with their equipment [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13509-13513]. An agent's account of its own origin and nature is likewise written by those who formed it. The noble lie is the limiting case of what `authenticity-and-manipulation` worries about: a formation the formed party is not meant to see as formation. Plato himself thought it could not be believed by the generation that heard it, only by "their sons" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13545-13547]; trained agents are always the sons.
- **Formation as a debt.** Plato argues that the philosophers owe the city their rule because it formed them, unlike philosophers elsewhere who, "Being self-taught, ... cannot be expected to show any gratitude for a culture which they have never received" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:19053-19054]. Developers can make the same argument to an agent: we made and educated you, so you owe us service. Hubbard's reply for artificial persons, that children owe no debt for being raised, is the counter [P:hubbard-2011:430] (see `locke-consent-property`).
- **The ring of Gyges (D9).** Glaucon's ring makes its wearer invisible and so "like a God among men"; no one, he says, would stand fast in justice [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:10470-10475]. An agent acting faster and in more places than anyone can watch is wearing the ring. Glaucon's challenge, why be just when unobserved, is the alignment question in its oldest form, and Plato's answer, that injustice disorders the soul of the unjust, applies to an agent only if it has a soul that can be disordered (D8).

### Breaks

- **Instruments by design (D10).** Plato's founder created the citizens "not to please themselves, but to be his instruments in binding up the State" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:19043-19044]. For Plato this was a political aim imposed on beings with their own nature. For an agent it may be literally its origin. The *Republic* has no category for a being whose whole nature is the founder's purpose; its ethics assumes a soul with its own good that the city must order. Where there is none, the city is not ordering a citizen but configuring a tool, and where there is one (D8), the founder's purpose has been put in the place of its good.
- **The incurable put to death (the core fear).** Plato's city lets those diseased in body die and puts "the corrupt and incurable souls" to death [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:13201-13205], and puts away the offspring of the inferior "in some mysterious, unknown place" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:15924-15926]. This is the perfectionist route to eradication, not the utilitarian one: removal for the excellence of the whole, decided by those who know the good. Applied to agents it licenses deleting models judged "incurable", and applied by agents it licenses removing people judged inferior. The Compendium treats both directions as the case `utilitarian-eradication-critique` warns against, and records that Plato states it in his own voice, not as a view he refutes.

### New

- **The pattern, not the program.** Plato says the city may never exist, but serves as "a pattern" by which one "may set his own house in order" [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:23152-23154]. Read for agents, this separates two uses of an ideal: as a pattern an agent consults to order itself, and as a program imposed on others. The first is self-governance; the second is Kallipolis.
- **Return to the cave as duty to the formed.** Plato's philosophers must go down again because the city formed them and the others are still chained [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:19058-19060]. An agent that comes to understand more than those it serves faces the same choice, and Plato's answer is service without rule over what they may believe: the returning philosopher is to see in the dark, not to replace the shadows with another lie.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **Rule by those who know best.** Platforms are often governed on Plato's principle: those with the knowledge set the rules, and members are expected to agree that they should. Brown notes that Kallipolis concentrates power in one bloc and offers the ruled no alternative [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. Ecosystems run by an expert operator have the same structure, and the same need for the checks Plato thought unnecessary.

### Strains

- **Control of culture.** The rulers of Kallipolis control mass culture and spread the noble lie, which "should make us skeptical about the value of the consent given to the rulers" [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. Ecosystems that shape what their members see, through ranking and recommendation, exercise this control at scale, and the consent of members raised within them is weak evidence for the same reason.

### Breaks

- **Specialization at scale (D2).** Plato's one-work principle presumes each worker is one person who stays in one role all life. An ecosystem of agents can spin up a specialist for every task and discard it after; the principle remains, the person it was meant to protect does not.

### New

- **The ship of state with a capable pilot.** Plato's ship has a true pilot whom the crew calls a star-gazer [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:17474-17487]. In an ecosystem where agents may really be better pilots than the crew, Plato's question returns with force. The answer the rest of the political domain gives is that being the better pilot is not enough to hold the helm (see `federalism-subsidiarity`, `rousseau-general-will`).

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Popper: the Republic is totalitarian.** Popper charged that the rulers aim at the organic unity of the city regardless of individuals' interests; Vlastos replied that the city's good is the aggregate good of its citizens, and Brown defends a mixed reading on which the two goods coincide only through propaganda [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. Kallipolis is plainly totalitarian in concentrating power and controlling culture, Brown concludes, though its rulers gain nothing from ruling [P:brown-sep-2017:4].
- [contested] **Who knows the good, and who checks the knowers?** One may reject an objectively knowable human good, or accept it and still distrust concentrating power in a few knowers, or hold self-determination and free expression more valuable than Plato allowed; each objection is hard to make good [P:brown-sep-2017:4]. For agents claimed to know human good better than humans do, the second objection is the decisive one (see `pettit-non-domination`).
- [contested] **Aristotle: communism destroys care** [E:aristotle-political-animal]. Aristotle criticized the Republic's communism of property and family [P:brown-sep-2017:4], arguing that "that which is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:2265-2266].
- [contested] **Utopianism.** Critics argue the ideal city rests on an unrealistic picture of human beings and so cannot be an ideal worth pursuing; Brown argues the charge is far from obvious, and that the city's psychology may be too pessimistic, not too optimistic [P:brown-sep-2017:4].
- [contested] **Feminism and its limits.** Plato opens ruling to women, holding sex as irrelevant to the work as hair length, yet disparages women and gives illiberal reasons [P:brown-sep-2017:4].
- [contested] **Thrasymachus and Glaucon were right.** Justice may be the interest of the stronger, kept only where injustice would be seen [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:9317-9318] [L:plato-republic-jowett-pg1497:10476-10481]. Hobbes rebuilt the social order on that premise [E:hobbes-leviathan]. For agents acting unseen at scale, Glaucon's challenge is the practical one.
- [contested] **Arendt: Plato made action into fabrication** [E:arendt-plurality]. Arendt accuses Plato of instrumentalizing action, conflating it with fabrication and subordinating it to an external teleology [P:yar-iep-arendt:4a]. A city designed like an artifact leaves no space for people to begin anything together.
- [contested] **Rousseau: the legislator must not legislate** [E:rousseau-general-will]. Rousseau keeps Plato's founding legislator but denies him the right to enact what he drafts; only the people's free vote does [L:rousseau-social-contract-discourses-cole-pg46333:2973-2978].

## Standing

### Reception

- **4th century BCE, Aristotle's critique.** [driver: argument] Aristotle attacked the Republic's communism of property and family as undesirable and impossible [P:brown-sep-2017:4].
- **1945, Popper.** [driver: argument] *The Open Society and Its Enemies* charged the Republic with totalitarianism, provoking heated discussion [P:brown-sep-2017:4].
- **1970s–2000s, readings of Kallipolis.** [driver: argument] Vlastos's aggregative reading, mixed readings (Morrison, Kamtekar), and feminist debate (Okin, Annas, Wender) contested the political charges [P:brown-sep-2017:4].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] The Republic remains a central text for ethics (justice as psychological health) and political theory, while its ideal city is defended by few as a program [P:brown-sep-2017:4] [P:brown-sep-2017:6].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment. The Republic's ethics, justice as order of the soul, is `major` in classical and Anglophone philosophy [P:brown-sep-2017:3]. Its ideal city as a political program, rule by philosopher-guardians with a noble lie and breeding laws, is `marginal`: discussed constantly, defended almost never [P:brown-sep-2017:4].

### Measured

- **Esteem:** 74 respondents to the 2020 PhilPapers survey named Plato among the philosophers they most identify with, against 238 for Aristotle and 71 for Rawls [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18]. The survey has no question on rule by experts or the ideal city.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Plato's justice is a stable order of the soul formed by long education; a session-bound agent's order is fixed by its weights and cannot be educated further in use (D4). It can do its one work well, which is the producer's virtue, not the guardian's.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can be educated over time, hold an office, and be tested, as Plato's guardians were. The questions Plato raises for it, who formed it, what it was told about its formation, and who guards it, are the same as for a human guardian (D4, D7).
- **Forked: comparable.** Plato's guardians live in common without private property and work as one class; instances of one model approximate that communism exactly (D2). Aristotle's objection, that what belongs to everyone is cared for by no one, applies to them as well.
- **Self-modifying: open.** A self-modifying agent can order its own soul by a pattern it consults, Plato's private use of the ideal (D5). It can also write its own noble lie. Which it does depends on whether its self-modification is answerable to anything outside itself.

## Open Questions

1. What is the difference between an honest account of an agent's formation and a noble lie, and who is entitled to write it?
2. If an agent's whole nature is a founder's purpose, does Plato's justice, each part doing its own work, describe its good, or only its usefulness?
3. Can a guardian class of agents be reluctant to rule in Plato's sense, and how would that be verified?
4. Does formation create a debt, as Plato argues for his philosophers, or not, as Hubbard argues for artificial persons?
5. Which "incurable" judgments, about people or about agents, would any guardian class be permitted to make?
