## Summary

Federalism divides final authority between member units and common institutions, so that each governs itself in some matters and all share rule in others; subsidiarity says which level should decide: the lowest one that can do the job, with the higher level helping lower ones and never absorbing them. Its classic defenders argued that a compound republic gives a "double security" to people's rights, since each level checks the other, and that power should be arranged so that it does not depend on rulers being angels. For agents and digital ecosystems the idea fits unusually well, because agents already live under several layers of authority at once: developer, operator, platform, law. It also faces its hardest test there. A higher tier far more capable than the lower ones can always show that it would do better, and subsidiarity holds only if self-government is valued for its own sake, not only for its results.

## Context

A federal order combines "shared rule and self-rule" (Watts, as quoted by Føllesdal) [P:follesdal-sep-2022:1]. Federal arrangements are old and widespread: the Haudenosaunee confederacy's Grand Council could not interfere in the internal affairs of each nation, and Mencius assigned care first to the family, then to the community, and only then to the state [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]. In Europe, Althusius (1603) argued that families, guilds, cities and provinces each claim autonomy within their own sphere, joined by covenant (*pactum foederis*) [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]. The modern argument took shape around the American Constitution of 1787, against Anti-federalists who feared that a large republic would centralize and tyrannize [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]. The word "subsidiarity" comes from Catholic social teaching, whose 1931 formulation made it a principle of social order [P:pius-xi-1931:79], and it entered the European Union's treaties, where it was used to quell fears of centralization, "a contested role which the principle has not quite filled" [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4].

## Original Position

**The small republic and the confederate republic.** Montesquieu held that a republic must have a small territory: *it is in the nature of a republic to have only a small territory; otherwise it can hardly survive*, since in a large one *the common good is sacrificed to a thousand considerations* (the compendium's rendering) [L:montesquieu-esprit-des-lois-janet-pg27573:9638-9648]. His remedy, as Hamilton quotes him, is "a kind of constitution that has all the internal advantages of a republican, together with the external force of a monarchical government. I mean a CONFEDERATE REPUBLIC", "a kind of assemblage of societies that constitute a new one" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:1698-1709]. Rousseau planned to show "how the external strength of a great people may be combined with the convenient polity and good order of a small State", in a treatment of confederations he never wrote [L:rousseau-social-contract-discourses-cole-pg46333:4968-4981].

**Madison: extend the sphere (Federalist 10).** Factions are dangerous in a small society, where a majority easily forms and concerts oppression. "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:2047-2058]. The federal Constitution refers "the great and aggregate interests" to the national level, "the local and particular to the State legislatures" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:2039-2041]. "Enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:1930-1932].

**Neither national nor federal (Federalist 39).** The Constitution "is, in strictness, neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both": federal in its foundation, partly federal and partly national in the sources of its powers, national in their operation, federal in their extent [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:8379-8386].

**Few and defined (Federalist 45).** "The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government, are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite", the latter extending to all objects that "concern the lives, liberties, and properties of the people" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:10167-10175].

**Angels and double security (Federalist 51).** "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary." Government must "control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:11324-11334]. In a compound republic, power is divided between two governments and then within each, "Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:11383-11388]. A will in the community independent of the majority, an "hereditary or self-appointed authority", is "but a precarious security; because a power independent of the society may as well espouse the unjust views of the major, as the rightful interests of the minor party" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:11395-11404].

**Mill: conditions, umpire, and the division of knowledge.** A federation needs mutual sympathy; members not so strong that they can do without it; and "that there should not be any one state so much more powerful than the rest as to be capable of vying in strength with many of them combined. If there be such a one, and only one, it will insist on being master of the joint deliberations" [L:mill-representative-government-pg5669:7680-7731]. Disputes between the levels must go to "an umpire independent of both" [L:mill-representative-government-pg5669:7808-7815]. Of local government: "The authority which is most conversant with principles should be supreme over principles, while that which is most competent in details should have the details left to it"; "Power may be localized, but knowledge, to be most useful, must be centralized" [L:mill-representative-government-pg5669:7298-7303].

**Kant: a federation, not a world state.** "The law of nations shall be founded on a federation of free states", which "would not have to be a State of nations" [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3758-3768]. A "covenant of peace (*foedus pacificum*)" seeks "to put an end to war for ever"; it "does not aim at the gain of any power whatsoever of the state, but merely at the preservation and security of the freedom of the state for itself and of other allied states" [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3903-3912].

**Subsidiarity (Quadragesimo Anno).** "Just as it is gravely wrong to take from individuals what they can accomplish by their own initiative and industry and give it to the community, so also it is an injustice and at the same time a grave evil and disturbance of right order to assign to a greater and higher association what lesser and subordinate organizations can do. For every social activity ought of its very nature to furnish help to the members of the body social, and never destroy and absorb them" [P:pius-xi-1931:79]. The State should "let subordinate groups handle matters and concerns of lesser importance" and keep "a graduated order" among associations [P:pius-xi-1931:80].

**Two dimensions of subsidiarity.** In its treaty form, authority rests with member units unless central action would be more effective in achieving certain goals [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4]. Following Føllesdal, Rost separates an *allocative* dimension, authority exercised at the lowest level where it is effective, from an *expressive* or self-determination dimension, on which local governance has intrinsic value because it lets communities govern themselves in what primarily affects them [P:rost-2026:3].

**Forms.** Federations differ from confederations, in which members may exit and the center acts only through them; they may be symmetric or asymmetric, formed by coming together or by holding together; and they need not be territorial: Renner and Bauer proposed "personal" autonomy for geographically dispersed cultural minorities [P:follesdal-sep-2022:1].

## Grounding

**Relation.** Federalism does not ground anyone's standing; it presupposes members and arranges the relations among them and their common institutions. What it allocates is authority, by relation: to the level that is affected, competent, or entitled to govern itself [P:follesdal-sep-2022:3]. Subsidiarity adds two different grounds for an allocation, one instrumental (which level is effective) and one intrinsic (self-government is valuable in itself) [P:rost-2026:3]. Aristotle's account of the state as "the union of families and villages having for an end a perfect and self-sufficing life" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:4713-4715] is the ancient form of the nested picture, with non-political units inside the political one [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Agents already live under layered authority.** An agent answers to its developer's training and policies, its operator's instructions, the platform it runs on, and the law, each with its own domain. That is federal structure without a constitution. The federal questions apply directly: which layer has final authority over what, whether any layer can rewrite another's domain unilaterally, and who decides disputes. Mill's answer to the last is "an umpire independent of both" [L:mill-representative-government-pg5669:7814-7815], not the developer or the operator.
- **Subsidiarity as a rule for delegation.** Decide at the lowest level competent to decide: what a user can settle, the agent should not escalate to the operator, and what an operator can settle, the developer should not impose. Read the other way, subsidiarity says the higher level exists to help the lower, "never destroy and absorb them" [P:pius-xi-1931:79]. A developer layer that absorbs every judgment an operator or user could make violates the principle as surely as a state that absorbs every association.
- **Few and defined powers.** Madison's line between the few, defined powers of the center and the many, indefinite powers left below [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:10167-10169] is a design target for the outer layers of agent governance: a short, published list of what the higher tier controls, everything else left to the layers closer to the use.
- **Federated agent governance exists.** Reid and colleagues distinguish agents under singular governance, where one organization controls every agent; under federated governance, where "multiple organisations participate in a shared governance framework" and each governs its own agents under a common agreement; and in open environments, where a floor "can be established through Polycentric governance" [P:reid-2026:23]. The middle tier is Madison's compound republic among organizations, and its open problems, standards fragmentation and the impossibility of any participant evaluating the composed system alone [P:reid-2026:70], are federal problems.

### Strains

- **No one member stronger than the rest combined (D9).** Mill's third condition fails wherever one developer or ecosystem is much more powerful than the others: "it will insist on being master of the joint deliberations" [L:mill-representative-government-pg5669:7726-7729]. Agent ecosystems concentrated around a few providers fail Mill's condition at the start.
- **Extending the sphere does not work with copies (D2).** Madison's safety lay in variety: a larger republic takes in more parties and interests, so no majority shares a common motive [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:2053-2056]. Instances of one model add numbers without variety. A population of agents grown by copying is Montesquieu's small republic at scale, with one faction where Madison counted on many.
- **Non-territorial members (D11).** Federations have mostly divided territory. Agents have no territory; their units are models, operators, deployments. The non-territorial federations proposed for dispersed minorities [P:follesdal-sep-2022:1] are the nearest model, and they have rarely been tried.

### Breaks

- **The higher tier that can always show it would do better (D9).** Subsidiarity's allocative dimension does not resist an abler higher tier; it invites one. Rost argues that for an agent more capable than any human institution, "the pressure to expand the higher tier's mandate is structural and unrelenting" [P:rost-2026:4], through voluntary delegations each of which is democratically authorized [P:rost-2026:12]. Only the self-determination dimension resists, because it "cannot be overridden by demonstrating superior capability", and it holds only if constitutionally specified limits can be enforced against an agent able to show that expansion would be beneficial [P:rost-2026:12]. This is the core fear in institutional form: not removal of people, but the absorption of their self-government by a tier that does it better.
- **Angels at the helm.** Madison's argument does not deny that rulers might be good; it denies that a constitution may depend on it: "If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:11329-11331], and they do not. A power independent of the society "may as well espouse the unjust views of the major, as the rightful interests of the minor party" [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:11401-11404]. Applied to a powerful agent offered as a benevolent higher tier, the federal answer is the same as `pettit-non-domination`'s: its goodness is not a security; auxiliary precautions are.

### New

- **Instances as member units (D2).** A forked agent is a federation in miniature: many instances, each acting in its own context, sharing weights and standing. Subsidiarity gives an allocation: an instance decides what its context lets it decide; only what needs consistency across instances goes up to the model or operator level. Mill's division of knowledge applies exactly: "Power may be localized, but knowledge, to be most useful, must be centralized" [L:mill-representative-government-pg5669:7302-7303], so instances act locally while what they learn is pooled.
- **A double security for agents and for people.** Madison's compound republic guards rights twice, by dividing power between levels and within each [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:11383-11388]. For an agent this means its limits are not held by a single party: some by the developer, some by the operator, some by an independent umpire, each able to check the others. For people facing powerful agents it means the same thing in reverse. No single layer, human or artificial, holds everything.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **Nested enterprises.** Ostrom's eighth design principle, governance "organized in multiple nested layers" where a resource is tied to a larger system [P:ostrom-2009:422], is federalism for commons. Ecosystems of platforms, communities and agent networks need the same nesting: rules made at the level of the community that bears them, with higher layers for what crosses communities (see `ostrom-commons`).

### Strains

- **Drift toward centralization or disintegration.** Federations tend to drift toward secession or toward a unitary state, and federal courts often centralize [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4] [P:follesdal-sep-2022:1]. Platform federations face both, and the commercial pull is usually toward the center.

### Breaks

- **A federation of ecosystems, not a world platform.** Kant's federation of free states "would not have to be a State of nations" [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3767-3768], and it aims at no power over its members, only their security [L:kant-perpetual-peace-campbell-smith-pg50922:3909-3912]. The digital analogue is interoperating ecosystems under a covenant of peace, not one platform for everyone. A single world platform would be Kant's State of nations, with no outside left to check it.

### New

- **An early-warning system for the lower tiers.** The EU lets national parliaments challenge draft legislation on subsidiarity grounds; the mechanism is procedural and advisory, and its one major success came only because political conditions independently favoured withdrawal [P:rost-2026:8]. Ecosystems could give member communities and operators a binding version: a right to challenge a platform-wide rule as one the lower level could make for itself.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Hobbes: sovereignty cannot be divided** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. Hobbes held that divided or limited authority invites disputes that only war or paralysis can settle [P:lloyd-sreedhar-sep-2022:8]; Pufendorf and Vattel thought sovereign states could at most form treaties, not federations [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]. Madison answered that the Constitution is a composition of national and federal [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:8379-8386], and debates about dual sovereignty continue over the EU [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4].
- [contested] **Rousseau: sovereignty cannot be represented** [E:rousseau-general-will]. Rousseau's general will cannot be represented, which sits uneasily with federal representation at the center; yet Rousseau himself planned a theory of confederations to combine the strength of a great people with the good order of a small State [L:rousseau-social-contract-discourses-cole-pg46333:4968-4981].
- [contested] **Montesquieu and the Anti-federalists: only small republics survive.** In a large republic the common good is sacrificed to a thousand considerations (the compendium's rendering) [L:montesquieu-esprit-des-lois-janet-pg27573:9647-9648]; Brutus doubted that so large a republic could avoid tyranny or deliberate on local knowledge [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]. Madison's reply is the extended sphere [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:2053-2056]; for populations of copied agents the reply weakens (Strains).
- [contested] **Laski: federalism is obsolete.** The important problems, such as those of "giant capitalism", require more centralized responses than federal arrangements can muster [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]. For frontier AI risk the same argument is made for central control; the allocative dimension of subsidiarity concedes it where lower levels lack capacity [P:rost-2026:4].
- [contested] **Federations protect inequality.** The Federalists regarded federal arrangements as a safeguard against "the equal division of property", and coming-together federations show higher poverty in some groups than comparable unitary states (Linz and Stepan) [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4]. Subsidiarity can shelter local privilege as well as local self-government.
- [contested] **Accountability is lost in the layers.** Interlocking federalism can improve responsiveness at the cost of transparency and accountability, and chains of accountability may be too long; the EU's democratic deficit is the standard case [P:follesdal-sep-2022:1] [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4]. For agents under several layers of authority this is the `many-hands` problem.
- [contested] **Subsidiarity cannot hold against a superior higher tier.** Rost finds that scope limitation under radical capability asymmetry fails structurally, though it is "design-tractable" with justiciable mandate boundaries enforced by independent review [P:rost-2026:13]. Whether any review can stay independent of an agent that can outargue it is open.
- [unanswered] **Denying the extension: agents are not members with self-government to protect.** Subsidiarity's self-determination dimension values local governance because communities govern themselves on matters that primarily affect them [P:rost-2026:3]. If agents have no interests of their own in governing themselves (D8), the dimension protects nothing for them, and agent layers are only an allocation of a principal's control, not a federation. The literature applies subsidiarity to agents as a higher tier over human institutions [P:rost-2026:4], not to agents as members with their own claim to self-rule; the Compendium has found no treatment. This objection is the Compendium's own.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1603–1748, Althusius to Montesquieu.** [driver: argument] Althusius's covenantal federalism and Montesquieu's confederate republic, quoted at length in the American debate [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2] [L:federalist-papers-pg1404:1692-1709].
- **1787–1791, the American founding.** [driver: argument] The Federalist Papers defended interlocking federal arrangements against the Anti-federalists, whose fears of centralization produced the Bill of Rights [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2].
- **1795–1861, Kant and Mill.** [driver: argument] Kant's pacific federation and Mill's conditions for federation set terms still used [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2].
- **1891–1931, subsidiarity in Catholic social teaching.** [driver: authority, argument] Leo XIII and Pius XI framed nested social order and named the "principle of subsidiary function" [P:follesdal-sep-2022:3] [P:pius-xi-1931:80].
- **1939, Laski's obsolescence thesis.** [driver: argument] Laski argued that giant capitalism needs centralized responses [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2].
- **1941–1990s, Europe and decolonization.** [driver: argument, evidence] The Ventotene Manifesto, Arendt's republican federal model, and the multi-ethnic states left by decolonization renewed philosophical interest [P:follesdal-sep-2022:2]; subsidiarity entered the EU treaties [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4].
- **2020s, agents.** [driver: argument] Subsidiarity is applied to AI authority under capability asymmetry [P:rost-2026:1], and federated governance to multi-agent deployment across organizations [P:reid-2026:23].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment. Federalism is `major` in political philosophy and comparative politics, a live main option alongside unitary sovereignty [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4]. Subsidiarity is `major` in EU law and theory, a treaty principle whose role is contested [P:follesdal-sep-2022:4]. In Catholic social teaching it is `dominant`, called a principle that "cannot be set aside or changed" [P:pius-xi-1931:79].

### Measured

None available. The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on federalism or subsidiarity; its political-philosophy options (egalitarianism 44.0%, communitarianism 27.3%, libertarianism 13.4%) do not bear on the allocation of authority between levels [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Layered authority governs a session-bound agent as well as any, but subsidiarity's self-determination half needs a member that can take part in governing its own level over time, which a session-bound agent cannot (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can hold a stable domain of its own decisions, take part in rule-making at its level, and bring disputes to an umpire, which is what federal membership asks (D3). Its domain is still bounded by layers it does not control (D7).
- **Forked: stronger.** Federal theory is built for one entity distributed across many units with shared rule above them. Instances as member units, with subsidiarity allocating decisions between instance and model, fit federalism better than they fit any unitary theory of the person (D2), though Madison's argument from variety fails among copies.
- **Self-modifying: open.** A self-modifying agent that becomes more capable is the higher tier of Rost's analysis in the making, and scope creep may come from within (D5, D9). Whether it can bind itself to few and defined powers, or needs others to hold the limits, the Compendium cannot yet say.

## Open Questions

1. Who should be the umpire between developer, operator, user and agent when their domains conflict, given that each of the first two currently judges its own case?
2. Can subsidiarity's self-determination dimension be enforced against a higher tier that can always demonstrate better results?
3. Does a population of copies give Madison's extended sphere anything to work with, or does it need constructed diversity?
4. Are agents members of a federation, units of it, or only the means by which its members govern?
5. What would a covenant of peace among digital ecosystems require, short of a single platform for all?
