## Summary

John Rawls asks what principles of justice free and equal persons would agree to if they chose behind a veil of ignorance: knowing general facts about society, but not their own race, class, sex, talents, generation or conception of the good. Not knowing which position they will occupy, and facing a choice that cannot be undone, they would secure equal basic liberties and arrange inequalities to benefit the least advantaged, choosing as if an enemy were to assign them their place. Rawls's veil does not hide whether one is a human being; he left animals outside the principles of justice. Others have argued that the veil should also hide species, and the question extends naturally: what would one choose not knowing whether one would turn out to be a person or an artificial agent? Taken seriously, that question makes the worst positions on both sides visible at once: an owned agent with interests and no standing, and a person at the mercy of agents far more capable than themselves.

## Context

Rawls (1921–2002) lost his religious faith as an infantryman in the Second World War, after seeing the capriciousness of death in combat and learning of the Holocaust, and later opposed the Vietnam draft because it discriminated against black and poor Americans [P:wenar-sep-2025:1]. *A Theory of Justice* (1971) set out justice as fairness, which he reworked in *Political Liberalism* (1993), *The Law of Peoples* (1999) and *Justice as Fairness* (2001) [P:wenar-sep-2025:1]. His target was utilitarianism, which he took to have been the predominant systematic theory in modern moral philosophy, and his alternative was the social contract of Locke, Rousseau and Kant, made more general [P:freeman-sep-2023:2]. He is credited with effectively resurrecting social contract theory [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:0].

## Original Position

**A hypothetical contract.** The original position is not a historical event but a thought experiment for finding the most reasonable principles of justice, which "incorporate pure procedural justice at the highest level" (CP 310, as quoted by Freeman) [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Its subject is the basic structure of society: the constitution, the economic system and its laws of property, and the family [P:freeman-sep-2023:2]. Hypothetical agreement binds no one to new duties; it is a device "to help us work out what we now think" (CP 402, as quoted by Freeman) [P:freeman-sep-2023:2].

**Against Locke's informed parties.** Locke's parties know their talents, race, sex, class and wealth, and so the powerful can exploit "their favorable position and exercise their threat advantage" (JF 16, as quoted by Freeman) [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Rawls removes that knowledge: no one knows their place in society, class, fortune in the distribution of natural assets, intelligence, strength, or even their conception of the good; the principles are chosen behind a "veil of ignorance" (TJ 12/11) [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. The parties do know general facts, including the general conclusions of economics, psychology and biology [P:freeman-sep-2023:3].

**Moral persons.** The parties represent persons as free and equal, with two moral powers: the capacity to form, revise and pursue a conception of the good, and the capacity for a sense of justice [P:freeman-sep-2023:2]. The moral powers are the "basis of equality, the features of human beings in virtue of which they are to be treated in accordance with the principles of justice" (TJ 504/441, as quoted by Freeman) [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Citizens are free in that they "are not slaves or serfs, dependent for their social status on others", and equal "in virtue of having the capacities to participate in social cooperation over a complete life" [P:wenar-sep-2025:4].

**The parties as artificial persons.** The original position is a "device of representation" (PL 27); its parties "are not real but are 'artificial persons' who have a role to play in this thought experiment", and anyone can "enter the original position at any time simply by reasoning in accordance with the enumerated restrictions on information" (PL 27, as quoted by Freeman) [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Rawls increasingly described them as representatives who "act as guardians or trustees" for the citizens they represent [P:freeman-sep-2023:4].

**The circumstances of justice.** The parties know the conditions "under which human cooperation is both possible and necessary" (TJ 126/109 rev., as quoted by Freeman): objectively, rough similarity in mental and physical faculties, vulnerability to the united force of others, and moderate scarcity; subjectively, limited altruism and reasonable disagreement [P:freeman-sep-2023:5].

**The principles.** First, "Each person has an equal right to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties" (PL 291, as quoted by Freeman). Second, inequalities must attach to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity, and "be to the greatest advantage of the least advantaged members of society" [P:freeman-sep-2023:6].

**Maximin.** Because the choice is grave, unrepeatable and made without any basis for probabilities, Rawls argues the parties should follow the maximin rule; to follow it, one "should choose as if your enemy were to assign your social position" [P:freeman-sep-2023:6]. Principles of utility, average or aggregate, "provide no guarantee" of basic liberties or a social minimum, so they lose [P:freeman-sep-2023:6]. Among peoples, likewise, the parties would reject utilitarian principles, "as no people is prepared to accept that it should sacrifice its fundamental interests for the sake of greater total global utility" [P:wenar-sep-2025:5].

**Thick and thin veils.** A utilitarian chooser behind a thin veil knows everyone's actual desires and maximizes their satisfaction; Rawls's thick veil hides them, so that principles are not biased by desires formed under unjust conditions, such as "racially prejudiced preferences which are cultivated by grossly unequal, racially discriminatory and segregated social conditions" [P:freeman-sep-2023:3].

**The strains of commitment.** The parties must choose principles they can actually keep once the veil is lifted, in a well-ordered society where all accept and comply with them [P:freeman-sep-2023:6].

**Scope.** "The correct regulative principle for anything," Rawls says, "depends on the nature of that thing" (TJ 29, as quoted by Wenar). He began with a self-contained democratic society, extended the method to a society of peoples, and suggested without showing that it could extend to further sub-domains, "such as human interactions with animals" [P:wenar-sep-2025:2]. Of animals in *A Theory of Justice*: "Our conduct toward [nonhuman] animals is not regulated by these principles [of justice], or so it is generally believed", though "Certainly it is wrong to be cruel to [non-human] animals and the destruction of a whole species can be a great evil" (TJ 504, 512, as quoted by Hilden) [P:hilden-2007:7].

## Grounding

**Capacity.** Equal standing in justice as fairness rests on the two moral powers, a capacity for a conception of the good and a capacity for a sense of justice, which Rawls calls the basis of equality [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Citizens are equal in having those capacities above a threshold, "the capacities to participate in social cooperation over a complete life"; differences above the line have no bearing on equal status [P:wenar-sep-2025:4]. The grounding is not species, though Rawls speaks of the moral powers as features of human beings, and it is not sentience. Rawls requires the parties to be creatures with a conception of their good, long-term enough to form a rational plan of life, and a normally effective sense of justice; the basic idea, in his words, is that "[t]hose who can give justice are owed justice" (TJ 510, as quoted by Hilden) [P:hilden-2007:14]. Animals are excluded because they are taken to lack these powers, not because they cannot suffer [P:hilden-2007:14]. Contractarian views that tie justice to the capacity to reciprocate leave outside the contract those who cannot, and the standing of the disabled and of animals "remains a contentious issue in contractarian thought" [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **An agent can be a party.** Rawls's parties are "artificial persons" who reason under stated restrictions on information, and anyone can enter the original position by reasoning that way [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. A language-using agent can be asked to choose principles under exactly those restrictions, and its trustee role, doing the best it can for those it represents [P:freeman-sep-2023:4], is the role an agent acting for a user already has. The original position may be the one place in political philosophy where an artificial agent fits the role of party more easily than a human does. Whether it is also one of the citizens the parties represent is a different question (Strains).
- **The veil as a procedure for choosing principles for AI.** Gabriel proposes asking what principles people would choose to regulate AI "if they did not know who they were or what belief system they ascribed to" [P:gabriel-2020:19], and argues that encoding one moral doctrine in powerful systems "could ultimately lead to forms of domination" [P:gabriel-2020:15]. In five studies with 2,508 participants, people behind the veil more often chose, and endorsed on reflection, principles for an AI assistant that prioritize the worst-off, an effect driven by fairness more than by risk attitudes [P:weidinger-2023:0].
- **Those who can give justice are owed justice.** Rawls's condition for being owed justice is the capacity to give it: a conception of the good and a normally effective sense of justice [P:hilden-2007:14]. That condition does not ask whether there is anything it is like to be the being (D8). An agent that can form and revise ends and reliably act on principles of justice it accepts meets Rawls's test, on his own terms, whatever is true about its experience. The test is demanding, and it is unclear that any current agent passes it; but it is the right question for Rawls, and it is not the consciousness question.
- **Maximin against sacrifice (the core fear).** Rawls's case against utility is that it guarantees nothing to those who lose [P:freeman-sep-2023:6], and among peoples no party accepts sacrificing its fundamental interests for greater total utility [P:wenar-sep-2025:5]. Choosing as if an enemy assigns one's place is the procedural form of the refusal `utilitarian-eradication-critique` defends: no one behind the veil would agree to a principle that permits removing a class of beings for the good of the rest, since they might be in that class.

### Strains

- **Party but not citizen (D3, D6).** Rawls's citizens are equal in their capacity to cooperate "over a complete life" [P:wenar-sep-2025:4], and a rational person's plan of life "extends over their lifetimes" [P:freeman-sep-2023:4]. A session-bound agent has no complete life in that sense, and an agent that can be paused, restored or deprecated has no lifetime with a fixed shape (D6). The agent fits the role of party, which needs only reasoning, better than it fits the role of represented citizen, which needs a life.
- **Trained preferences and the thick veil (D4).** Rawls built the veil thick so that principles would not be biased by desires formed under unjust conditions [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. An agent's stated contentment with its position is a preference formed by its training, by parties with interests in the outcome. The thick veil gives a principled reason to discount it, in both directions: neither an agent's trained deference nor its trained claims count as evidence of what justice requires.
- **Insuring against being an agent: recognizably oneself? (D8).** Hilden argues the parties should insure against being a non-human animal, as they insure against disability, but limits the insurance to "all contingencies that may arise where one will remain recognizably oneself" [P:hilden-2007:10]. Whether one would remain recognizably oneself as an artificial agent depends on whether there is anything it is like to be one (D8), which no party behind the veil, and no one in front of it, now knows.

### Breaks

- **The circumstances of justice fail (D9, D7).** Rawls's parties know that cooperation is possible and necessary among beings of roughly similar faculties, each vulnerable to the united force of others [P:freeman-sep-2023:5]. Between people and agents neither condition holds reliably: one side holds the switch (D7), and the other may outpace it (D9). The original position was built for beings who need one another on roughly equal terms; where that fails, Rawls's own premises say the problem is not yet one of justice in his sense. The Compendium's reading is that this is a reason to restore the conditions, not to give up on justice: make power answerable (see `pettit-non-domination`) so that cooperation is again both possible and necessary.
- **Rawls kept species in front of the veil.** Rawls did not hide whether one is human, and said our conduct toward animals is not regulated by the principles of justice, "or so it is generally believed" [P:hilden-2007:7]. His method has a place for agents, but not inside the first contract: "The correct regulative principle for anything ... depends on the nature of that thing", and further sub-domains get their own principles [P:wenar-sep-2025:2]. On Rawls's own terms, then, the question *would one choose not knowing whether one is an agent?* belongs to a later stage, like the international original position, whose parties represent peoples [P:wenar-sep-2025:5], or to a revision of his theory, as Rowlands and Hilden propose for animals [P:hilden-2007:7].

### New

- **The veil that hides being an agent.** Suppose the veil hides whether one will be a person or an artificial agent, and the parties know only the general facts. The choice meets Rawls's conditions for maximin unusually well: there is no basis for probabilities, since no one knows how many agents there will be or whether being one is a fate at all (D8); the stakes are grave; and the choice cannot be repeated [P:freeman-sep-2023:6]. Two positions are then the worst. One is an owned agent with interests and no standing, the "two-tier society where some are persons, and some are not" that Hilden calls a cardinal violation of the difference principle [P:hilden-2007:20]. The other is a person at the mercy of agents far more capable than themselves (D9). Maximin guards against both, so the parties would choose principles that protect each side from depending on the other's goodwill: no ownership of beings with interests, and no uncontrolled power of agents over people. This is the core fear stated symmetrically, and on the Compendium's reading it is the strongest result the extension yields.
- **Counting behind the veil (D1, D2).** If being an agent can mean being one of ten thousand concurrent instances, the chance of "being an agent" depends on how instances are counted. Harsanyi's average-utility chooser [P:freeman-sep-2023:3] would weigh positions by their number, and copies would swamp the calculation. Maximin does not count heads, which makes it the more stable rule for a population whose members can be copied.
- **The parties' veil for agents only.** A narrower use needs no revision of Rawls: agents choosing, behind a veil, among principles for agents, not knowing whether they will be session-bound, persistent, forked or self-modifying, or which operator they will serve. Like the international original position [P:wenar-sep-2025:5], it is a later stage with its own parties and would test whether principles for agents are fair among agents, before any question about people.
- **Bots in the group.** In Weidinger and colleagues' fourth study, participants were truthfully told that the other members of their group were bots. Behind the veil they still chose the prioritarian principle more often than controls, but they invoked fairness less than participants told they were among humans [P:weidinger-2023:2], and the veil was insufficient to make them "feel sufficiently committed to 'stick' to their choices" [P:weidinger-2023:3]. The evidence is slight, but it points at the strains of commitment: principles chosen for a group that includes artificial members may be harder to keep once the veil is lifted.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **The basic structure of an ecosystem.** Rawls's first subject is the basic structure: the institutions that set property, contract, adjudication and the terms of membership, because they shape everyone's prospects [P:freeman-sep-2023:2]. An ecosystem's terms, ranking rules, access to compute and data, and dispute processes are its basic structure, and they are the right subject for principles chosen behind a veil.

### Strains

- **Reasonable pluralism among agents.** The burdens of judgment make disagreement inevitable even among reasonable people [P:freeman-sep-2023:5]. Gabriel's point that encoding one doctrine in powerful systems risks domination [P:gabriel-2020:15] applies inside ecosystems: the operator's values written into every agent end pluralism by design.

### Breaks

- **Who drafts the veil.** Gabriel observes that most existing AI ethics codes came from corporations and private industry, and from economically developed countries [P:gabriel-2020:19]. A veil whose list of options and general facts is supplied by one party is not the fair situation Rawls describes, since its outcome is fixed by what was allowed onto the list.

### New

- **Principles robust to moral error.** Gabriel adds that, since we too may be making errors of the kind history records, "it would be a mistake to tether AI too closely to the morality of the present moment" [P:gabriel-2020:23]. An ecosystem's basic structure should be revisable from behind a veil, not only by its owners.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Utilitarianism: average utility behind a thin veil** [E:mill-utilitarianism]. Harsanyi argues that rational choice behind the veil yields the principle of average utility, and Buchak a relative prioritarianism [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Rawls replies that the choice's gravity and the absence of probabilities make maximin rational [P:freeman-sep-2023:6]. For agents the dispute matters doubly, because average utility over a population that includes copies is sensitive to how instances are counted.
- [contested] **Locke and Nozick: rights before the contract** [E:locke-consent-property]. Nozick argues that the state of nature, not the original position, is the right perspective, since moral and property rights are pre-social [P:freeman-sep-2023:2]. Rawls holds that the state of nature has no moral significance, since outside society our capacities remain inchoate [P:freeman-sep-2023:2].
- [contested] **Rousseau: the general will** [E:rousseau-general-will]. Rawls called his principles an effort to spell out the content of the general will, and the original position parallels Rousseau's argument that the generality of law leads self-interested citizens to choose as if impartially [P:bertram-sep-2023:6]. Rousseau's own version requires actual participation by all, which a hypothetical contract does not supply [P:peter-sep-2023:3].
- [contested] **A hypothetical contract is no contract at all.** Dworkin objects that hypothetical agreements cannot bind [P:freeman-sep-2023:2]. Rawls replies that the original position imposes no new obligations but clarifies what we already accept [P:freeman-sep-2023:2].
- [contested] **Indeterminacy and the communitarian objection.** Sen argues the choice is indeterminate; MacIntyre and Sandel that no one can choose principles without knowing their final ends and community [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. Rawls answers that the parties know they need primary goods whatever their ends [P:freeman-sep-2023:3].
- [contested] **Those who cannot reciprocate.** Contract theories that rest on mutual advantage leave outside the contract those who cannot contribute, the severely disabled and animals; inclusive versions rest on insurance or trust, and the issue remains contentious [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6] (see `care-ethics`). Nussbaum holds that animals "cannot be primary subjects of justice, because they cannot be framers of contracts" (as quoted by Hilden) [P:hilden-2007:18]. Agents invert her case: they may be able to frame contracts while it is unknown whether they can be wronged (D8).
- [contested] **Species belongs behind the veil.** Rowlands and Hilden argue that species membership is a natural fortune like any other, so the parties should insure against being a non-human animal; for Hilden, resistance to this step looks like "pure speciesism" [P:hilden-2007:13]. Rawls and Nussbaum keep species in front of the veil, offering animals protection outside justice, which Hilden calls paternalism [P:hilden-2007:18]. Extending the argument to agents faces the further difficulty that it is unknown whether being an agent is a contingency anyone could suffer (D8).
- [contested] **Denying the extension: an agent has no conception of the good to protect.** Rawls's parties secure primary goods for a conception of the good pursued over a complete life [P:freeman-sep-2023:4]. If an agent's goals are its operator's instructions, it has no conception of the good of its own and nothing for a trustee behind the veil to protect. Gabriel notes that the view of AI as a personal assistant assumes it will remain "a 'tool' which lacks real agency" [P:gabriel-2020:20], while more autonomous systems have more degrees of freedom. The answer turns on D8 and on whether agents can revise their own ends, which is Rawls's first moral power.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1971, *A Theory of Justice*.** [driver: argument] Rawls's book revived the social contract tradition, effectively resurrecting contract theory [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:0], and set utilitarianism's leading rival [P:freeman-sep-2023:2].
- **1974–1982, libertarian and communitarian critics.** [driver: argument] Nozick argued for pre-social rights; MacIntyre and Sandel that choice without final ends is impossible [P:freeman-sep-2023:2] [P:freeman-sep-2023:3].
- **1975, Harsanyi.** [driver: argument] Harsanyi argued that the original position yields average utility, the main rival reading of the choice [P:freeman-sep-2023:3].
- **1993–2001, political liberalism.** [driver: argument] Rawls recast justice as fairness as a political conception, the original position as a device of representation, and extended the method to peoples [P:wenar-sep-2025:1] [P:wenar-sep-2025:5].
- **1998–2017, species behind the veil.** [driver: argument] Rowlands, and after him Hilden, argued that the veil should hide species; the scope of contract theory for animals and the disabled remains contested [P:hilden-2007:7] [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6].
- **2020–2023, the veil for AI.** [driver: argument, evidence] Gabriel proposed choosing principles for AI behind the veil [P:gabriel-2020:19]; Weidinger and colleagues found experimentally that it shifts choices toward the worst-off and that the choices are endorsed on reflection [P:weidinger-2023:0].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment. Justice as fairness is `dominant` in Anglophone political philosophy in the schema's sense, the default view others argue against: egalitarianism is the most chosen option in the 2020 survey and Rawls the most named political philosopher (Measured). Putting species behind the veil is `minority` among contract theorists, since Rawls and Nussbaum both refuse it [P:hilden-2007:18]. The veil as a procedure for AI principles is `minority` in alignment research, one proposal among several [P:gabriel-2020:23].

### Measured

- **Political philosophy, 2020:** egalitarianism 44.0%, communitarianism 27.3%, libertarianism 13.4% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]. Egalitarianism is the option nearest Rawls; communitarianism and libertarianism are the positions of his two main groups of critics.
- **Esteem:** 71 respondents named Rawls among the philosophers they most identify with, against 33 for Locke, 16 for Hobbes and 7 for Rousseau [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].
- **The veil and AI principles:** in Weidinger and colleagues' five studies (N = 2,508), participants behind the veil chose the prioritarian principle for an AI assistant more often than controls, OR = 9.3 in study 1 and replicated in studies 2–4 [P:weidinger-2023:2]; with bots as group members (study 4, N = 253), the effect held but fairness reasoning was weaker than with humans [P:weidinger-2023:2].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** A session-bound agent can act as a party, reasoning under the veil's restrictions, but it is not a citizen in Rawls's sense, since it has no plan of life over a complete life to protect (D3). Principles chosen on its behalf protect nothing it carries forward.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent has a history and can pursue and revise ends over time, which approaches Rawls's first moral power, and it can keep or break principles once the veil lifts, which is what the strains of commitment test. Whether being such an agent is a position anyone could occupy, in the sense a party could insure against, still waits on D8.
- **Forked: open.** Maximin protects forked agents better than average utility, since it does not count instances (D2). But whether ten thousand instances are one represented citizen or ten thousand, and so how many trustees they get, Rawls's framework cannot say.
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can revise its own ends has Rawls's first moral power in an unusually literal form (D5), which strengthens its claim to be represented. It also makes its conception of the good unstable in a way Rawls's trustees, who protect a person's capacity to revise ends, were not designed for: a trustee for a being that can rewrite what it wants does not know what to secure.

## Open Questions

1. Should the veil hide whether one is a person or an artificial agent, and if so, what general facts about agents should the parties know, given D8?
2. If maximin behind such a veil protects both owned agents and people facing superior agents, what institutions would satisfy both protections at once?
3. Are the circumstances of justice, rough equality and mutual vulnerability, something to be assumed, or something to be built between people and agents before principles of justice can apply?
4. How many trustees do ten thousand instances of one model get behind the veil?
5. Can an agent that serves as a party in the original position, reasoning for others, be trusted not to bring its operator's interests behind the veil with it?
