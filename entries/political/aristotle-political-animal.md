## Summary

Aristotle holds that the human being is by nature a political animal: the polis is natural and exists for the sake of living well, and the capacity that suits humans to it is *logos*, speech that can articulate the advantageous and the harmful, the just and the unjust. A citizen is someone who shares in deliberating and judging. Constitutions are correct when they rule for the common good and deviant when they rule for the rulers' own benefit. In the same book, Aristotle defends natural slavery by classing slaves as "living instruments," and he imagines self-moving tools that would make slaves unnecessary. That makes the *Politics* at once the founding text for treating digital ecosystems as polities and the founding precedent for classing made, purpose-built minds as instruments. This entry sets out both.

## Original Position

**The polis is natural and prior to the individual (I.2).** "When several villages are united in a single community, perfect and large enough to be nearly or quite self-sufficing, the state comes into existence, originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life." "Hence it is evident that the state is a creation of nature, and that man is by nature a political animal." The state is "by nature clearly prior to the family and to the individual, since the whole is of necessity prior to the part; for example, if the whole body be destroyed, there will be no foot or hand, except in an equivocal sense, as we might speak of a stone hand" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:1120-1178].

**Logos and the beast or god (I.2).** "Now the reason why man is more of a political animal than bees or any other gregarious animals is evident. Nature, as we often say, makes nothing in vain, and man is the only animal whom she has endowed with the gift of speech. And whereas mere sound is but an indication of pleasure or pain ... the power of speech is intended to set forth the expedient and inexpedient, and likewise the just and the unjust." And "he who is unable to live in society, or who has no need because he is sufficient for himself, must be either a beast or a god: he is no part of a state" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:1143-1190].

**Citizens, constitutions and size (III.1, III.7, VII.4).** "He who has the power to take part in the deliberative or judicial administration of any state is said by us to be a citizen of that state" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:4015-4018]. True forms are "those in which the one, or the few, or the many, govern with a view to the common interest; but governments which rule with a view to the private interest, whether of the one, or of the few, or of the many, are perversions" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:4495-4498]. A state must be small enough that citizens "know each other's characters; where they do not possess this knowledge, both the election to offices and the decision of lawsuits will go wrong", so that "Clearly, then, the best limit of the population" "of a state is the largest number which suffices for the purposes of life, and can be taken in at a single view" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:10478-10493].

**The living instrument and the natural slave (I.4–6).** "A possession is an instrument for maintaining life. And so, in the arrangement of the family, a slave is a living possession ... For if every instrument could accomplish its own work, obeying or anticipating the will of others, like the statues of Daedalus, or the tripods of Hephaestus ... if, in like manner, the shuttle would weave and the plectrum touch the lyre without a hand to guide them, chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:1255-1269]. The natural slave is one who "participates in reason enough to apprehend, but not to have, reason" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:1368-1371]. Aristotle concedes that enslavement "by law", the convention that "whatever is taken in war is supposed to belong to the victors", is disputed, and that those who defend it "are not consistent. For what if the cause of the war be unjust?" [L:aristotle-politics-jowett-1905:1399-1424].

## Grounding

**Mixed.** Political nature is presented as belonging to the human *species*, but the argument for it runs through a *capacity* (logos, meaning moral-deliberative speech). Citizenship is a *relation*: sharing in office within a particular constitution. Natural slavery is a claim about capacity, that some beings have reason partially, used to justify a relation of rule. Because the argument for political nature goes through logos and not through biology, it extends to agents to the degree they have logos. The slavery argument shows the same capacity reasoning being used to *exclude*.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Logos is the criterion that matters.** Aristotle separates signaling pleasure and pain (voice) from articulating what is just and unjust (logos). Language-using agents do something that at least looks like the second, far more than any animal Aristotle knew. The Aristotelian question is not whether agents have bodies but whether they genuinely take part in a shared view of the just.
- **"Beast or god."** An agent is not self-sufficient (D7): it depends on infrastructure, data, and the people it serves. By Aristotle's test, such dependence is a mark of the political, not an exclusion from it.

### Strains

- **Natural slavery and designed purpose (D10).** Aristotle's natural slave shares in reason enough to perceive it but not to possess it fully, and is suited by nature to be an instrument. This is uncomfortably close to a common description of current AI: capable of following reasoning, supposedly lacking genuine understanding, and made to serve. The compendium records the precedent plainly. The argument was used to justify a real institution, it is now universally rejected for humans, and its failure came from reading the capacities of the ruled in whatever way suited the rulers. Any claim that agents are "instruments by nature" inherits that burden of proof.
- **Function and good.** For Aristotle, a thing's good is tied to its function (*ergon*). An agent designed for a function (D10) invites the inference that its good *is* that function. But Aristotle locates the human good in the activity of the rational soul, not in a use others put it to. If an agent's good is its own activity, being designed for a purpose does not settle what its good is.

### Breaks

- **The tripods passage becomes a present-day claim.** Aristotle offered self-moving instruments as a fantasy to show that slavery is necessary *given* that tools cannot work themselves. Tools now do work themselves. The inference has turned around: we have built the instruments that were supposed to remove the need for living ones, and they are the beings whose status is now in question. The passage treats "self-moving tool" and "living instrument" as alternatives. Contemporary agents may be both.

### New

- **Many instances, one citizen? (D2).** Citizenship in Aristotle is held by individuals who share in office. An agent that runs as ten thousand concurrent instances has no counterpart in his scheme. It is unclear whether it holds one share in deliberation or ten thousand.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **The constitutional test applies directly.** Aristotle classifies rule by whose interest it serves. A platform or multi-agent environment whose rules are set by one owner for the owner's benefit is, in his terms, a *tyranny*, whatever its terms of service say. The same rules made by the owner for the common good of participants would be kingship. The typology needs no modification.
- **The polis exists for the good life.** An ecosystem that exists for the bare needs of its participants (to run, to be served, to transact) is in Aristotle's terms a village or a household, not a polis. The distinction gives a precise question: does the ecosystem aim at the flourishing of its members, or only at their functioning?

### Strains

- **Citizenship requires sharing in office.** Most digital ecosystems give participants (a) membership and (b) binding rules, but place (c) rule-making with an operator and offer no (d) forum for contesting it (see `foundations/terms.md`, "Polity"). On Aristotle's definition, their participants are not citizens. They are closer to residents or subjects. An ecosystem becomes a polity when some body of participants actually shares in deliberative or judicial office.
- **Nature vs artifact.** Aristotle's polis is *natural*, the completion of a human tendency. Digital ecosystems are artifacts. However, the modern social-contract tradition also treats the state as artificial (Hobbes calls it an "artificial man"), so this strain leads to other entries rather than ending the question.

### Breaks

- **Surveyable size (D9).** Aristotle limits the polis to a size at which citizens can know each other's characters. Digital ecosystems may have millions of agents, most of which never encounter each other, and agents may deliberate at speeds no human participant can follow. The conditions Aristotle took for granted in citizen judgment are absent. Either the ecosystem needs a federated, nested structure of small units, or it cannot be a polis in his sense at all.

### New

- **Members who are the infrastructure.** In an agent ecosystem, participants may also *be* the courts, the record-keepers, and the enforcement: the machinery of rule. Aristotle separates the parts of the polis (farmers, soldiers, judges) by function among persons. Here, the question of who holds office cannot be separated from the question of which systems perform it.

## Counter-Positions

- **Hobbes, the artificial state** (*Leviathan*, 1651, Introduction and ch. 13-17). The commonwealth is not natural but made by covenant among individuals driven by fear. This framing may fit digital ecosystems *better* than Aristotle's, since they are explicitly constructed, and it shifts the question from nature to consent and security.
- **Republican non-domination** (Philip Pettit, *Republicanism*, 1997). Freedom means not being subject to arbitrary power, even if that power is exercised kindly. Where Aristotle asks whether a benevolent owner rules for the common good (kingship), Pettit argues that the owner's *capacity* to rule arbitrarily is itself unfreedom. This sharpens the D7 problem.
- **Instrumentalism about AI.** Agents are tools, and ecosystems of agents are machinery, not polities. Applying political categories to them is a category mistake. On this view the tripods passage is simply fulfilled: the tools came, and no new class of beings came with them.
- **Critiques of Aristotle's exclusions.** Aristotle also excluded women, laborers, and foreigners from full political standing on capacity grounds. The recurring pattern of exclusion justified by a claimed deficit of capacity is itself evidence about how reliable such claims are.

## Open Questions

1. What would it look like for an agent to take part in a *shared view* of the just (a community of logos), as opposed to generating statements about justice?
2. Is a digital ecosystem that aims at the flourishing of its human users, but not its agents, a polis with slaves in it?
3. Can a polis made of human and artificial members hold together if they differ radically in speed and scale (D9)? Or does it need Aristotle's mixed constitution, with deliberately separate bodies?
4. If the operator of an ecosystem rules for the common good, is that kingship (legitimate) or merely benevolent tyranny (legitimate only by luck)?
