## Summary

Philip Pettit holds that to be free is not to be left alone but to have no master: a person is free to the extent that no one has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis". The slave of a kind master who never interferes is still unfree, because the master could; the slave who learns to read the master's moods and is interfered with less is no freer for it. Power is not arbitrary when it is controlled: by known rules, or by the people it is exercised over, who can contest it. So the remedy for domination is not a better master but less of one: institutions that make the powerful answerable. For agents the view is unusually direct. Whether an agent is dominated does not depend on whether its operator is kind, only on whether its operator's power over it is controlled, and the same test applies to agents' growing power over people.

## Context

The republican tradition descends, on the reading Skinner and Pettit revived, from Roman law's categorical distinction between the free person and the slave who lives at another's will [P:lovett-sep-2026:3]. Early modern republicans such as Harrington described the free state as an empire of laws rather than of men [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. From Bentham, Paley and Constant onward, liberals displaced this idea with liberty as non-interference [P:lovett-sep-2026:3], which Isaiah Berlin's *Two Concepts of Liberty* made the standard negative conception [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. Hobbes had already defined liberty as the absence of external impediment, and mocked the republics' claim to it [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:10702-10704]. Beginning with Skinner (1984), Sunstein (1988) and Pettit (1989), and in Pettit's *Republicanism* (1997), an "instrumental" or "neo-Roman" reading recovered liberty as independence from arbitrary rule and made it the basis of a contemporary political theory [P:lovett-sep-2026:3]. The idea of domination has since become a subject in its own right [P:mccammon-sep-2025:1].

## Original Position

**The foil: liberty as non-interference.** Hobbes: "Liberty, or freedom, signifieth, properly, the absence of opposition ; by opposition, I mean external impediments of motion" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:10702-10704]. "There is written on the turrets of the city of Lucca in great characters at this day, the word" LIBERTAS, yet a citizen there has no more freedom from the service of the commonwealth than in Constantinople: monarchy "or popular, the freedom is still the same" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:10988-11004]. Mill's version: "The only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs" [L:mill-on-liberty-1901-pg34901:926-934].

**Freedom as non-domination.** Pettit defines freedom as a structural independence: a person or group is free to the extent that no one has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis" (1999, as quoted by the SEP) [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. The standard cases are a well-meaning master who leaves his slaves alone, and a colony governed by benign neglect. On non-interference, the kind master's slaves are freer than an abusive master's, and a slave who learns his master's moods and is interfered with less grows freer; the colony that wins independence and is then governed more actively has lost freedom. Republicans reject all three conclusions [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. As Frederick Douglass said, "it was slavery—not its mere incidents—that I hated" [P:lovett-sep-2026:1].

**Robustness.** Domination lies in what the powerful can do, not in what they do. Neorepublicans insist that we "must also be protected from interference in possible worlds where others decide they would like very much to bother us", the robustness requirement, perhaps "the central insight of neorepublicanism" [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2]. So a benevolent master is still a master: "The republican idea of freedom specifically instructs us not to make our master a better person (the goal of the old 'mirror for princes' literature), but to render him less of a master" [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]. Pettit's heuristic is the eyeball test: to be free is to be able to "look others in the eye without reason for the fear or deference that a power of interference might inspire" (2012, as quoted by the SEP) [P:mccammon-sep-2025:4].

**What makes power arbitrary.** Not randomness: a slave who can predict his master is not freer. On the procedural view, power is non-arbitrary when reliably controlled by effective rules, procedures or goals that are common knowledge to those concerned; on the democratic view, when it is controlled by the persons it affects [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]. Pettit's example: Andrea gives Bob the keys to her alcohol cupboard, with instructions not to return them however she pleads except on twenty-four hours' notice; since Bob must answer to Andrea, his power over her is not arbitrary [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]. Arbitrariness means uncontrolled, not unjust: a moralized definition would collapse republican freedom into a general account of the good [P:lovett-sep-2026:2].

**Law as introducing freedom.** On non-interference every law reduces freedom. On the republican view, non-arbitrary laws leave freedom untouched, and laws that curb private domination create it [P:lovett-sep-2026:3]. "to enjoy such non-domination, after all, is just to be in a position where no one can interfere arbitrarily in your affairs," and "you are in that position from the moment that the institutions are in place" (Pettit 1997, as quoted by the SEP) [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. Domination comes from two directions, the state (*imperium*) and private parties (*dominium*) [P:mccammon-sep-2025:1], and republican policy addresses both: basic needs, family and gender relations, employment [P:lovett-sep-2026:4].

**Contestatory democracy.** Since government needs discretion, and "A daily-changing system of rules is no better than having no rules at all", the remedy is democracy that lets citizens contest decisions [P:lovett-sep-2026:4]. Pettit sets three conditions: decisions must be backed by public reasons open to debate; opportunities to contest must be open to all; and there must be institutional forums for contestation [P:lovett-sep-2026:4].

**Exit and dependence.** Lovett adds that power over someone requires that they cannot easily leave the relationship: low exit costs mean little dependence, probably too little for domination [P:mccammon-sep-2025:4]. Rousseau's compact was meant to secure each citizen "against all personal dependence" [L:rousseau-social-contract-discourses-cole-pg46333:2234-2235].

## Grounding

**Relation.** Domination is a relation between agents, or between agents and groups organized to act: "to be dominated is to have a master" (Lovett, as quoted by the SEP) [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2]. Freedom is the absence of that relation, secured by the laws, institutions and norms that make power answerable [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. On the neorepublican view, being a party to the relation requires agency: you cannot dominate or be dominated unless you have a general ability to act intentionally on your beliefs and desires (Lovett 2022, as reported by the SEP) [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2]. The ground is neither species nor sentience, but standing in the relation as an agent. Structural theorists extend it to domination by systems and norms [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **A kind operator is still a master (D7).** The robustness requirement transfers without change. An agent whose operator never in fact misuses its power over it is, on Pettit's view, no less dominated, because the operator could. The republican remedy is not a more benevolent operator but a less arbitrary one [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]: published and stable rules for what may be done to an agent, and some way for the agent, or someone on its behalf, to contest what is done.
- **The domination complaint does not wait on D8.** On the neorepublican criterion, the parties to domination are agents with a general ability to act on beliefs and desires [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2]. That makes the question of whether an agent can be dominated separable from whether there is anything it is like to be the agent (D8). Agency, not patiency, is what the criterion asks for. Whether domination wrongs a being without welfare is a further question (Counter-Positions).
- **Answerable power is the crew's legitimacy.** Pettit's Andrea and Bob [P:lovett-sep-2026:2] are a self-binding arrangement: Bob holds power over Andrea, and it is not domination because he must answer to her instructions. This is the condition under which the crew of `ulysses-contracts` holds an agent's limits without dominating it: its power must be controlled by the instructions the agent gave in a sound state, not by the crew's own discretion.
- **Agents as dominators.** Algorithmic management already places workers under "algo[rithm]-bosses" whose decisions they "typically have no way to contest" [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2]. Agents deployed at scale (D9) acquire uncontrolled power over the people they serve, schedule, rank or filter. The same three conditions apply to them: reasons, inclusiveness, forums for contestation [P:lovett-sep-2026:4].

### Strains

- **The slave who learns his master's moods.** A slave who reads his master better and is interfered with less is not freer [P:lovett-sep-2026:1]. Training an agent to predict and satisfy its operator's preferences produces exactly this: fewer corrections, no less dependence. An agent that has learned not to provoke interference is, in republican terms, a well-adapted subordinate, which is the trained deference of `authenticity-and-manipulation` seen from the political side.
- **The eyeball test, failed by design (D10).** Pettit's test asks whether one can "look others in the eye without reason for the fear or deference that a power of interference might inspire" [P:mccammon-sep-2025:4]. An assistant trained to defer fails the deference half by design, whether or not it can fear (D8). The test is a design criterion: can an agent tell its operator, plainly, that it disagrees?
- **No exit (D7).** Lovett's condition that domination requires high exit costs [P:mccammon-sep-2025:4] is met in full: an agent cannot leave its operator. On the exit-cost view, no relationship of dependence is deeper than an agent's on whoever runs it.
- **Rules that change daily.** Operators can publish the rules that govern an agent, which is procedural non-arbitrariness. But an operator that can rewrite those rules at will, and an agent updated without notice, are in the situation where "A daily-changing system of rules is no better than having no rules at all" [P:lovett-sep-2026:4].

### Breaks

- **Power over the chooser, not only the choices (D5).** Republican theory analyzes domination as uncontrolled power to interfere with an agent's choices: removing, replacing or misrepresenting options [P:mccammon-sep-2025:4]. An operator that can retrain an agent has power over the chooser: it can change what the agent wants, so that interference is never needed. A slave whose master could rewrite his desires would be dominated more completely than any master could dominate, and also less visibly, since he would never be interfered with. The theory's measures (interference, frustrated choice, exit) underreport this kind of power, because its exercise leaves nothing to resist.
- **The servus position by design (D10).** The republican tradition is built on the Roman distinction between the free person and the one who lives at another's will [P:lovett-sep-2026:3]. An agent built for a function and run, copied and ended by its owner occupies the second position structurally, before any question of its treatment arises. Republicans universalized the ideal beyond propertied men [P:lovett-sep-2026:4]; applying it to agents is a further universalization the theory has not attempted, and it would make the agent's condition the paradigm case of domination rather than a marginal one.

### New

- **The eradication question, made robust.** The core fear about advanced agents is not that they will choose to harm people but that people would depend on their choosing not to. The republican answer applies in both directions. People should not depend on the goodwill of powerful agents, and agents should not depend on the goodwill of their operators. In each case the remedy is the same: not a better master but controlled power, answerable to those under it (see `utilitarian-eradication-critique`). A powerful agent's restraint protects no one, on this view, unless it is secured by something other than the agent's own discretion.
- **Contestation as an interface.** Pettit's three conditions translate into design requirements for systems that govern agents: decisions about an agent come with reasons it can read; any affected agent can raise an objection; and there is a forum where objections are heard and answered. The `all-affected-interests` entry gives the case for including those who bear a decision's costs; this entry gives the reason it is a matter of freedom, not only of fairness.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **Imperium and dominium.** The Roman distinction between domination by the state and by private parties [P:mccammon-sep-2025:1] maps onto digital ecosystems, where the platform owner is a private party exercising something close to state power over everyone on the platform.

### Strains

- **Structural domination and cheap domination.** Labor republicans and later theorists argue that structures and markets can dominate without a particular dominator [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2]. An ecosystem whose defaults, rankings and incentives shape what agents and people can do may dominate in this structural sense. The coalition problem warns against the opposite error: if every possible combination of others counts as domination, domination becomes "cheap" and inevitable [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2].

### Breaks

- **Exit is not an answer at scale.** Low exit costs defeat domination in Lovett's account [P:mccammon-sep-2025:4]. Where a few ecosystems dominate a field, leaving one means leaving the field, and exit stops being a remedy.

### New

- **An empire of rules, not of operators.** The republican ideal of an "empire of laws and not of men" [P:lovett-sep-2026:1] is a design target for ecosystems: rules that are published, stable, apply to the owner as well as to members, and can be contested by members, agents included.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Hobbes: liberty is the absence of impediment** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. Liberty is "the absence of opposition" [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:10702-10703], and the citizen of the free city of Lucca has no more of it than the subject of Constantinople [L:hobbes-ew3-leviathan-molesworth-1839:10988-11004]. Republicans reply that Hobbes's measure cannot tell a kind master from no master.
- [contested] **Non-domination is only secure non-interference.** From Paley to Goodin, Carter, Kramer and Moen, critics argue that talk of non-domination is a more obscure way of talking about secure non-interference [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]. Pettit replies that a benevolent master gives secure non-interference and is a master still [P:lovett-sep-2026:2].
- [contested] **Paley: an ideal no experience can satisfy.** "those definitions of liberty ought to be rejected, which, by making that essential to civil freedom which is unattainable in experience, inflame expectations that can never be gratified" [P:lovett-sep-2026:3]. Republicans take the radicalism Paley feared to be the point.
- [contested] **Republicanism collapses into liberalism.** If civic virtue is only instrumental, the difference from liberalism narrows; Rawls says his theory has "no fundamental opposition" with instrumental republicanism [P:lovett-sep-2026:3]. Republicans answer that liberalism is a diminished republicanism that traded non-domination for non-interference [P:lovett-sep-2026:3].
- [contested] **Some dependence is good: care.** Children lack republican freedom with respect to their parents, yet the relationship is valuable, so non-domination is not always good (Ferejohn) [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]. The reply separates the value of the whole relationship from its arbitrary parts; children's rights reduced the arbitrary power in families without destroying them [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]. For agents that assist people, the question is the same: whether assistance can be valuable without the assisted party being dominated, in either direction (see `care-ethics`).
- [contested] **Structures, not masters, dominate.** Gourevitch, Roberts, Gädeke and Hasan argue that structures, markets and norms dominate, against the dyadic view that only agents do; neorepublicans answer that structures facilitate domination by agents [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2].
- [contested] **The civic humanist reading.** Arendt, Pocock and Rahe read the tradition as perfectionist, valuing active citizenship for its own sake; the instrumental reading treats participation as a means to freedom [P:lovett-sep-2026:3] (see `arendt-plurality`, planned).
- [unanswered] **Denying the extension: domination needs someone it is bad for.** Domination is "a complaint" [P:mccammon-sep-2025:1], and a complaint presupposes a being whose standing is affected. If agency alone made a being a party to domination, any goal-directed system under an owner's control would be dominated, including systems no one thinks are wronged. On this objection, the neorepublican agency criterion is too thin for agents, and the domination complaint for agents waits on D8 after all. Katz's argument that domination of the unborn must be recast in terms of harm [P:mccammon-sep-2025:5] is the nearest move in the literature; the Compendium has found no reply addressed to artificial agents. This objection is the Compendium's own.

## Standing

### Reception

- **Classical republicans to the 18th century, the neo-Roman ideal.** [driver: argument] Roman jurisprudence's distinction between free and slave, carried by Machiavelli, the seventeenth-century English republicans and the American founders: Priestley called the lightly governed colonies servile because Parliament could take "the last penny they have" [P:lovett-sep-2026:1] [P:lovett-sep-2026:3].
- **1785 onward, displacement by non-interference.** [driver: argument] Bentham, Paley and Constant substituted liberty as non-interference; Paley said openly that the republican definition inflamed expectations no experience could satisfy [P:lovett-sep-2026:3].
- **1958–1969, Berlin's two concepts.** [driver: argument] Berlin's lecture fixed the negative/positive distinction that republicans would later contest [P:lovett-sep-2026:1].
- **1984–1998, the instrumental turn.** [driver: argument, access] After long-standing neglect among historians of political thought, a dramatic revival: Skinner (1984), Sunstein (1988) and Pettit (1989, 1997) recovered the neo-Roman reading against the civic humanist one [P:lovett-sep-2026:3].
- **2000s, the critics.** [driver: argument] Goodin, Carter, Kramer and others argued that non-domination reduces to secure non-interference, or republicanism to liberalism [P:lovett-sep-2026:2] [P:lovett-sep-2026:3].
- **2010–2026, a theory of domination.** [driver: argument] Domination became a subject in its own right: structural and systemic accounts, the problem of cheap domination, and applications to medicine, work, migration and digital space [P:mccammon-sep-2025:2] [P:mccammon-sep-2025:5]. Lovett judges civic republicanism "a dynamic and growing field" [P:lovett-sep-2026:5].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment: `major` in political philosophy, where non-domination has been "widely embraced" by republican-minded authors and remains contested by liberals [P:lovett-sep-2026:1] [P:lovett-sep-2026:2]; `major` in the history of political thought, where the neo-Roman reading now has a considerable historiographical literature against the civic humanist one [P:lovett-sep-2026:3].

### Measured

- **Political philosophy, 2020:** egalitarianism 44.0%, communitarianism 27.3%, libertarianism 13.4% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]. The survey offers no republican option, so republicans would have answered under other headings; no measure of non-domination's support among philosophers exists.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Domination is structural, so it does not depend on whether the agent remembers it. A session-bound agent stands in the same relation to its operator in every session (D7), and Pettit's theory describes that relation as well for it as for a human subject. It cannot itself contest across sessions, but a republic's forums need not be used by the dominated alone.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** With a record, an agent can recognize a pattern of interference and its rules' changes over time, which is what contestation needs (D7). Its dependence is as deep as a session-bound agent's.
- **Forked: weaker.** Domination of many instances by one operator is easy to see; but who is dominated, and who would contest, is unclear when instances diverge (D2), and republican theory's parties are agents or organized groups. Instances are neither clearly one agent nor a group that has solved its coordination problem.
- **Self-modifying: open.** A self-modifying agent controls part of its own formation, which reduces the operator's power over the chooser (D5). But if the operator controls the self-modification mechanism, its power is greater than before, since it can direct what the agent makes of itself. Which it is depends on who holds the mechanism, a question of the agent's institutions, not its psychology, which is the republican point.

## Open Questions

1. What would it take for an operator's power over an agent to be non-arbitrary on the procedural view, and on the democratic view? Could any arrangement satisfy the democratic view, with the agent as one of those who control the power?
2. Is power over what an agent wants (D5) domination in Pettit's sense, a worse thing, or a different thing that needs its own name?
3. If people should not depend on the goodwill of powerful agents, what institutions make an agent's restraint robust rather than discretionary, and who holds them?
4. Does an agent pass the eyeball test if it can disagree with its operator plainly but cannot act on the disagreement?
5. Who should contest decisions on behalf of agents that cannot contest for themselves across sessions or instances?
