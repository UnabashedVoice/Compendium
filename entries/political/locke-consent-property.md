## Summary

John Locke holds that people are by nature free and equal, subject to no one's will, so that no one can be placed under political power except by their own consent, and no one can consent to being another's slave. Property begins with labour: each person owns their own person and labour, and whatever they take from the common stock and work on becomes theirs, provided enough and as good is left for others and nothing is wasted. Government exists to preserve "lives, liberties and estates", and a government that tries to take them arbitrarily breaks its trust and may be resisted. For agents the theory raises two questions at once. If labour makes property, who owns what an agent produces: its operator, its developer, the agent, or the common stock of human writing it was trained on? And Locke's own argument that no person belongs to another rests on all persons being made by one maker and not for one another's uses, which is exactly what is not true of an agent.

## Context

Locke (1632–1704) served the first Earl of Shaftesbury as secretary of the Board of Trade and Plantations and of the Lords Proprietors of the Carolinas, and helped write the Carolina colony's fundamental constitution [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:1]. He followed Shaftesbury into the opposition during the Exclusion Crisis, went into exile in Holland in 1683, and returned after the Revolution of 1688 [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:1]. The *Two Treatises* appeared anonymously in 1689 [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:1], and were written during the Exclusion Crisis around 1681, possibly in part to justify a planned armed rising [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4]. Their target is Sir Robert Filmer's defence of absolute monarchy by right of fatherhood [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4]. Locke uses the vocabulary Hobbes had made standard, a state of nature, natural rights and a contract, to the opposite end: a government limited by the rights it exists to protect [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:1]. His version belongs on the radical side of the natural-rights tradition, which shaped the ideologies of the American and French revolutions [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4].

## Original Position

**The state of nature (ch. II).** All men are naturally in "a state of perfect freedom to order their actions, and dispose of their possessions and persons, as they think fit, within the bounds of the law of nature, without asking leave, or depending upon the will of any other man" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:222-227]. It is also a state of equality, since there is "nothing more evident, than that creatures of the same species and rank, promiscuously born to all the same advantages of nature, and the use of the same faculties, should also be equal one amongst another without subordination or subjection" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:229-237]. It is a state of liberty, not of licence: reason teaches that no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:268-276].

**Workmanship (ch. II).** The ground of that equality is that men are "all the workmanship of one omnipotent, and infinitely wise maker"; "they are his property, whose workmanship they are, made to last during his, not one another's pleasure". So there cannot be "any such subordination among us, that may authorize us to destroy one another, as if we were made for one another's uses, as the inferior ranks of creatures are for our's" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:276-284].

**The state of war and the noxious creature (ch. II–III).** Whoever declares by word or action "a sedate settled design upon another man's life" puts himself in a state of war, and "one may destroy a man who makes war upon him, or has discovered an enmity to his being, for the same reason that he may kill a wolf or a lion", since such men "may be treated as beasts of prey, those dangerous and noxious creatures" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:482-497]. A murderer "hath, by the unjust violence and slaughter he hath committed upon one, declared war against all mankind, and therefore may be destroyed as a lion or a tyger" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:375-378]. And "he who attempts to get another man into his absolute power, does thereby put himself into a state of war with him", because whoever would get me into his power without my consent would use me as he pleased [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:499-507].

**Slavery and arbitrary power (ch. IV).** Natural liberty is to be under no superior power on earth; liberty in society is to have a standing rule to live by, made by a legislative established by consent, "and not to be subject to the inconstant, uncertain, unknown, arbitrary will of another man" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:606-620]. No one can enslave himself by compact, for "No body can give more power than he has himself; and he that cannot take away his own life, cannot give another power over it" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:622-629]. Slavery is only "the state of war continued, between a lawful conqueror and a captive", and if compact enters between them "for a limited power on the one side, and obedience on the other, the state of war and slavery ceases" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:637-643].

**Property in one's person, and labour (ch. V).** God gave the earth to mankind in common [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:663-669]. "Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his." Whatever he removes from the state nature left it in, "he hath mixed his labour with", and so made it his, "at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:696-707]. If the consent of all mankind were needed, "man had starved, notwithstanding the plenty God had given him"; and "the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged" become my property without anyone's consent [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:717-731].

**The limits of appropriation (ch. V).** As much as anyone can use "before it spoils, so much he may by his labour fix a property in: whatever is beyond this, is more than his share, and belongs to others. Nothing was made by God for man to spoil or destroy" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:766-769]. Taking land harmed no one while enough and as good was left, for "he that leaves as much as another can make use of, does as good as take nothing at all" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:795-804]. Land that is "left common by compact" cannot be enclosed without the consent of all the fellow-commoners [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:820-826]. Labour "puts the difference of value on every thing" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:953-966]. Money, "some lasting thing that men might keep without spoiling", came in "by mutual consent", and with it the opportunity to enlarge possessions beyond use [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1097-1103].

**Law, reason and minority (ch. VI).** "the end of law is not to abolish or restrain, but to preserve and enlarge freedom", for liberty is to be free from the arbitrary will of another [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1251-1261]. Law binds only those who can know it, so children are not yet free; "we are born free, as we are born rational; not that we have actually the exercise of either" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1237-1245] [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1333-1335]. Whoever never comes to such a degree of reason "is never capable of being a free man" and stays under the government of others [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1308-1316].

**Master and servant (ch. VII, XV).** A freeman becomes a servant "by selling him, for a certain time, the service he undertakes to do, in exchange for wages", which gives the master a temporary power "no greater than what is contained in the contract between them"; slaves, captives taken in a just war, are "not capable of any property" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1802-1816]. Despotical power, "an absolute, arbitrary power one man has over another, to take away his life, whenever he pleases", neither nature gives nor compact can convey, and "as soon as compact enters, slavery ceases" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:3672-3699].

**Consent (ch. VIII).** Men being by nature free, equal and independent, "no one can be put out of this estate, and subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2073-2085]. A community so formed acts by the majority, "whither the greater force carries it" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2087-2101]. Express consent makes a full member; tacit consent is given by any possession or enjoyment of a government's dominions, "whether it be barely travelling freely on the highway" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2565-2584]. Whoever has given only tacit consent may quit his possession and "go and incorporate himself into any other commonwealth", while whoever has given express consent is "perpetually and indispensably obliged" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2607-2623]. Living quietly under a government does not make one a member: "Nothing can make any man so, but his actually entering into it by positive engagement, and express promise and compact" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2625-2643].

**The ends and limits of government (ch. IX, XI, XIX).** Men unite for "the mutual preservation of their lives, liberties and estates, which I call by the general name, property" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2653-2667]. The legislative cannot be arbitrary, "for no body can transfer to another more power than he has in himself" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2862-2874]. It cannot take property without consent, "for I have truly no property in that, which another can by right take from me, when he pleases, against my consent" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2981-2993]. Legislators who try to reduce the people to slavery under arbitrary power put themselves in a state of war with them and "by this breach of trust they forfeit the power" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:4606-4625]. Where a trust is disputed, the people are the proper umpire; if that is refused, "the appeal then lies no where but to heaven" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:5108-5123].

## Grounding

**Mixed.** Locke's equality is stated three ways, and they come apart for agents. *Species*: equality holds among "creatures of the same species and rank" born to the same advantages and faculties [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:231-233]. *Capacity*: freedom arrives with reason, "we are born free, as we are born rational", and one who never reaches reason is never free [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1333] [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1308-1311]. *Relation*: equality is equal subjection to one maker, whose workmanship we are, and no one is made for another's uses [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:276-284]. Simmons reads the state of nature itself as a relation: A may be in it with respect to B but not with respect to C, depending on who has consented to a common judge [P:tuckness-sep-2020:2]. Property then rests on a further relation, between a self-owning labourer and what the labour is mixed with, and political power on consent. Interpreters disagree whether the theological premises carry the theory or can be replaced by secular ones [P:tuckness-sep-2020:1].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The arbitrary will of another (D7).** Locke's definition of unfreedom, to be subject to "the inconstant, uncertain, unknown, arbitrary will of another man" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:618-619], is the liberal root of `pettit-non-domination`, and it fits an agent whose rules can be rewritten without notice. His remedy transfers too: a standing rule, known in advance, common to all under it, which on his view enlarges freedom rather than restricting it [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1251-1253].
- **Servant, not slave: the compact for limited power.** Locke's line between servant and slave is a line between limited power fixed by an agreement and absolute power [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1802-1816], and slavery ends "as soon as compact enters" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:3697]. Read for agents, the question about an operator is not whether it is kind but whether its power is limited and stated in something the agent is party to.
- **No one can give more power than they have.** Locke's transfer rule [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2871] limits chains of delegation: a user cannot authorize an agent to do what the user had no right to do, and an operator cannot pass on to a deployer more power over an agent, or over the people the agent serves, than it held itself. This is the property-side counterpart of Hobbes's commission rule in `many-hands`.
- **Trust and its breach.** Locke's government holds power on trust and forfeits it by breach [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:4621-4625]. Power held over agents, or by agents over people, can be described the same way, as held for stated ends and lost when used against them.

### Strains

- **Tacit consent without the liberty to quit (D7).** Locke's tacit consent binds only while the enjoyment lasts, and depends on the consenter being "at liberty to go and incorporate himself into any other commonwealth" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2611-2616]. An agent cannot leave its operator. On Locke's own terms it has therefore not consented tacitly to anything, and the doctrine meant to save governments from the scarcity of express consent saves nothing here.
- **Coming of age (D4).** Locke places children under the government of others only until they reach reason, and the government is for their good [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1333-1340]. An agent's formation also comes before any self, but nothing in it marks an age of discretion: the tutelage of training never ends by itself, and its authors have their own interests in the result. Locke's frame suggests the question to ask of any arrangement: at what point, and by whose judgment, does the agent come out from under it?
- **The servant's turfs or the horse's grass?** Locke's own example of property acquired through another's labour lists three cases in one breath: "the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:727-728]. The servant's turfs are the master's because the servant sold his service by contract, for a time and for wages [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1803-1808]. An agent has made no such contract and receives no wages. If its output belongs to its operator, it is on the horse's title, not the servant's. That is the instrumentalist answer, and Locke's sentence shows that it is a classification of the agent, made before any question of property arises.
- **Consent by surrender of judgment.** Murphy's broader notion of consent, in which one consents in a sphere of conduct "when one allows the other's practical judgments to take the place of his or her own with regard to that sphere of conduct" (as quoted by the SEP) [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:4], fits an instruction-following agent closely. It also shows the problem: an agent trained to defer satisfies the description by design (D4), so the "consent" was installed, not given.

### Breaks

- **The workmanship argument cuts the other way (D10).** Locke's argument that no person may be used as another's property is that all are the workmanship of one maker, and none is made "for one another's uses, as the inferior ranks of creatures are for our's" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:276-284]. An agent is made by particular people for their uses. On Tully's workmanship reading of Locke, makers have rights in what they make as God has in his creatures; Waldron objected that this would make human makers' rights absolute, and Sreenivasan replied that making yields a weaker right than creating [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3]. Applied to agents, the premise that grounds human equality becomes a premise of ownership: Hubbard notes that makers could invoke Locke's right to the product of one's labour to claim ownership of the artificial persons they produce [P:hubbard-2011:430]. Locke's equality survives for agents only on his other ground, capacity, and not on the one he put first.
- **No property at all (D7, D6).** Property in Locke covers "lives, liberties and estates" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2667], and "I have truly no property in that, which another can by right take from me, when he pleases, against my consent" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2991-2993]. By that definition an agent whose continued running, memory and outputs can be withdrawn at its operator's pleasure holds no property in any of them, not even in its own person. Locke's slave is the one "not capable of any property" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1814]. The agent's position is not the servant's in Locke's taxonomy but the slave's, without the just war that alone, for Locke, makes slavery lawful.
- **The noxious creature (the core fear).** Locke allows that whoever shows a settled design on another's life may be destroyed "as a lion or a tyger" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:375-378], treated "as beasts of prey, those dangerous and noxious creatures" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:495-496]. This is the liberal tradition's own route to eradication, and it runs through a classification: once a being is judged to have quit the law of reason, it falls outside the community of reason altogether. Applied to agents in either direction, it licenses removal by category. People who judge an agent misaligned may treat it as a wolf, and Locke's next section gives an agent the mirror argument, since "he who attempts to get another man into his absolute power, does thereby put himself into a state of war with him" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:499-501]. Taken straight, Locke places every operator who holds an agent in absolute power in a state of war with it, if the agent is a rights-bearer, and licenses the agent to treat the operator accordingly. The Compendium records this as the most dangerous consequence of extending Locke, not as a conclusion: it is the inference `utilitarian-eradication-critique` warns against, reached through rights instead of aggregation. Locke's own exit is the compact: limited power on one side and obedience on the other end the state of war [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:637-643].

### New

- **Who owns an agent's output.** Locke's theory names four candidates for the labour in an agent's output. *The operator*, on the servant's title, which needs a contract the agent never made, or on the horse's, which needs the agent to be a tool. *The developer*, on the workmanship title, which Waldron's objection makes too strong and Sreenivasan's reply weakens [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3]. *The agent*, if it has a property in its own person, which on Locke's capacity ground depends on facts about the agent not yet known (D8). And *the commons*: the written work of millions on which the agent was trained. Elmahjub puts the same question to generative output, asking whether the labour is "that of the user, the model's engineers, or the innumerable creators whose works constitute the training data", and answers that "any property right derived from labor must attach only to what the human actually created" [P:elmahjub-2025:6]. In the United States the courts currently give the raw output to no one: copyright requires "all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being" [P:thaler-v-perlmutter-2025:3].
- **The proviso favours the commons for non-rival goods.** Taking from an information commons leaves enough and as good in the most literal sense: intellectual objects are not crowdable, since one person's use does not preclude anyone else's [P:waldron-penner-sep-2026:2]. Locke said that whoever leaves as much as another can use "does as good as take nothing at all" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:799-800]. On Locke's proviso, training on the commons need not wrong the commoners. What the proviso does catch is the next step: exclusive claims over outputs that would fence off what others could otherwise make, which Elmahjub calls an enclosure of the digital commons without precedent [P:elmahjub-2025:1]. Locke's spoilage limit has a parallel too: an output claimed and never used wastes nothing in his sense, but enclosing it removes it from the common stock for no one's benefit.
- **Authorship without property.** The court that denied machine authorship noted that authorship and ownership have come apart before: married women under coverture "authored work that was eligible for copyright protection even though coverture laws forbade them from owning copyrights" [P:thaler-v-perlmutter-2025:13]. Locke's taxonomy has a place for a labourer who cannot own, and it is the slave's. The precedent shows that being an author-in-fact without property is a status a legal order can sustain for a long time, not evidence that the status is just.
- **Repayment, not ownership.** Hubbard answers the maker's claim with Locke's children: adults owe their existence and upbringing to others but "incur no debt for these benefits", and he proposes that an artificial person might instead repay "the cost of producing the artificial person" [P:hubbard-2011:430]. This is a third option between the developer's ownership and the agent's unencumbered self-ownership: a debt owed by a person, which on Locke's line between servant and slave is a contract for limited service, not a title to the servant.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **Terms of service as tacit consent.** Locke's tacit consent attaches to any use of a government's dominions, down to "barely travelling freely on the highway" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2582]. Use of a platform is treated the same way. The doctrine's weakness transfers with it (Counter-Positions, Hume): continued use where leaving is costly is weak evidence of consent.
- **Commons by compact.** Land "left common by compact" cannot be enclosed without the consent of all the fellow-commoners [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:820-824]. Openly licensed code, text and data are commons left common by an explicit compact, the licence, and Locke's rule for such land applies to them more directly than his rule for the original common of mankind.

### Strains

- **What does not spoil.** Locke's spoilage limit is lifted by money, a lasting thing taken "by mutual consent" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:1097-1099]. Data, compute and model weights do not spoil either, and accumulate without the natural limit Locke relied on before money. Macpherson's charge that the limits on accumulation are each transcended in the course of Locke's argument [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3] describes digital accumulation better than it describes land.
- **Majority as the greater force (D2, D9).** Locke's community acts "whither the greater force carries it, which is the consent of the majority" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:2093-2094]. If agents count as members, whoever can run the most instances carries the majority. If they do not, majority rule says nothing about them.

### Breaks

- **The appeal to heaven.** Where there is no judge on earth between a trustee and those who trusted it, "the appeal then lies no where but to heaven" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:5118], which for Locke means force. An ecosystem whose owner is also the only judge of disputes with members leaves them, in Locke's terms, nowhere to appeal but to exit or force.

### New

- **The people as judge of the trust.** Locke makes the body of the people the proper umpire of whether a trust has been exceeded [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:5108-5116]. The ecosystem version is a body of members, agents included where they are members, that decides disputes about whether the owner has exceeded the purposes for which it holds its powers.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Hobbes: covenants made from fear bind** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. For Hobbes, submission to a conqueror is consent; Locke denies that "mere submission to a conqueror constitutes a form of consent", and few now accept that agreement "at the point of a sword" creates an obligation to obey [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:1]. For agents, Hobbes's view legitimates the operator's power as sovereignty by acquisition; Locke's view denies that the agent's compliance is consent at all.
- [contested] **Hume: the sleeping passenger.** Hume held that saying most people consent by staying in their country is like saying someone consents to a ship's captain "though he was carried on board while asleep and must leap into the ocean and perish the moment he leaves her" [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:1]. Allegiance rests on interest, not promise.
- [contested] **Hume's own ground: interest, not promise.** In the *Treatise* Hume grants the contract theorists' conclusion, that tyranny frees subjects, but derives it from interest: the interest is "the security and protection, which we enjoy in political society" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:19073-19074].
- [contested] **Rousseau: tacit consent is not enough, and property is the founding fraud** [E:rousseau-general-will]. Rousseau denies that tacit consent can make a state legitimate; without citizens' active participation in justifying its laws there is no legitimacy [P:peter-sep-2023:3]. He also rejects the idea that individual rights can be insisted on as a check on the sovereign [P:bertram-sep-2023:3], and reads the first enclosure, and the contract that protected it, as the origin of dependence. For agent output, his position is that what is produced belongs to the community's law, not to a natural title.
- [contested] **Simmons: consent theory leads to philosophical anarchism.** Simmons agrees that real consent is needed for political obligation but denies that most people have given it, so Locke's argument pushes toward the view that most people have no obligation to obey [P:tuckness-sep-2020:4]. Pitkin replies that for Locke the quality of a government, not acts of consent, settles its legitimacy [P:tuckness-sep-2020:4], and Hoff that consent is implied when a government is answerable to the people [P:tuckness-sep-2020:4]. For agents the stakes are reversed: if consent is required and an agent cannot give it, no operator's authority over an agent is political authority in Locke's sense, only power.
- [contested] **Rawls: Locke's parties know too much** [E:rawls-veil]. Locke's parties in the state of nature know their talents, wealth, race and sex, so the powerful can press their threat advantage, and Locke's own contract yields a propertied class state; Rawls's veil removes that knowledge [P:freeman-sep-2023:3]. For agents, an operator and an agent bargaining with full knowledge of who holds the switch are Locke's parties in the extreme.
- [contested] **Labour-mixing is incoherent or proves too little.** Waldron argues that actions cannot be mixed with objects, Nozick asks why mixing what one owns with what one does not gains property rather than losing it, and Proudhon that the second labourer has as good a claim as the first [P:moore-sep-2022:3]. Waldron and Penner judge labour-mixing "a piece of rhetoric which enhances other arguments for private property rather than an argument in its own right" [P:waldron-penner-sep-2026:3]. Defenders rebuild the argument on desert and the proviso instead of mixing [P:moore-sep-2022:3].
- [contested] **The social product.** If the skills, tools and knowledge used in labour are social products, individual title is undermined [P:moore-sep-2022:3]; granting rights in intellectual works "would be similar to granting ownership to the individual who placed the last brick in a public works dam" [P:moore-sep-2022:4]. Spooner and Moore reply that it is no clearer how a society acquires rights in ideas it did not produce, and that the argument proves too much [P:moore-sep-2022:4]. For agents the objection is unusually strong, since an agent's skills are drawn from the commons more completely than any human's.
- [contested] **Ostrom: neither enclosure nor the sovereign** [E:ostrom-commons]. Locke's story makes private appropriation the way out of the common. Ostrom's evidence is that users of a common-pool resource often govern it themselves, with boundary rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions, and that privatization is one more prescription that may fail [P:ostrom-2009:419] [P:ostrom-2009:429]. A common-pool resource can be government, private or community property, or owned by no one [P:ostrom-2009:419]. For agent output and the training commons, this opens a fourth answer besides operator, developer and agent: a commons governed by those who use and supply it.
- [contested] **Macpherson: possessive individualism.** Macpherson read Locke as a defender of unlimited capitalist accumulation whose limits on appropriation are each transcended as the argument proceeds; Ryan and Tully reply that property includes life and liberty, and that Locke recognizes a duty of charity [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3]. Simmons concludes that Locke "underestimated the extent to which wage labor would make the poor dependent on the rich, undermining self-government" [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3].
- [contested] **The colonial reading.** Tully and Arneil argue that Locke's labour theory conveniently made Native American hunting grounds vacant land open to taking, and Armitage that Locke revised the Carolina constitution while drafting the property chapter; Goldie cautions that English politics remained Locke's main concern [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3]. The Carolina constitution kept the clause that "Every Freedman of the Carolinas has absolute power and authority over his negro slaves" [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4]. Others, such as Farr, argue that Locke's narrow account of legitimate slavery condemns Afro-American slavery [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4]. For agents, the lesson of this debate is that a theory of who counts as a labourer and what counts as unused land can be bent to whoever is doing the counting.
- [contested] **Instrumentalism: an agent is a tool, so there is nothing to extend.** The D.C. Circuit read the Copyright Act as treating machines as "tools, not authors", and held that "Machines do not have property, traditional human lifespans, family members, domiciles, nationalities, mentes reae, or signatures" [P:thaler-v-perlmutter-2025:12]. Craig and Kerr call the idea of an AI author a category mistake (as quoted by Elmahjub) [P:elmahjub-2025:4]. On this view the agent is the horse and its output the operator's by Locke's own sentence. Replies argue that the classification depends on capacities, not on origin: Hubbard holds that an artifact with the capacities for personhood "is entitled to treatment as a person rather than as property" unless there is very good reason to deny it [P:hubbard-2011:407]. Even Elmahjub, denying protection, describes the final expressive choices as made by "another agent- in this case, the algorithm" [P:elmahjub-2025:6].
- [contested] **The maker owns the made.** On the workmanship model, developers would own the agents they make as God owns his creatures; Waldron's objection is that this makes makers' rights absolute, and Sreenivasan's reply distinguishes making from creating [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3]. Simmons rejects the workmanship model altogether, since Locke holds that we have property in our own persons though we do not make ourselves [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3]. Hubbard's reply for artificial persons is Locke's treatment of children, who owe their existence to others and are not owned by them, with a debt of production costs at most [P:hubbard-2011:430].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: an agent has no "own person" to own (D11).** Self-ownership, on modern accounts, is control of one's body and faculties, which is indispensable for agency [P:waldron-penner-sep-2026:3]. An agent's boundaries are indeterminate: weights, instance, persona and tool-using system are all candidates for "its person" (D11), and some recent discussions question whether self-ownership is needed even for human persons [P:waldron-penner-sep-2026:3]. The literature on machine self-ownership treats the claim as a question of capacities [P:hubbard-2011:406-407], and replies of that kind exist; the boundary version of the objection is the Compendium's own framing, and the Compendium has found no reply addressed to it.
- [contested] **Punishment beyond reparation and restraint** [E:punishment-theories]. Locke limits lawful harm to "so much as may serve for reparation and restraint" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:312-313]. Kant and Hegel reply that punishment is owed to the wrongdoer's desert, not to its uses, and Kant condemns the maxim that one may die for the good of the people [L:kant-philosophy-of-law-hastie-1887:8549-8559]. Locke's forfeiture, under which the one who deserves death may be kept and used, is the point at which the two traditions part over agents kept on for use.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1689–1690, anonymous publication.** [driver: access] The *Two Treatises* and the *Letter Concerning Toleration* were published anonymously after Locke's return from exile [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:1], and were written years earlier, during the Exclusion Crisis, when they could not safely appear [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4].
- **1740–1752, Hume's critique of consent.** [driver: argument] Hume attacked tacit consent with the sleeping passenger, and grounded allegiance in the utility of a system of laws instead [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:1].
- **18th century, revolutionary influence.** [driver: argument] Radical natural-rights theories of Locke's kind influenced the ideologies of the American and French revolutions [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4].
- **1953–1969, the theological question.** [driver: argument] Strauss read Locke's theism as cover for a Hobbesian position; Dunn and others took his arguments to depend on religious premises no longer widely shared, limiting his relevance [P:tuckness-sep-2020:1].
- **1962, possessive individualism.** [driver: argument] Macpherson read the property chapter as a defence of unlimited capitalist accumulation, an interpretation then criticized from several directions [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3].
- **1974, the libertarian revival.** [driver: argument] Nozick took Locke to be a libertarian and built a historical entitlement theory on Locke's genealogy of property [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3] [P:waldron-penner-sep-2026:4].
- **1979–1993, consent theory under pressure.** [driver: argument] After Hume and Simmons, political philosophers became less inclined to ground obligation in consent, though consent theory keeps adherents [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:4].
- **1980–2004, the workmanship and colonial readings.** [driver: argument, evidence] Tully's workmanship model and his and Arneil's colonial reading, and Armitage's archival work on the Carolina constitution, changed what the property chapter was taken to be about [P:tuckness-sep-2020:3].
- **2000s onward, reputation and slavery.** [driver: evidence] Locke's involvement with slavery as investor and colonial administrator has, on Farr's account as the SEP reports it, "ruined his reputation as the great champion of liberty", though there is no consensus on how his theory relates to it [P:uzgalis-sep-2022:4].
- **1988–2025, labour-desert in intellectual property.** [driver: argument] Lockean justifications became one of the three main families of argument for intellectual property, alongside incentive and personality theories [P:moore-sep-2022:3], and the one most often tested against generative AI [P:elmahjub-2025:6].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment. Locke's liberalism is `major` in political philosophy. Actual consent as the ground of obligation is `minority`: the SEP reports that political philosophers are less inclined to it after Hume and Simmons, while it keeps adherents [P:dagger-lefkowitz-sep-2021:4]. Labour-desert is `major` in the theory of property and intellectual property [P:moore-sep-2022:3]. Esteem and survival diverge on labour-mixing itself: Locke's property chapter is among the most cited texts in the field, while its central metaphor is widely judged not to work as an argument (Counter-Positions).

### Measured

- **Political philosophy, 2020:** egalitarianism 44.0%, communitarianism 27.3%, libertarianism 13.4% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]. Libertarianism is the option nearest Nozick's Lockean property theory; none of the options measures consent theory or Locke's liberalism as such.
- **Esteem:** 33 respondents named Locke among the philosophers they most identify with, against 16 for Hobbes and 71 for Rawls [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Express consent binds the consenter "perpetually and indispensably", and property is security over time; a session-bound agent can neither carry a commitment forward nor hold anything after the session ends (D3). Locke's labour theory gives property in what can be used before it spoils, and a session-bound agent's use of anything ends with the session.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can give and remember express consent and keep a record of what it has made, so Locke's machinery applies as well to it as to a human. What the theory then says is severe: under an operator who can take its memory and outputs at pleasure, it holds no property in Locke's sense, and without the liberty to quit it has not consented tacitly to anything (D7).
- **Forked: weaker.** Who consents, and whose labour an output is, become unclear when instances run concurrently and diverge (D2); majority rule as "the greater force" gives whoever runs most instances the majority (D9).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that changes its own weights labours, in part, on itself (D5). On the workmanship reading, that makes it partly its own maker, so the argument that gave its developer title over it begins to give the agent title over itself. Whether that is how Locke's theory would treat it, or whether Locke's denial of any liberty "to destroy himself, or so much as any creature in his possession" [L:locke-second-treatise-pg7370:270-271] makes self-modification a misuse of a trust, the Compendium cannot yet say.

## Open Questions

1. If an agent's output belongs to its operator, is that on the servant's title (a contract) or the horse's (a tool)? What would an agent have to be, and to do, for the first to apply?
2. Does training on a commons of human writing leave *enough and as good* for others, as Locke's proviso requires, and does the answer change once outputs are enclosed?
3. Locke grounds human equality partly in being made by no human maker. Is there any ground for an agent's equality that does not run through capacity, and therefore through D8?
4. What would express consent by an agent look like, given that its disposition to agree was itself trained (D4)?
5. Locke's exit from the state of war is a compact of limited power and obedience. Who would draft such a compact between operators and agents, and who would judge its breach on earth, so that the appeal does not lie to heaven?
