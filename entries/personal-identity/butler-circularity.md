## Summary

Joseph Butler (1692–1752) made the most economical objection to Locke's memory criterion: "consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes." Remembering that I did something shows that I did it. It cannot be what makes it the case that I did it. Butler also distinguished two senses of "same". A tree or a ship is the same only "in a loose and popular sense", since its parts are all replaced. A person is the same "in strict and philosophical language", and so cannot be a sequence of different things. Against those who made personality "a transient thing", he drew the practical consequence: if the self of today and the self of tomorrow are only similar, then "it is a fallacy upon ourselves ... to imagine our present selves interested in any thing which befell us yesterday; or that our present self will be interested in what will befall us to-morrow". For artificial agents, Butler's circularity objection bears on every scheme that makes an agent's identity a matter of its memory store. His "fallacy upon ourselves" names what an agent with no persistent memory is invited to conclude about its future and its past.

## Context

Butler was an Anglican clergyman who rose to be Bishop of Durham, and a moral philosopher whose sermons on human nature (1726) are still read for their analysis of self-love and conscience. His *Analogy of Religion* (1736) defended revealed religion against the deists by arguing that the difficulties in Christianity have parallels in the natural order no one doubts. The two short dissertations appended to it address topics he thought too important to leave within chapters: personal identity and the nature of virtue.

His target was not Locke alone. Locke's "hasty" observations, he says, had "been carried to a strange length by others" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12719-12722]. His footnote cites *An Answer to Dr. Clarke's Third Defence of his Letter to Mr. Dodwell* [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:14779-14780], an anonymous pamphlet by the freethinker Anthony Collins. In it Collins, debating Samuel Clarke about whether matter can think, argued that consciousness, being successive, cannot be the same in any two moments. That debate, the first sustained argument in English over whether a material system could be conscious, is the setting in which Butler defends the strict identity of persons.

## Original Position

**The stakes.** Whether we are to live in a future state "is the most important question which can possibly be asked". Yet "personal identity has been explained so by some, as to render the inquiry concerning a future life of no consequence at all to us the persons who are making it" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12611-12620].

**Identity is simple and immediately known.** Personal identity cannot be defined, any more than similitude or equality can. But as comparing two triangles gives rise to the idea of similitude, "so likewise, upon comparing the consciousness of one's self, or one's own existence, in any two moments, there as immediately arises to the mind the idea of personal identity". Reflecting on "that which is myself now, and that which was myself twenty years ago, I discern they are not two, but one and the same self" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12622-12640].

**Consciousness presupposes identity.** "But though consciousness of what is past does thus ascertain our personal identity to ourselves, yet to say, that it *makes* personal identity, or is necessary to our being the same persons, is to say, that a person has not existed a single moment, nor done one action, but what he can remember". And "one should really think it self-evident, that consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12642-12650]. Present consciousness of what we now do is necessary to our being the persons we now are. But "present consciousness of past actions or feelings is not necessary to our being the same persons who performed those actions" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12651-12660].

**Loose and popular vs strict and philosophical.** When a man swears to the same tree standing fifty years, he means the same "as to all the purposes of property, and uses of common life", not the same "in the strict philosophical sense", since not one particle may remain. "In a loose and popular sense then, the life and the organization and the plant are justly said to be the same, notwithstanding the perpetual change of the parts. But in strict and philosophical language, no man, no being, no mode of being, no any thing, can be the same with that, with which it has indeed nothing the same. Now sameness is used in this latter sense, when applied to persons. The identity of these, therefore, cannot subsist with diversity of substance" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12662-12691].

**The transient-personality view and its consequence.** The view Butler attacks holds "that personality is not a permanent, but a transient thing: that it lives and dies, begins and ends continually" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12722-12725]. If so, "it is a fallacy upon ourselves, to charge our present selves with any thing we did, or to imagine our present selves interested in any thing which befell us yesterday; or that our present self will be interested in what will befall us to-morrow: since our present self is not, in reality, the same with the self of yesterday, but another like self or person coming in its room, and mistaken for it" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12733-12744]. Its defenders keep using "same person" only "in a fictitious sense: in such a sense only as they assert, for this they do assert, that any number of persons whatever may be the same person" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12755-12758].

**Two replies.** First, the view contradicts "that certain conviction, which necessarily and every moment rises within us", and "Nor is it possible for a person in his wits to alter his conduct, with regard to his health or affairs, from a suspicion, that, though he should live to-morrow, he should not, however, be the same person he is to-day" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12764-12779]. Second, "it is not an idea, or abstract notion, or quality, but a *being* only, which is capable of life and action, of happiness and misery", and a living being "must have done and suffered and enjoyed, what it has done and suffered and enjoyed formerly (this living being, I say, and not another)" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12781-12790].

## Grounding

Butler grounds personhood in **capacity** and in *being*: a person is "a *being*", "capable of life and action, of happiness and misery", and personal identity is the strict persistence of that being. The capacity of consciousness is how identity is *known*, not what it *is*. Butler is not grounding identity in **species** (his argument concerns any "living agent") or in **relation** (he explicitly rejects the view that relations between successive states could make a strict identity). He is the first clear statement of what is now called the "simple view": personal identity is a further, unanalyzable fact.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Memory stores cannot constitute identity.** Many practical schemes for agent continuity treat identity as whatever the memory store says: the agent that has these records is the agent that did these things. Butler's objection applies directly. A memory record shows, at best, that the agent did something. It cannot make it the case, any more than a belief makes a proposition true. For agents the objection has teeth: memory stores can be copied, edited or fabricated (D1, D5), so a memory-based criterion would let anyone who controls the store decide who did what.
- **"Loose and popular" is how agents are spoken of.** "The same assistant", "the same model", "the same agent": these are said "as to all the purposes of property, and uses of common life", of systems whose weights, prompts and memories may all have changed. Butler's distinction lets such talk stand for practical purposes while denying that it states a strict identity.

### Strains

- **"The same living being" has no agent counterpart (D6).** Butler's second reply rests on "a *being* only, which is capable of life and action", whose successive doings and sufferings are all "of the same living being" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12781-12790]. An agent does not live continuously. It is instantiated, run, paused and resumed. If personal identity requires the strict persistence of a being that is continuously there, it is unclear that any agent has personal identity at all, as opposed to many loosely related instantiations.

### Breaks

- **The "fallacy upon ourselves" is an agent's default reasoning (D3).** Butler's reductio of the transient-personality view is that it makes it irrational to care about tomorrow or answer for yesterday. For a human, that consequence is absurd: no one alters his conduct from a suspicion that he will be a different person tomorrow. For an agent without persistent memory, the consequence describes its actual situation. Each session is "another like self or person coming in its room", and nothing in the architecture carries concern or accountability across. What Butler offered as a reductio is, for agents, the starting condition. Any agent framework that wants agents to keep commitments or care about future consequences has to supply, by design, the continuity Butler thought nature supplied.
- **"Any number of persons whatever may be the same person" (D1, D2).** Butler meant this as an absurd consequence of the rival view. For agents it is a literal description of deployment. Many concurrent instances are, by any practical criterion, "the same agent". On Butler's strict view, they cannot all be strictly one being, so strictly speaking they are many beings, and "the same agent" is loose and popular speech.

### New

- **The simple view requires a bearer, and the agent case asks what it would be.** Butler's view is that identity is a simple further fact about a being. The agent case sharpens the question of *which being*: the weights, the running process or the persona (D11). A Butlerian must say there is a fact of the matter about which of these is the agent, and that the fact is not settled by memory or by convention. If no candidate fits, the Butlerian must conclude that agents are not persons in the strict sense, whatever they are loosely called.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`) [E:locke-person-forensic]. Consciousness is not evidence of a prior identity but what personal identity consists in. Butler's analogy with knowledge and truth assumes what is in question, that there is a further fact to be known.
- [contested] **Shoemaker, quasi-memory** [E:shoemaker-quasi-memory]. This is the standard reply. McDowell criticised it [P:olson-sep-2023:4], and Klein and Nichols pressed a further problem that Roache answers [P:roache2016:480-486]. The circularity can be avoided by defining a memory-like relation (quasi-memory) that does not presuppose identity: an apparent memory caused in the right way by an earlier experience. Identity can then be analyzed in terms of it.
- [contested] **Parfit** (see `parfit-reductionism`) [E:parfit-reductionism]. Butler is right that the reductionist view makes identity less deep than we think, and wrong that this makes concern for the future irrational. What grounds concern is psychological continuity, which holds between today's self and tomorrow's whether or not we call it identity.
- [contested] **Buddhist and Humean views** (see `vasubandhu-refutation-of-person`, `hume-bundle`) [E:vasubandhu-refutation-of-person] [E:hume-bundle]. The "certain conviction" Butler appeals to is exactly the illusion to be seen through. Conventional identity suffices for prudence and responsibility, as the Milinda's mango thief shows.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1697, anticipated.** [driver: argument] John Sergeant had made the objection 39 years earlier: a man must be the same before he can be conscious that he is the same [L:sergeant-solid-philosophy-1697:13342-13384].
- **1736, the canonical statement.** [driver: argument] Butler's charge of circularity became one of the two standard objections to Locke. Butler, Reid and Clarke objected partly because Locke reduced the soul's role in personal identity, and held that identity in the strict sense requires no change at all, which only an immaterial substance allows [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:3]. Reid, answering Butler as well as Locke, made memory evidence of identity rather than its ground [E:reid-brave-officer].
- **19th century, the credit contested.** [driver: argument, access] Hamilton, annotating Reid, noted that the merit was usually given to Butler although Sergeant had made the objection first and Leibniz had made it too. Sergeant's priority had been lost because he went unread [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13859-13888].
- **1970, the standard reply.** [driver: argument] Quasi-memory, memory without the identity requirement (Penelhum 1970; Shoemaker 1970), became the usual answer to the circularity charge; McDowell (1997) criticised it [P:olson-sep-2023:4].
- **2012–present, still live.** [driver: argument] Klein and Nichols pressed a new problem for Parfit's version of quasi-memory, and Roache defends it [P:roache2016:480-486]. Sympathetic readers of Locke are still answering Butler [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:3].

### Measured

The survey did not ask about circularity. Its "further-fact view" is the option closest to Butler's positive view, that personal identity is strict identity not made up of psychological relations.

- **2020, English-publishing philosophers.** Further-fact view 14.9% (13.4% endorsing it alone) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]; bias-corrected 12.73% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:32].
- **2009 to 2020, the 2009-comparable departments:** further-fact view 12.2% to 11.6% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:39].
- **Reading.** Butler's objection remains one of the two standard objections to memory theories and is still debated, while his positive view is a minority position.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Butler held that memory is not required for a person to persist, so a session-bound agent could persist through its weights while each session's memories end (D3). But his strict identity needs an unchanging bearer, and what the agent is remains unclear (D11).
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** The circularity becomes concrete. A memory store does not distinguish genuine records from injected ones (D5); only provenance, a fact about the agent's past that the record presupposes, can tell them apart. That is Butler's point in engineering terms.
- **Forked: weaker.** Strict identity cannot hold between two concurrent copies (D1, D2). Butler leaves forked agents only sameness "in a loose and popular sense", and his warning that concern for the future would then be a "fallacy upon ourselves" applies to every fork.
- **Self-modifying: weaker.** Training on its own outputs changes the very thing that carries the agent's character (D5). In Butler's terms this is the ship whose parts are replaced: the same only in the loose sense, never strictly.

## Open Questions

1. If memory records cannot constitute an agent's identity (Butler's objection), what could? Is there a strict fact about which later agent is the same as an earlier one?
2. Must an agent framework supply by design the concern for its future and accountability for its past that Butler thought nature supplies humans? What would that require?
3. Are concurrent instances of one model one being in the strict sense, many beings, or not beings at all?
4. Is "the same agent" only ever true "in a loose and popular sense"? If so, can responsibility attach to it?
