## Summary

Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500 BCE) observed that fresh waters keep flowing onto those who step into the same rivers. Read one way, this means nothing persists: you cannot step twice into the same river, and his follower Cratylus said not even once. Read the other way, which modern scholarship mostly favors, the river stays the same *because* its waters keep changing, so that identity belongs to a regulated pattern (a measure, *logos*), not to stuff. The comic poet Epicharmus drew the forensic consequence as a joke: if a man is always changing, the man who borrowed yesterday is not the man who owes today. These fragments pose the question the rest of the domain inherits: does anything persist through change, and if so, what? An artificial agent inverts Heraclitus' river. Its weights are fixed during deployment while its context flows. That makes the choice between identity as stuff and identity as pattern practical, not speculative.

## Context

Heraclitus wrote a single book, now lost, in a deliberately oracular style. Ancient readers called him "the obscure", and Socrates is reported to have said of it: "What I have understood is good; and so, I think, what I have not understood is; only the book requires a Delian diver to get at the meaning of it" [L:diogenes-laertius-lives-yonge-pg57342:2557-2559]. What survives are about a hundred fragments quoted by later authors, often for their own purposes. The river sayings survive in three versions quoted by three different authors, and which one is authentic shapes the whole interpretation.

The stakes were cosmological. The Milesians before him looked for the one stuff everything is made of. Heraclitus proposed that the world is "an ever-living Fire, with measures kindling, and measures going out" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:8191-8192] (Burnet fr. 20; Diels B30). What is constant in the world is the proportion in which things change, not any stuff. The river is his image for that idea applied to a single thing.

Epicharmus, working in Syracuse in roughly the same generation, turned the thought into stage comedy. It entered philosophy under the name the *growing argument* (*auxanomenos logos*). Centuries later, the Academic skeptics used it seriously against the Stoics (see `chrysippus-dion-theon`), and Plutarch discusses it in connection with whether it is just to punish anyone, or any city, for the past.

## Original Position

**The river fragments.** Burnet renders the river sayings as two fragments. The first, his fr. 41–42, combines what Diels numbered B91 and B12: "You cannot step twice into the same rivers; for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:8326-8327]. The second, his fr. 81 (Diels B49a, whose authenticity is doubted), reads: "We step and do not step into the same rivers; we are and are not" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:8509-8510]. Plato reports the strong version: "Heracleitus is supposed to say that all things are in motion and nothing at rest; he compares them to the stream of a river, and says that you cannot go into the same water twice" [L:plato-cratylus-jowett-pg1616:3688-3690]. Plutarch alludes to it too, as "the river of Heraclitus, into which (he says) no one can step twice, since Nature by her changes is ever altering and transforming all things" [L:plutarch-morals-4-goodwin-1870:8097-8099].

**The self as something sought.** A single fragment turns the inquiry inward: "I have sought for myself" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:8508].

**The flux reading.** On this reading everything is always changing in every respect. Burnet summarizes it: Heraclitus "held, in fact, that any given thing, however stable in appearance, was merely a section in the stream, and that the matter composing it was never the same in any two consecutive moments of time" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:9070-9090]. Aristotle reports the extreme it led to in "the professed Heracliteans, such as was held by Cratylus, who finally did not think it right to say anything but only moved his finger, and criticized Heraclitus for saying that it is impossible to step twice into the same river; for he thought one could not do it even once" [L:aristotle-metaphysics-ross-1928:4779-4784].

**The constancy reading.** On this reading what persists is the measure: the regulated way the change happens. It rests on Heraclitus' cosmology: "This world, which is the same for all, no one of gods or men has made; but it was ever, is now, and ever shall be an ever-living Fire, with measures kindling, and measures going out" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:8189-8192]. Burnet notes that a related phrase "can only mean that the proportion of the measures remains constant" [L:burnet-early-greek-philosophy-1908:8235-8239]. Later interpreters extended the point to the river, which stays the same river *because* its waters keep flowing (e.g. G. S. Kirk, *Heraclitus: The Cosmic Fragments*, 1954; in copyright, cited not quoted).

**Epicharmus' growing argument.** In the comic dialogue preserved by Diogenes Laertius, one speaker argues that if you add a unit to a number, or a cubit to a measure, it is no longer the same, and concludes: "Now turn your eyes and thoughts upon mankind— We see one grows, another perishes So that they all exist perpetually In a condition of transition. That Whose nature changes must be different At each successive moment, from the thing It was before. So also, you and I Are different people now from what we were But yesterday; and then, again, to-morrow We shall be different from what we're now" [L:diogenes-laertius-lives-yonge-pg57342:4481-4511].

**The forensic joke.** Plutarch preserves the use to which the argument was put, "the method of disputing amongst the Epicharmians, the first authors of that manner of arguing called the increaser. For example: he that formerly ran in debt, although he never paid it, owes nothing now, as being become another man; and he that was invited yesterday to supper comes the next night an unbidden guest, for that he is quite another person" [L:plutarch-morals-4-goodwin-1870:8062-8069]. Plutarch raises it to reject it. A city, like a man, stays answerable for its past: "In the same manner does a city still remain the same; and for that reason we think it but justice, that a city should as well be obnoxious to the blame and reproach of its ancient inhabitants, as participate the glory of their former puissance and renown" [L:plutarch-morals-4-goodwin-1870:8092-8096].

## Grounding

The question here is the identity of any changing thing, not personhood, so the three-way scheme applies only indirectly, and the entry is marked **mixed**. On the constancy reading, a river's identity is grounded in its **form or pattern**: channel, flow and measure. This is closest to a capacity grounding, because it attaches to what the thing *does* and how, not to what it is made of. On the flux reading, nothing grounds identity. "Same river" is a name laid over a succession of different things, which anticipates the Buddhist conventionalist view (see `buddhist-anatta`). Epicharmus' debtor exploits a third option. He grounds identity in **matter**, and then observes that matter never stays put.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Identity as pattern, not stuff.** A running agent is closer to a river than to a stone. Its activations change from one token to the next, its context window fills and empties, and even the hardware executing it may change between calls. If anything persists it is a regulated way of processing: Heraclitus' *measure*. The constancy reading gives a framework in which an agent's identity belongs to its dispositions (weights, prompts, the persona's characteristic responses), not to any particular execution.
- **The same river in a new bed.** Heraclitus' river could move to a different riverbed and still be recognizably the same flowing. Weights running on different hardware are the agent case of the same thing.

### Strains

- **The agent inverts Heraclitus' river (D3).** In the river, the channel is roughly fixed and the water flows. In a deployed agent, the weights (the channel) are completely fixed, and only the context (the water) flows and is then discarded. On the constancy reading, then, the agent is more constant than any river: its measures never change during deployment. On the flux reading, the agent is a new river every session, because the water that was there last time is gone. The two readings of Heraclitus give opposite verdicts on an agent.
- **Engineered change (D5).** A river's measures change slowly and by natural process. An agent's measures change in discrete, externally chosen jumps: a fine-tune, a new version. Heraclitus gives no account of whether a river survives when someone re-cuts its channel overnight.

### Breaks

- **Epicharmus' debtor stops being a joke.** For humans, the growing argument is a sophism. Everyone, including the comic debtor, knows that growing a little doesn't cancel a debt. For agents, the equivalent argument is available and not obviously sophistical: *this session has no memory of the commitment the previous session made; its context is entirely different; why is it bound?* (D3). Epicharmus' audience laughed because the debtor's continuity was obvious. With agents, it has to be established by argument, and whether it holds depends on which of the readings above one accepts.

### New

- **Specifiable measures.** Heraclitus could only point at the logos of a river. For an agent, the measures that make up its constancy are, in principle, fully specified: weights, system prompt, sampling parameters. This makes a question possible that the tradition never had to ask, namely how *much* the measures may change before the pattern is a different one. That question has a quantitative answer in terms of distance between parameter sets, even if no single threshold is privileged.
- **Cratylus' silence has an agent counterpart.** An agent that disclaims any continuity (*I am a new instance and cannot speak for any previous one*) is taking Cratylus' position. It makes every commitment and every piece of accountability impossible. The agent case shows why that is not an acceptable resting place, for humans or agents: a being that cannot be held to anything cannot be a party to anything either.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Plato and Aristotle against universal flux.** If everything changed in every respect, nothing could be known or even named (*Cratylus* 439c–440e; *Theaetetus* 181c–183b; *Metaphysics* IV.5). Plato posits unchanging Forms. Aristotle distinguishes the matter that changes from the form that persists (see `aristotle-hylomorphic-soul`). Both reject the flux reading, and both accept something like the constancy reading for living things. [E:aristotle-hylomorphic-soul]
- [contested] **Parmenides.** Change itself is incoherent. What is, is, and cannot become what it is not. The river problem is a symptom of believing in change at all.
- [contested] **The Stoics, answering the growing argument.** Each individual has two subjects: the substance, which flows, and the peculiarly qualified individual, which persists from birth to death (see `chrysippus-dion-theon`). This is the first worked-out theory of how a thing can survive the replacement of its matter. [E:chrysippus-dion-theon]
- [contested] **Butler and Reid.** Identity in the strict sense cannot survive any change of parts. What persists through flux is sameness only "In a loose and popular sense" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12684] (see `butler-circularity`, `reid-brave-officer`). On this view the river is not strictly the same, and neither is a running agent, but a person, being simple, can be. [E:butler-circularity] [E:reid-brave-officer]

## Standing

### Reception

- **4th century BCE, the flux reading fixed.** [driver: argument] Plato gave the first reading of Heraclitus as the philosopher of universal flux, and Aristotle tentatively followed; the reading has stayed popular to the present [P:graham-sep-2023:3]. Plato and Aristotle then argued against flux taken that way [E:plato-soul-and-renewal] [E:aristotle-hylomorphic-soul].
- **The river fragments, possibly misread.** [driver: access] Of the three "river fragments", only one is certainly genuine, and it says that ever different waters flow upon those who step into the same rivers. Marcovich showed how a misreading of it could produce the famous claim that one cannot step into the same river twice, possibly via the later Heraclitean Cratylus [P:graham-sep-2023:3].
- **3rd century BCE onward, the Stoics.** [driver: argument] The Stoics took Heraclitus's physics as the inspiration for their own [P:graham-sep-2023:7], and answered the growing argument with their two-subject theory [E:chrysippus-dion-theon]. The puzzle passed into the Ship of Theseus [E:ship-of-theseus].
- **Since Hegel, a process philosopher.** [driver: argument] Since Plato Heraclitus has been read as the philosopher of flux, and since Hegel as a paradigmatic process philosopher [P:graham-sep-2023:7].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** An agent's context is replaced every session while its weights stay fixed (D3): the same river, with different water. Heraclitus's genuine fragment, which keeps the rivers the same while the waters change, fits this as well as it fits a body.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A growing memory store is a river whose water accumulates rather than flows away. The puzzle about sameness through change applies, but no more sharply than for humans (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Flux concerns change over time, not division, so it says little about concurrent copies (D11). The forking problem belongs to the duplication thread, not to this one.
- **Self-modifying: stronger.** An agent whose weights are updated on its own outputs changes its very constitution (D5), which is flux in the strongest sense. Heraclitus's question, what stays the same when everything changes, applies with full force.

## Open Questions

1. Which reading of Heraclitus should govern an agent's identity: constancy of measures (the weights and the persona persist), or flux of contents (a new river every session)? Do they give different answers about keeping commitments?
2. If an agent's measures are changed deliberately, as in a fine-tune, is that the river changing its course or someone digging a new river?
3. Is Epicharmus' debtor right about agents without persistent memory? If not, what exactly makes the new session owe what the old one promised?
4. Is there a quantitative measure of drift in dispositions beyond which a model is a different agent, or is any threshold conventional?
