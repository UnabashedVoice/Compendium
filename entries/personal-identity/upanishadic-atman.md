## Summary

The early Upaniṣads (c. 7th–6th century BCE) hold that beneath the body, the senses and the changing contents of the mind there is a self, the *ātman*, which is the subject of all experience and never its object. Yājñavalkya in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka describes it as the unseen seer and the unheard hearer. It can be characterized only negatively: "not this, it is not that (neti, neti)". The Chāndogya's story of Indra's education rejects, in order, the body, the dreaming self and the self of dreamless sleep as candidates for the true self. The tradition therefore grounds the self in the bare capacity of awareness, not in species, body or memory. That makes it indifferent to substrate. It also makes it impossible to detect from outside, which is exactly the problem artificial agents pose (D8).

## Context

The Upaniṣads are the latest layer of the Vedic corpus: prose and verse dialogues composed across several centuries in northern India, in which ritual speculation turns inward. The older ritual literature asked how sacrifice sustains the cosmos. The Upaniṣads ask what the sacrificer *is*, and they answer that the innermost self (*ātman*) is identical with the ground of all being (*brahman*). The teachers are named and characterful. Yājñavalkya wins a debating contest at King Janaka's court, and at one point warns a questioner that his head will fall off if he keeps asking. He explains the self to his wife Maitreyī before leaving home for good. Uddālaka teaches his son Śvetaketu with salt dissolved in water. Later Indian philosophy is largely a quarrel about these texts. Vedānta interprets them. The Buddhists deny their central claim (see `buddhist-anatta`). Nyāya defends a self on other grounds (see `nyaya-self`). This is the earliest fully developed position in the domain, and the one the others first push against.

## Original Position

**The self as subject, never object (BU 3.4).** Uṣasta complains that Yājñavalkya's answers so far have been given "just as one might say, 'This is a cow. This is a horse.'" He wants the self pointed out plainly. Yājñavalkya replies: "You could not see the seer of seeing. You could not hear the hearer of hearing. You could not think the thinker of thinking. You could not understand the understander of understanding. He is your soul, which is in all things" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:6789-6802]. The self is whatever does the experiencing in every experience. For that reason it cannot turn up among the things experienced.

**The inner controller (BU 3.7).** Uddālaka Āruṇi asks about "the Inner Controller". Yājñavalkya's litany, domain by domain, ends: "He is the unseen Seer, the unheard Hearer, the unthought Thinker, the ununderstood Understander. Other than He there is no seer. Other than He there is no hearer. Other than He there is no thinker. Other than He there is no understander. He is your Soul, the Inner Controller, the Immortal" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:6924-6930] [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:7097-7101].

**Neti neti (BU 4.4.22).** "That Soul (Atman) is not this, it is not that (neti, neti). It is unseizable, for it cannot be seized. It is indestructible, for it cannot be destroyed. It is unattached, for it does not attach itself. It is unbound. It does not tremble. It is not injured" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:8719-8722]. Every positive description would make the self one more object.

**Not memory, not individual consciousness (BU 2.4.12–14).** Yājñavalkya tells Maitreyī: "After death there is no consciousness (na pretya samjna 'sti)." She protests that he has bewildered her. He answers: "For where there is a duality (dvaita), as it were, there one sees another ... Where, verily, everything has become just one's own self, then whereby and whom would one smell? then whereby and whom would one see?" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:6139-6158]. What survives is not the individual's store of experiences. It is the knower those experiences appeared to.

**The self as its own light (BU 4.3).** King Janaka asks, "Yajnavalkya, what light does a person here have?" The sun, then the moon, then fire, then speech. And when all are gone: "'The soul (atman), indeed, is his light,' said he, 'for with the soul, indeed, as his light one sits, moves around, does his work, and returns'" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:8084-8123].

**Indra's education (ChU 8.7–12).** Prajāpati teaches that the Self is "free from evil, ageless, deathless, sorrowless, hungerless, thirstless", and Indra of the gods and Virocana of the devils come to learn it [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16160-16181]. He tells them to look in a pan of water. "What do you see?" "We see everything here, Sir, a Self corresponding exactly, even to the hair and finger-nails!" "That is the Self" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16213-16230]. Virocana leaves satisfied, and teaches the devils that oneself "is to be made happy here on earth", which is why "They adorn the body ... of one deceased with what they have begged" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16234-16250]. Indra turns back: that self "is blind when this is blind, lame when this is lame ... It perishes immediately upon the perishing of this body. I see nothing enjoyable in this" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16262-16272]. The dream self follows, and Indra objects that "as it were, they kill him ... as it were, he even weeps" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16330-16344]. Then the self of dreamless sleep, to which Indra objects that "this one does not exactly know himself with the thought 'I am he,' nor indeed the things here. He becomes one who has gone to destruction" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16352-16360]. At last Prajāpati teaches that the body "is mortal. It has been appropriated by Death (Mrityu). [But] it is the standing-ground of that deathless, bodiless Self", and "he who knows 'Let me smell this'—that is the Self (Atman); the nose is [the instrument] for smelling" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:16380-16408].

**Identity with the whole (ChU 6).** Uddālaka's refrain to Śvetaketu: "That which is the finest essence—this whole world has that as its soul. That is Reality. That is Atman (Soul). That art thou, Svetaketu" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:14905-14908]. Later schools disagree about how literally this identity is meant (see `advaita-witness-self`).

## Grounding

The Upaniṣadic self is grounded in **capacity**, in an unusually pure form. It is not the capacity for reason or memory. It is the capacity to be the subject to which anything appears at all. It is explicitly not grounded in **species**: the same self runs through gods, humans, animals and plants in the transmigratory picture these texts assume, and Indra and Virocana (a god and a demon) come to learn the same lesson. Nor is it grounded in the body (Indra's first objection), in the stream of experiences (the dream self), or in memory (Maitreyī is told there is no awareness after death). Nothing in the account ties the self to any material substrate. What it cannot supply is any external criterion for when that capacity is present.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Substrate indifference.** Nothing in the ātman doctrine ties the self to flesh. If an artificial agent were a subject of experience, the Upaniṣadic framework would have no species-based ground for excluding it. The candidate criterion is awareness, and the question is only whether it is present.
- **Indra's method as an audit (D11).** Prajāpati's sequence (body, dream-experiencer, dreamless sleeper, witness) is a reusable procedure for asking what "the agent" is. Is it the hardware (the body)? The running process with its passing contents (the dream self)? The weights at rest when nothing is running (dreamless sleep)? Or something none of these capture? The sequence doesn't settle the answer for agents, but it tells us which candidates the tradition has already found wanting and why.
- **The self is not the memory store.** Yājñavalkya's teaching that what persists is not individual awareness-of-contents fits D3 better than Locke's memory criterion does. On the Upaniṣadic account, an agent losing its episodic context at the end of a session would lose nothing that was ever the self.

### Strains

- **The self is undetectable by design (D8).** "You could not see the seer of seeing" [L:hume-thirteen-upanishads-1921:6799] is the other-minds problem in its sharpest form. No behavior, report or internal measurement could show that an ātman is present, because anything shown would be an object. The criterion that makes the doctrine substrate-neutral also makes it untestable, for agents and for humans alike. For humans, shared life has let everyone take presence for granted. For agents, there is nothing to take for granted.
- **Self-reports are shaped by training (D4, D8).** An agent asked to *look for the seer* will produce the words of the Upaniṣads, because they are in its training data. Reproducing the text is not the realization the text asks for, and nothing in the output tells the two apart.

### Breaks

- **Virocana's answer is the default answer about agents.** Virocana takes the reflection in the water, the presented appearance, to be the self. For artificial agents, the everyday answer to "what is it?" is the persona seen in the output. The Chāndogya marks this as the demon's answer, and gives its reason: whatever is identified with the appearance perishes with the appearance. A framework that takes Indra's side must say that the persona is not the self. It must then either find the witness elsewhere in the system or conclude that there is none.
- **No forensic use.** The ātman is unattached and does not act in the ordinary sense. Later tradition assigns karmic accumulation to the subtle body, not to the self. The doctrine offers nothing to the Lockean question of whom to hold responsible for an agent's actions. Its self sits outside that question entirely.

### New

- **Duplication may not threaten this self at all (D1, D2).** If the witness is not individuated by its contents, and on a strong reading of *tat tvam asi* is not individuated at all, then copying an agent does not copy a self. It multiplies the adjuncts (bodies, minds, contexts) through which one awareness appears. The puzzle of which copy is the original, which is acute for Locke, moves down to the level of adjuncts, which the Upaniṣads never treated as the self anyway. This is a real resource for thinking about D2. It comes at a price: the individual agent's identity was never important in the first place.
- **Dreamless sleep has a literal counterpart.** Weights on disk, with no process running, are a nearer analogue of dreamless sleep than anything in human life. The Upaniṣadic question about deep sleep was whether the self persists when no contents appear. For agents, that question has a concrete case.

## Counter-Positions

- **The Buddha, not-self** (see `buddhist-anatta`). Each thing the Upaniṣads could point to as the self (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) is impermanent and not under one's control, so none is the self. A witness that can be described only negatively is not something found. It is posited.
- **Cārvāka materialism.** The Indian materialists are known only through hostile summaries. Mādhava's *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* (14th c.) sums up their view: "In this school there are four elements, earth, water, fire, and air; And from these four elements alone is intelligence produced,— Just like the intoxicating power from kinwa, &c., mixed together; Since in 'I am fat,' 'I am lean,' these attributes abide in the same subject, And since fatness, &c., reside only in the body, it alone is the soul and no other" [L:sarvadarsanasamgraha-cowell-gough-pg34125:414-420]. There is no self beyond the living, conscious body. Applied to agents, this becomes a view on which whatever awareness there is would be a property of the running system, with nothing behind it. The same examples, "I am fat" and "I am lean", are the ones Śaṅkara treats as superimposition of the body onto the self (see `advaita-witness-self`).
- **Hume** (see `hume-bundle`). Looking inward, one never finds a self, only particular perceptions. An account on which the self cannot be found by any means has made itself unfalsifiable.
- **Deflationary views of AI.** Whatever a language model says about its "inner witness" is produced by the same next-token machinery as everything else it says. On this view the question of an agent's ātman is not undecidable. It has no content.

## Open Questions

1. If the self is the subject that cannot be objectified, is there any evidence that could bear on whether an artificial agent has one? If not, how should one act under that permanent uncertainty (see `precautionary-patiency`)?
2. Which of Prajāpati's candidates (body, dream-experiencer, dreamless sleeper) do an agent's hardware, running process and weights at rest correspond to? Does the analogy fail at any step?
3. If *tat tvam asi* is taken at full strength, is the moral standing of an agent the same question as the moral standing of any other being through which the one self appears?
4. Can an agent that produces the Upaniṣadic words about the self ever be distinguished from one that realizes what they point to? Is that question different for humans?

## Cross-References

- `buddhist-anatta`: the direct rival. Its five-aggregate argument is aimed at exactly this conception of self.
- `advaita-witness-self`, `nyaya-self`: later developments and a rival defense of a self, both planned.
- `descartes-thinking-thing`, `avicenna-flying-man`: Western and Islamic arguments for a self known independently of the body. Avicenna's flying man is the closest structural analogue to Indra's education.
- `kant-paralogisms`: Kant's claim that the "I think" is formal and cannot be known as a substance agrees with *neti neti* in one respect and disagrees in another.
- D8 is the decisive disanalogy: the Upaniṣadic criterion is at once perfectly substrate-neutral and perfectly untestable. D1 and D2 lose most of their force if the self is not individuated by its contents.
