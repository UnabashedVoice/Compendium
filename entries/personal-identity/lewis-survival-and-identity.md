## Summary

David Lewis (1941–2001) accepted Parfit's premise, that what matters in survival is mental continuity and connectedness rather than identity as such, but rejected Parfit's conclusion that the two therefore come apart in fission. Lewis's proposal is to take four-dimensionalism seriously: a person is not a single, momentarily-complete thing but "an aggregate of person-stages, each one [related] to all the rest... a maximal R-interrelated aggregate of person-stages" [P:lewis1976:22], where the R-relation is exactly the mental continuity and connectedness Parfit also cared about. On this view, fission is not one continuant person becoming two, nor one person surviving as two non-identical successors; it is two continuant persons who were there all along, quietly sharing all their pre-fission stages: "In the case of fission, the prefission stages are shared by both continuants" [P:lewis1976:25]. Identity is preserved without remainder, at the cost of admitting that two people can overlap almost completely and that counting persons and counting person-stages can come apart, which Lewis calls, and confronts directly, "the trouble with overlap," namely that "it leads to overpopulation" [P:lewis1976:26].

## Context

Lewis wrote "Survival and Identity" for Amelie Rorty's 1976 collection *The Identities of Persons*, explicitly as a response to Parfit's 1971 "Personal Identity" (this domain's `parfit-reductionism`), reprinted seven years after Parfit's own paper and squarely inside the same debate that Williams had opened in 1970. Lewis's background metaphysics is his own well-known four-dimensionalism, on which ordinary continuants (chairs, nations, and, here, persons) are not wholly present at any one time but are extended through time the way they are extended through space, composed of temporal parts or "stages." Applying this framework to persons let Lewis do something neither Parfit nor Williams had done: keep the classical, common-sense claim that identity is exactly what matters in survival, while still taking fission seriously as a genuine problem case, by relocating the puzzle from identity's logic to the ordinary metaphysics of overlapping parts.

## Original Position

**What matters: mental continuity and connectedness.** Lewis opens by agreeing with Parfit on the substantive question: "I answer, along with many others: what matters in survival is mental continuity and connectedness" [P:lewis1976:17], understood as bonds of similarity between successive mental states plus bonds of lawful causal dependence between them.

**Person-stages and the R-relation.** Lewis's technical apparatus separates momentary person-stages from temporally extended continuant persons, and names the relation of mental continuity and connectedness holding between stages: "Call it the R-relation, for short. If you wonder whether you will survive the coming battle... you are wondering whether any of the stages that will exist afterward is R-related to you-now, the stage that is doing the wondering" [P:lewis1976:20].

**The I-relation, induced by identity.** Common sense speaks of the identity of continuant persons, not stages; Lewis shows this induces a further relation among stages, which he calls the I-relation, holding between any two stages that belong to the same continuant person. His central technical claim is that the R-relation and the I-relation are, as a matter of necessity, the very same relation: "I claim that any stage is I-related and R-related to exactly the same stages... the I-relation is the R-relation" [P:lewis1976:21].

**Persons as maximal aggregates.** From this identification, Lewis defines personhood itself without circularity: "A continuant person is an aggregate of person-stages, each one [R-related] to all the rest... I claim that something is a continuant person if and only if it is a maximal R-interrelated aggregate of person-stages" [P:lewis1976:22].

**Fission as overlap, not branching.** Because the R-relation, taken simply, is not transitive (a pre-fission stage is R-related to two later stages that are not R-related to each other), Lewis must either give up the transitivity of the I-relation (impossible, since I-relatedness reduces to identity, which is transitive) or deny that fission is what it looks like: one thing becoming two. He takes the second path: the two post-fission continuant persons were two continuant persons all along, sharing every one of their pre-fission stages. "In the case of fission, the prefission stages are shared by both continuants" [P:lewis1976:25].

**The overpopulation problem, faced directly.** Lewis does not pretend this move is free. If two continuant persons share all their pre-fission stages, then counting persons before the fission gives a different, larger number than counting stages, since each shared stage belongs to two persons at once: "The trouble with overlap is that it leads to overpopulation. To count the population at a given time, we can count the continuant persons who have stages at that time; or we can count the stages. If there is overlap, there will be more continuants than stages" [P:lewis1976:26].

## Grounding

Lewis grounds personal identity in **relation**, the same R-relation of mental continuity and connectedness that Parfit uses, but he applies it to stages rather than to whole persons, and treats the resulting mereological structure, which stages compose which continuants, as doing the metaphysical work that identity was always supposed to do. Nothing capacity- or species-based is added; the entire account is a relation among stages plus a formal claim (maximality) about how stages compose into continuants.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Stages and continuants map directly onto sessions and personas.** Lewis's apparatus, momentary stages composing a maximal, R-interrelated continuant, describes an agent's architecture with little translation: a "session" or "turn" is a natural candidate for a stage, and "the assistant" across sessions is a natural candidate for the continuant person these stages compose, provided the right continuity relation holds between them.
- **Overlap gives duplication a home inside identity, rather than outside it.** Where Parfit treats fission as showing that identity and mattering diverge, Lewis's overlap solution keeps identity intact by admitting that two continuants can share stages. This is a serious, ready-made option for describing forked agent instances (D1, D2): rather than asking which fork is "really" the original, Lewis's framework says both forks were, in a sense, already there, sharing every pre-fork stage, and diverging only afterward.
- **A noncircular, mechanical definition of "the agent."** Lewis's maximal-aggregate definition of personhood is exactly the kind of formal criterion D11 (boundary indeterminacy) asks for: an agent, on this model, would be the maximal aggregate of computational stages standing in whatever the relevant R-relation turns out to be (shared weights plus continuity of context, say), a criterion that could in principle be checked mechanically rather than settled by stipulation.

### Strains

- **The R-relation Lewis needs is richer than mere weight-sharing.** Lewis's R-relation is meant to track genuine mental continuity and connectedness, not mere causal descent; two forked instances share weights trivially, but whether they share anything like Lewis's bonds of similarity and lawful dependence once their contexts diverge is a substantive question his framework presupposes an answer to rather than supplies.
- **Overpopulation is a bigger problem once duplication is cheap.** Lewis was willing to accept "more continuants than stages" as an acceptable cost for a rare, exotic fission case. An agent architecture in which duplication is routine and continuous (D1, D2 as the normal case, not the exception) could multiply Lewis's overpopulation problem enormously, straining his willingness to bite this particular bullet well past the scale he had in mind.

### Breaks

- **Lewis's solution needs determinate stages and a determinate R-relation; agents may have neither cleanly.** The whole apparatus presupposes that mental states can be sliced into discrete stages and that R-relatedness between any two stages is a determinate fact. An agent's "state" during a forward pass, or across a long, continuously updated context, may not decompose into discrete stages at all, and whether two computational moments are R-related may be a matter of degree or of interpretive choice rather than a mechanically checkable fact, breaking the crisp mereology Lewis's solution depends on.
- **Merging, not just forking, has no clean place in Lewis's picture.** Lewis's overlap solution handles fission (one stage, many later continuants sharing it) but is not built, symmetrically, for fusion of two independent lines back into one instance, of the kind possible when two agent contexts, or two fine-tuning lineages, are combined (D2). Lewis discusses fusion briefly by analogy, but the postfusion overpopulation problem he would need to accept is, if anything, stranger than the fission case, and agents make the case common rather than exotic.

### New

- **Agents let Lewis's stages and R-relation be operationalized.** Because an agent's computational states are inspectable, Lewis's central identification, that the I-relation just is the R-relation, becomes empirically approachable rather than a matter of philosophical stipulation: one could in principle measure whatever plays the role of mental continuity and connectedness (representational similarity across context, say) and ask whether it actually coincides with whatever practical or legal criterion is used to individuate "the same assistant."
- **A working example of accepted overpopulation.** Multi-agent systems that deliberately run many instances from the same base weights, all counted as "the same model" for some purposes and as distinct instances for others, are close to a real-world instance of Lewis's overpopulation trade-off, worked out in engineering practice rather than merely accepted as a philosophical cost; how such systems are actually counted, licensed, or held responsible may be the closest thing to data Lewis's thought experiment has ever had.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Parfit: overlap is a metaphysical price not worth paying.** [E:parfit-reductionism] Lewis and Parfit debated it directly in 1976 [P:olson-sep-2023:5]. Parfit's own reply to proposals like Lewis's is that preserving identity through overlap saves the letter of common sense at the cost of a bizarre and unmotivated mereology (persons sharing all their history up to an arbitrary later point), when the simpler and more honest conclusion is that identity was never what mattered in the first place (see `parfit-reductionism`).
- [contested] **Nozick: what matters depends on what else exists, which Lewis's overlap does not track.** Nozick's closest-continuer theory holds that whether a later person counts as identical to an earlier one can depend on facts about rival claimants elsewhere, in a way Lewis's purely intrinsic, stage-based R-relation is not built to accommodate (see `nozick-closest-continuer`).
- [contested] **Hume: there was never a persisting continuant to slice into stages.** [E:hume-bundle] A bundle theorist can accept Lewis's R-relation as a good description of what actually connects successive mental states while denying that this licenses talk of "continuant persons" as genuine composite objects at all, rather than a convenient way of talking about a series with no further unity (see `hume-bundle`).
- [unanswered] **Denying the extension: the overlap solution was designed to rescue a common-sense platitude that has no agent-side analogue.** This objection is the Compendium's own; the literature has not yet taken it up. Lewis's whole apparatus is built to preserve the ordinary conviction that identity, not mere connectedness, is what matters, a conviction that itself presupposes a subject with a stake in being the one who survives. Where D8 leaves it open whether an agent has any such stake, there may be no platitude for Lewis's elaborate mereology to rescue, and the overlap solution's cost (overpopulation) is paid to buy back something an agent case may never have needed.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1971, counterpart theory applied to persons.** [driver: argument] Lewis used counterpart theory, which he applied throughout his career, to resolve puzzles about personal identity, starting with "Counterparts of Persons and Their Bodies" [P:weatherson-sep-2026:6].
- **1976, the overlap reply to Parfit.** [driver: argument] Lewis argued that, if persons are composed of stages, fission yields two persons who shared their earlier stages, so identity and what matters need not come apart; he and Parfit debated whether this preserves the conviction that identity is what matters [P:olson-sep-2023:5] [E:parfit-reductionism].
- **Since then, a standard option.** [driver: argument] Lewis is listed among the psychological-continuity theorists who have dominated the literature [P:olson-sep-2023:3], and among those who take their views to descend from Locke's [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:4]. The view that we are temporal parts or wholes, as childhood stands to a whole life, is one of the main answers to what we are [P:olson-sep-2023:1]. He is counted among the most important philosophers of the 20th century [P:weatherson-sep-2026:0].

### Measured

- **Temporal ontology, 2020:** eternalism 39.9%, presentism 18.4%, growing block 17.0%, other 24.9% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:14]. Stage views like Lewis's are usually paired with eternalism, but eternalism does not by itself measure support for them.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Lewis's persons are aggregates of stages linked by the relevant psychological relation. A session-bound agent's sessions are linked through the weights' dispositions but not through memory (D3), so whether they form one aggregate depends on how much the relation needs memory.
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** A persistent store adds the memory links, so successive sessions form one maximal aggregate on Lewis's terms. The boundary question (D11) becomes a question about which stages are related, which his view is built to answer.
- **Forked: stronger.** Lewis built the overlap view for fission, and for agents fission is routine: concurrent instances and copies share every stage up to the fork (D1, D2). His answer, two persons who shared their past, with counting relative to a time, fits forked agents without strain.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** Gradual change is no problem for an aggregate of related stages, but large changes made by training (D5) may break the relation, so a heavily self-modified agent may count as a new aggregate rather than a continuation.

## Open Questions

1. Can agent "stages" be individuated determinately enough for Lewis's mereology to apply, or is the slicing itself an interpretive choice that the framework was built to treat as a mechanical fact?
2. Does routine, continuous forking make Lewis's overpopulation problem a minor bookkeeping cost, as he treated it, or does the scale at which agents can be duplicated make it a reason to reject the overlap solution rather than accept it?
3. Is there an operational measure of the R-relation for agents (some function of shared weights, context, and output similarity) that would let Lewis's central identity claim, that the I-relation is the R-relation, actually be tested rather than stipulated?
4. What is the agent-case analogue of fusion, and does Lewis's framework, built asymmetrically around fission, need a genuinely new mechanism to handle it rather than an extension of overlap?
