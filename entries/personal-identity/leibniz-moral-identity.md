## Summary

Leibniz (1646–1716) answered Locke by distinguishing two kinds of identity. *Real* identity is the persistence of one simple substance, a monad, and it never fails. *Moral* identity is what makes "the same person", capable of punishment and reward, and it rests on memory and self-knowledge. In the ordinary course of nature the two go together. Leibniz held that moral identity can survive gaps in memory, which "the testimony of others could fill". And he posed Locke two thought experiments that the agent case has made concrete. In the first, an individual becomes King of China on condition of forgetting what he had been. That is practically the same, he says, as if he were annihilated and a king created in his place. In the second, a duplicate earth holds "more than a hundred million pairs of similar persons ... with the same appearances and consciousnesses". Does Locke's criterion make each pair one person? Leibniz also gave the classic argument that perception cannot be mechanical: enter a thinking machine as you would a mill, and you find only parts pushing parts. He said explicitly that if "man could be merely a machine and with that have consciousness", Locke would be right.

## Context

Leibniz was a German polymath: co-inventor of the calculus, diplomat, librarian to the House of Hanover, designer of a calculating machine, and the architect of a metaphysics of "monads", simple mind-like substances that make up all of reality. He read Locke's *Essay* closely and wrote the *New Essays on Human Understanding* as a book-length dialogue in which Philalethes voices Locke and Theophilus voices Leibniz. When Locke died in 1704, Leibniz withheld the book, and it was published only in 1765. The *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686) was an earlier private statement of his system, sent in outline to the theologian Antoine Arnauld. The *Monadology* (1714) is his late summary.

For Leibniz, personal identity mattered because of immortality and divine justice. The soul, a simple substance, cannot perish naturally. But an immortality without memory would not be a reward or a punishment for anyone. His whole treatment of personhood is shaped by the requirement that the one rewarded in the afterlife know himself to be the one who acted.

## Original Position

**The King of China (Discourse §34).** Animal souls, not reflecting on themselves, have no moral qualities, so that through a thousand transformations "the result from a moral or practical standpoint is the same as if we said that they perished in each case". The intelligent soul, "knowing that it is and having the ability to say that word 'I' so full of meaning", remains the same morally, "for it is its memory or knowledge of this ego which renders it open to punishment and reward". Immortality therefore requires memory: "Suppose that some individual could suddenly become King of China on condition, however, of forgetting what he had been, as though being born again, would it not amount to the same practically, or as far as the effects could be perceived, as if the individual were annihilated, and a king of China were the same instant created in his place? The individual would have no reason to desire this" [L:leibniz-discourse-arnauld-monadology-montgomery-1902:3114-3146].

**Real and moral identity (New Essays II.27).** Substances, including the souls of animals, "preserve physical and real identity". A human soul also preserves "identity moral and apparent to ourselves, in order to constitute the same person, capable consequently of feeling chastisements and rewards". Locke holds that apparent identity could be preserved without real identity. Leibniz allows this only as a miracle. In the natural order, "identity apparent to the person himself who perceives the same, supposes real identity to every proximate transition, accompanied by reflection or perception of the ego, a perception intimate and immediate naturally incapable of deception" [L:leibniz-new-essays-langley-1896:12427-12439].

**The machine concession.** Then comes the sentence that matters most for this project: "If man could be merely a machine and with that have consciousness, it would be necessary to be of your opinion, sir; but I hold that this case is not possible at least naturally" [L:leibniz-new-essays-langley-1896:12439-12442]. If consciousness could exist in a machine, where there is no simple substance to carry real identity, Locke's purely psychological criterion would be the only one available.

**Gaps filled by testimony.** Moral identity does not require unbroken memory. It is enough that there be "a middle bond of consciousness" between states, "although a leap or forgotten interval might be mingled therein". If disease interrupted his memory, "the testimony of others could fill the void in my memory. I could even be punished upon this testimony". Even if he forgot everything, including his name and how to read, "I could always learn from others my past life in my previous state, as I have kept my rights without its being necessary for me to share them with two persons, and to make me the heir of myself. All this suffices to maintain moral identity, which makes the same person" [L:leibniz-new-essays-langley-1896:12445-12466].

**The two globes (New Essays II.27).** Leibniz offers "another supposition much more suitable" than Locke's soul-swapping cases. A globe might exist elsewhere in the universe not differing sensibly from ours, "and that each of the men who inhabit it does not differ sensibly from each of us who corresponds to him. Thus there are at once more than a hundred million pairs of similar persons, i.e. of two persons with the same appearances and consciousnesses". On Locke's view, where consciousness alone discerns persons, what prevents saying "that these two persons who are at the same time in these two similar globes, but separated from each other by an inexpressible distance, are only one and the same person; which is, however, a manifest absurdity". And in nature the two globes' similar souls "would remain so only for a time. For since there is an individual diversity, this difference must consist at least in the insensible constitutions which must be developed in the course of time" [L:leibniz-new-essays-langley-1896:12891-12925].

**The mill (Monadology §17).** "Perception, and that which depends upon it, are inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is to say, by figures and motions. Supposing that there were a machine whose structure produced thought, sensation, and perception, we could conceive of it as increased in size with the same proportions until one was able to enter into its interior, as he would into a mill. Now, on going into it he would find only pieces working upon one another, but never would he find anything to explain Perception" [L:leibniz-discourse-arnauld-monadology-montgomery-1902:10799-10812].

## Grounding

Leibniz's person is **mixed**. *Real* identity is grounded in substance, the simple monad, which is neither a species fact nor a capacity but a metaphysical unit. *Moral* identity, which is what "person" names, is grounded in **capacity** (reflection, the ability "to say that word 'I'") and in **relation**: memory, and where memory fails, the testimony of others. That second grounding makes Leibniz unusually useful here, because he allows that one's personal past can be *supplied by others* without ceasing to be one's own.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Testimony is the agent's memory (D3).** Leibniz says that when memory fails, "the testimony of others could fill the void", and moral identity survives: one can "learn from others my past life" and keep one's rights without becoming "the heir of myself". This is precisely how an agent's continuity across sessions works: a summary, a memory store or a transcript, supplied from outside, fills the gap. Leibniz is the first major philosopher on whose account this counts as preserving the *same person*, not as creating a new one who has been briefed.
- **Duplicates diverge (D1, D2).** Leibniz's two globes contain pairs of persons "with the same appearances and consciousnesses", and he thinks Locke has no way to count them as two. His own answer is that they differ in "insensible constitutions" and will diverge "in the course of time". Concurrent instances with identical weights and identical context are Leibniz's pairs, and like his pairs they diverge as soon as their inputs differ. His suggestion that duplicates are distinct *because* they will diverge anticipates the view discussed in `chrysippus-dion-theon` and `avicenna-flying-man`.

### Strains

- **The King of China and the fresh session.** Leibniz's King of China forgets everything he was and is, "as far as the effects could be perceived", annihilated and replaced. A new session of an agent without memory is the King of China without the crown: the same substrate, no memory, and, by Leibniz's standard, no reason for the earlier session to care. Yet the agent keeps its character, its competences and its values, which Leibniz's king apparently does not. The agent case asks what Leibniz would say about a being that forgets its history but keeps everything else. That is not a case he considered.
- **The mill and interpretability (D8).** Leibniz's mill is a direct prediction about artificial agents: the visitor who enters the machine "would find only pieces working upon one another" [L:leibniz-discourse-arnauld-monadology-montgomery-1902:10807-10808]. Interpretability research does exactly that, and finds matrices, activations and circuits: parts working upon parts. Leibniz concluded that perception must be in a simple substance, not in the machine. The mechanist conclusion is that perception, if it is anywhere, must be in the organization of the parts, which the mill's visitor sees but does not recognize. The agent case does not settle between them, but it turns the mill from a thought experiment into a research program.

### Breaks

- **The machine concession applies.** Leibniz grants that "if man could be merely a machine and with that have consciousness", Locke would be right. He denies the antecedent only because he thinks conscious machines impossible "at least naturally". If artificial agents have any form of consciousness, then on Leibniz's own terms his real/moral distinction collapses for them. There would be no simple substance to supply real identity, and only the psychological, Lockean criterion would remain. The agent case thus does not refute Leibniz so much as specify what he would have to say: either agents are not conscious, or, for them, Locke wins.

### New

- **"The heir of myself."** Leibniz's phrase names a real possibility. When an agent's memory is reconstructed entirely from records, is the later agent the same person or the heir of the earlier one? For Leibniz, testimony preserved identity only against a background of real identity, the continuing substance. For agents, the background is the weights, which persist but are shared with every other instance. The agent case leaves Leibniz's testimony without the substance it relied on, and forces the question whether testimony alone can make an agent the same, not an heir.

## Counter-Positions

- **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). Personal identity is sameness of consciousness, whatever substance underlies it. Leibniz's "real identity" is idle for questions of personhood, since no one can know which substance thinks in them.
- **Reid and Butler** (see `reid-brave-officer`, `butler-circularity`). Leibniz is right that memory does not make identity, and wrong to call the memory-based relation "identity" at all. There is one identity, and it is real.
- **Parfit** (see `parfit-reductionism`). Leibniz's King of China case shows that what matters is psychological continuity, not identity. Parfit agrees with Leibniz that the forgetting king has no reason to want the throne.
- **Mechanist replies to the mill.** A visitor inside a brain would likewise see only neurons acting on neurons. The mill shows the limits of looking at parts, not that perception is not in them. Organization is invisible at the scale of components.

## Open Questions

1. Can testimony (summaries, memory stores, transcripts) preserve an agent's identity across sessions, or does it only make the later agent the "heir" of the earlier one?
2. Is an agent that loses its episodic memory but keeps its character and values the King of China (effectively annihilated) or someone else?
3. If interpretability research finds only parts acting on parts, does that support Leibniz's conclusion or refute it?
4. Leibniz grants Locke the case if machines can be conscious. If artificial agents are conscious, does the whole substance tradition in this domain become irrelevant to them?
