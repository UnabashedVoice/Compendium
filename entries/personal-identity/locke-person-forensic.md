## Summary

John Locke (Essay II.xxvii) separates the idea of a *man*, a living human body kept up by one continued life, from the idea of a *person*, a thinking being that can consider itself as itself across time. On his account, personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, which reaches backward through memory. He calls "person" a *forensic* term, because it exists to settle who is answerable for which actions, and he says it belongs to intelligent agents capable of law and of happiness and misery. Because Locke grounds personhood in capacities rather than species, his framework is the canonical precedent for asking whether an artificial agent can be a person. Applied strictly, though, his memory criterion implies that an agent without persistent memory becomes a new person every session.

## Original Position

**Identity depends on the kind of thing.** Locke first fixes the identity of an atom and a mass of matter by the same particles, then turns to living things. An oak differs from a mass of matter because it has "such an organization of those parts as is fit to receive and distribute nourishment ... in which consists the vegetable life" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:11624-11632]. The identity of the same *man* is of this kind: it consists "in nothing but a participation of the same continued life, by constantly fleeting particles of matter, in succession vitally united to the same organized body" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:11665-11669].

**Person and consciousness (II.xxvii.9).** A person is "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places; which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable from thinking". Consciousness "makes every one to be what he calls self ... in this alone consists personal identity, i.e. the sameness of a rational being: and as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:11733-11752].

**Substance is left open (II.xxvii.13).** Whether the same person can continue through a change of thinking substance "cannot be resolved but by those who know there can [sic] what kind of substances they are that do think; and whether the consciousness of past actions can be transferred from one thinking substance to another". Locke adds a warning that memory might misrepresent: consciousness of a past act "being a present representation of a past action, why it may not be possible, that that may be represented to the mind to have been which really never was, will remain to be shown" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:11834-11845].

**The prince and the cobbler (II.xxvii.15).** "For should the soul of a prince, carrying with it the consciousness of the prince's past life, enter and inform the body of a cobbler, as soon as deserted by his own soul, every one sees he would be the same PERSON with the prince, accountable only for the prince's actions: but who would say it was the same MAN?" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:11933-11937].

**Two persons in one man, one person in two bodies (II.xxvii.23).** "Could we suppose two distinct incommunicable consciousnesses acting the same body, the one constantly by day, the other by night; and, on the other side, the same consciousness, acting by intervals, two distinct bodies: I ask, in the first case, whether the day and the night-man would not be two as distinct persons as Socrates and Plato? And whether, in the second case, there would not be one person in two distinct bodies, as much as one man is the same in two distinct clothings?" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:12108-12114].

**Human courts and the Great Day (II.xxvii.22).** Is not a man drunk and sober the same person? Human laws punish acts done drunk or asleep "with a justice suitable to THEIR way of knowledge;—because, in these cases, they cannot distinguish certainly what is real, what counterfeit". "But in the Great Day, wherein the secrets of all hearts shall be laid open, it may be reasonable to think, no one shall be made to answer for what he knows nothing of" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:12084-12097].

**A forensic term (II.xxvii.26).** "PERSON, as I take it, is the name for this self. Wherever a man finds what he calls himself, there, I think, another may say is the same person. It is a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit; and so belongs only to intelligent agents, capable of a law, and happiness, and misery" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:12194-12198]. To be punished for an act one cannot be made conscious of would be no different from being "CREATED miserable" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:12206-12214].

## Grounding

The *man* concept rests on **species** and biology: one continued animal life. The *person* concept rests on **capacity**: reason, reflection, reflexive self-consciousness over time, the capacity for law, and the capacity for happiness and misery. Locke explicitly says the person question is independent of both the body and the underlying substance. That independence is what makes the concept extensible. Locke did not have artificial agents in mind, but his framework was built so that "person" would not require being a man.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The man/person split is exactly the distinction the compendium needs.** No concept has to be stretched. *Is this agent a person?* is a well-formed Lockean question, and *it is not a man* does not answer it.
- **Substance-independence.** Locke's indifference to what substance does the thinking removes the *it is only silicon* objection, at least within his framework. The question is whether the capacities are present.
- **Forensic function.** Societies that deploy agents already face Locke's practical problem: to whom are this action and its merit to be appropriated? Locke's answer, the self that is conscious of doing it, is one candidate among several (operator, developer, the model family).

### Strains

- **The two forensic conditions come apart.** Being capable "of a law" is plausibly met by current agents, which can represent rules, apply them, and be held to them. Being capable of "happiness, and misery" is exactly what is uncertain (**D8**). Locke never had to consider a being that clearly meets the first condition while the second stays open. If both are required, agent personhood waits on the hardest question in the field.
- **Consciousness as self-report.** Locke's criterion runs through consciousness of past acts. An agent's reports about its own states were shaped by training and may not track anything (**D8**). Locke's courts faced the same verification gap with drunks and sleepwalkers, and resolved it pragmatically.

### Breaks

- **The memory criterion under the weights/context split (D3).** Most deployed agents keep their dispositions (weights) but carry no memory from one session to the next. Applied strictly, Locke's criterion says each session is a new person that lasts one conversation. The character, values, and competence carry over, but by Locke's lights none of that is identity. Either one accepts this ephemeral-persons conclusion, or one must move to a view that counts continuity of character, not only memory (see Parfit).
- **Duplication (D1) and concurrency (D2).** If two instances share the same weights and the same injected memory, both can extend consciousness back to the same past act. By Locke's criterion, both are that past person. Since they are not each other, identity (which is transitive and one-to-one) cannot be the relation that consciousness tracks. For humans this is a thought experiment. For agents it is standard practice.

### New

- **Memory is an editable, external store.** An agent's memory can be written, deleted, or transplanted by an operator. If personal identity is extension of consciousness, whoever controls the memory store controls who the agent *is*, and can assign or remove its responsibility for past acts. Locke had no reason to consider an identity criterion that can be manipulated by a third party.
- **The agent case clarifies the human one.** Because agents make fission and memory loss routine, they force a decision Locke could put off: is identity what matters for responsibility, or is it something weaker, like psychological connectedness, that can hold to degrees and branch?

## Counter-Positions

- [conceded] **Thomas Reid, the brave officer** [E:reid-brave-officer] (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785, III.6). A boy is flogged; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers the officer's feat but not the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is and is not the boy, which violates transitivity. Neo-Lockeans concede the point against direct memory and replace it with overlapping chains of memory (psychological continuity), which are transitive [P:olson-sep-2023:4] [E:parfit-reductionism]. The concession is the neo-Lockeans'. Interpreters of Locke still dispute whether the objection touches Locke's own view [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:3]. The objection applies with particular force to agents with selectively injected memory.
- [contested] **Joseph Butler, circularity** [E:butler-circularity] (*The Analogy of Religion*, 1736, Dissertation I). Consciousness of one's past presupposes personal identity, so it cannot constitute it. Genuine memory is distinguished from apparent memory only by the fact that the rememberer did the act. For agents, the difference between "genuine" and "injected" memory makes Butler's point concrete. The standard reply is Shoemaker's quasi-memory (1970), which is widely cited but not universally accepted [E:shoemaker-quasi-memory].
- [conceded] **David Hume, memory discovers identity** [E:hume-bundle] (*A Treatise of Human Nature*, 1739, I.iv.6). We ascribe identity to stretches of our lives we cannot remember, by following the chain of causes, so memory reveals personal identity rather than producing it. Neo-Lockeans conceded the point when they replaced memory with the more general notion of causal psychological dependence [P:olson-sep-2023:4]. Hume's further claim, that the identity is a fiction of the imagination, is not conceded.
- [contested] **Immanuel Kant, the elastic balls** [E:kant-paralogisms] (*Critique of Pure Reason*, Third Paralogism, 1781). Kant answers Locke's question whether consciousness can be transferred between substances (II.xxvii.13). Imagine a series of substances, each passing its states and its consciousness of them to the next. The last would be conscious of all the earlier states as its own, yet would not have been one and the same person. On this view, consciousness of identity shows only a formal unity of thought, not the numerical identity of a subject. Parfit's reply is that such a chain is all that matters, whether or not we call it identity [E:parfit-reductionism].
- [contested] **Animalism** (e.g., Eric Olson, *The Human Animal*, 1997). We are fundamentally organisms, and "person" names a phase or a role, not a separate thing. An animalist can hold that an agent's "person" has no underlying persisting subject at all, so the Lockean extension describes a role without a bearer.
- [contested] **Parfit's reductionism** [E:parfit-reductionism] (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, Part III). Identity is not what matters. What matters is psychological connectedness and continuity, which can hold to degrees and can branch. On this view, the agent's ephemeral-persons problem dissolves, and it does so at the price of giving up strict identity as the basis for responsibility.
- [contested] **Deflationary views of AI self-models.** An agent's ability to "consider itself as itself" [L:locke-essay-1-pg10615:11735-11736] is text generation about a self-description, not reflexive consciousness. On this view Locke's first condition is not met at all, and the discussion never gets started.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1694–1704, immediate challenge.** [driver: argument] The chapter was added to the second edition. Leibniz, writing in 1704 (published 1765), accepted that *moral* identity rests on memory but insisted that it rides on a *real* identity of substance that never fails, and pressed Locke with the King of China and duplicate-earth cases [E:leibniz-moral-identity].
- **1697, Sergeant: circularity.** [driver: argument] John Sergeant, Locke's earliest critic, answered Essay II.xxvii in *Solid Philosophy Asserted*. Consciousness is knowledge that an act belonged to us, so the knower must have his individuality from other principles, prior to that knowledge. In his words, "a Man must be the same, ere he can know or be Conscious that he is the same" (Reflexion XIV sec. 12, pp. 265-266; checked against the page image, long s transcribed as s) [L:sergeant-solid-philosophy-1697:13342-13384]. This is Butler's objection, 39 years early.
- **1732, Berkeley: transitivity.** [driver: argument] In *Alciphron* (Seventh Dialogue, sec. 8), Euphranor runs the consciousness criterion through three stages, A, B and C, where C shares no ideas with A, and concludes that "personal identity doth not consist in consciousness" [L:berkeley-works-2-fraser-1901:19345-19360]. It anticipates Reid by 53 years, but it was not the first version. Martin and Barresi trace the line of objection to Henry Grove (1720), Thiel finds it in Jonathan Edwards's early notes on the mind, and Stewart thinks Reid's source was George Campbell (1748), not Berkeley [P:gordon-roth-2019:12]. Gordon-Roth argues that Berkeley's target was Anthony Collins's consciousness view rather than Locke's, although the commentators she surveys all take the objection to be aimed at Locke [P:gordon-roth-2019:3]. Olson credits Sergeant and Berkeley with both objections, following Behan 1979, and places both in the 18th century [P:olson-sep-2023:4]. Sergeant's title page is dated 1697 (the OCR garbles the year) [L:sergeant-solid-philosophy-1697:84-87], so that dating is wrong for him. Whether the slip is Olson's or Behan's is unknown, since Behan's article is not accessible.
- **1702–1708, defenders and radicalizers.** [driver: argument] Not all early readers attacked Locke. Catharine Trotter Cockburn's *Defence of Mr. Locke's Essay* (1702) answered three pamphlets that attacked his account of persons. In his correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1707–1708), Anthony Collins held a consciousness-based view and pushed Locke's position further than Locke had [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:3].
- **1736–1785, the standard refutations.** [driver: argument] Butler's circularity objection [E:butler-circularity] and Reid's brave officer [E:reid-brave-officer] became the canonical case against the memory criterion. Against *direct* memory they were decisive. The later response was to reformulate the criterion rather than abandon it: indirect, overlapping memory chains answer Reid, and quasi-memory answers Butler [P:olson-sep-2023:4].
- **1739–1781, from the substance question to the subject.** [driver: argument] Hume, finding no self but a bundle of perceptions, made personal identity a fiction of the imagination, and made causation, not memory, its source [E:hume-bundle]. Kant accepted that consciousness always finds its own identity, and denied that this shows a persisting subject, answering Locke's transfer question with the elastic balls [E:kant-paralogisms]. Both kept Locke's question and changed what an answer could prove.
- **19th century, treated as refuted in the dominant school.** [driver: argument, access] Reid's Scottish common-sense school supplied the psychology generally taught in Britain and America for the first half of the century [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:83-85]. Its leading editor, Sir William Hamilton, wrote in a note to Reid that "Long before Butler, to whom the merit is usually ascribed", Locke's doctrine had been attacked and refuted, first by Sergeant, and that Leibniz made the same objection. He pointed to Cousin's lectures for the best criticism [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13859-13888]. The note also shows how priority was lost: Hamilton quotes Sergeant at length because he was "an author wholly unknown to all historians of philosophy" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13862-13864]. That is an `access` effect, not an argument. Hamilton cites the passage as Reflexion XIV sec. 14. The 1697 page images show it is sec. 12 (pp. 265-266); sec. 14 is Sergeant's later passage on angels.
- **1816–1906, the clinical test.** [driver: evidence] French and American clinicians recorded cases in which one body carried more than one self, each with its own memories, and memory split asymmetrically: Félida X.'s second state remembered the first but was not remembered by it. Prince's patient denied being the waking self on psychological grounds, not bodily ones [E:dissociation-cases]. These were the first cases in which Locke's criterion met real subjects rather than thought experiments. Law and common sense kept one body, one person. The patients themselves reasoned like Lockeans.
- **1849, outside the Scottish school.** [driver: argument] Kierkegaard defined the self as a relation that relates itself to itself: an activity, not a substance. This is a practical, self-relating criterion that continues Locke's forensic person by other means [E:kierkegaard-self-as-relation]. The dominant English-language school's verdict was not everyone's.
- **1890, James: the soul made superfluous.** [driver: argument, evidence] William James identified most modern writers of the dualistic philosophy as "the Scotch school" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:13408-13412]. He credited Locke with making readers feel that the important unity of the self was "its verifiable and felt unity" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:13534-13539]. He pointed to the split selves of the clinical cases against the soul's supposed guarantee of one closed consciousness [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:13491-13498], and concluded that the substantial soul was superfluous for scientific purposes [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:13522-13526]. This is where the dissociation cases turned from evidence into argument.
- **1921–1925, the return.** [driver: argument, fashion] Russell defined one biography as "a series of occurrences linked by mnemic causation" [L:russell-analysis-of-mind-pg2529:2262-2271], and held that mnemic phenomena give the continuity of a person or a mind [L:russell-analysis-of-mind-pg2529:3538-3547]. This is a causal-memory account of persons, half a century before Shoemaker, and it descends from Hume's causal version of the view as much as from Locke's memory version. By 1925 Broad could report that denying a Pure Ego meant flogging "what most of his contemporaries rightly or wrongly regard as a dead horse" [L:broad-mind-place-nature-1925:25098-25124]. The substance alternative that the Scottish school had defended was by then widely treated as dead, and Broad's "rightly or wrongly" marks that as a shift in opinion, not a refutation. TODO(source): Grice's memory theory (*Mind*, 1941) is the first full modern statement and is still pending as a source.
- **20th century, the prevailing view among specialists.** [driver: argument] Most philosophers writing on personal identity since the early 20th century have endorsed some version of the psychological-continuity view [P:olson-sep-2023:3]. Taken with the 19th-century record, that is a real fall and return, but the return came early in the century, not in 1970, and it came in a reformulated form. The later theorists claim the descent themselves: Perry, Lewis, Shoemaker and Parfit each take their views to be Locke's descendants, and Parfit defended what he called a "Lockean view" as late as 2016 [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:4].
- **1970–1984, reformulation.** [driver: argument] Quasi-memory (Penelhum 1970; Shoemaker 1970) answered Butler, and McDowell (1997) later criticised it [P:olson-sep-2023:4]. Shoemaker then generalized memory into causal psychological continuity, which also covers dreamless sleep [P:olson-sep-2023:4]. Williams showed the same case could be framed to support or to undercut the psychological criterion [E:williams-self-and-future]. Parfit rebuilt the view as overlapping psychological continuity and argued that identity itself is not what matters [E:parfit-reductionism]. The result is *neo-Lockean* psychological continuity, not Locke's direct-memory criterion.
- **1979–present, the reading in dispute.** [driver: argument] Whether Locke held a memory criterion at all is contested. Olson notes that it is uncertain, citing Behan 1979 [P:olson-sep-2023:2]. The strict-memory reading of Locke dates back at least to Reid, and several scholars deny that Lockean consciousness is simply memory [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:2]. Sympathetic readers of Locke are still answering Reid's and Butler's objections [P:gordon-roth-sep-2025:3]. So what Reid and Butler refuted may be a reading of Locke rather than Locke himself.
- **1990s–present, contested plurality.** [driver: argument] Psychological-continuity views face two rivals: brute-physical views, most of whose defenders are animalists, and anticriterialism, which denies there are informative persistence conditions at all [P:olson-sep-2023:3]. Animalists concede the transplant intuition against them but hold it outweighed [P:olson-sep-2023:7]. Olson calls fission a more serious worry for psychological views than the memory objections [P:olson-sep-2023:5]. Roache (2016) calls memory-based accounts "perhaps the dominant contemporary view" [P:roache2016:479]. The 2020 survey instead measures a plurality, not a majority (see `### Measured`), which is an example of published esteem and measurement disagreeing. The quasi-memory reply to Butler is still debated: Klein and Nichols (2012) pressed a new problem for Parfit's version, and Roache defends it [P:roache2016:480-486].

### Measured

These figures measure the neo-Lockean *psychological view* in general, not Locke's direct-memory criterion, which few now defend as stated.

- **2020, English-publishing philosophers.** Of a target population of 7,685, 1,785 completed the survey. On *Personal identity*, the share who "accept or lean toward" each view (counting multiple endorsements, excluding skips and "insufficiently familiar") was: psychological view 43.7%, biological view 19.1%, further-fact view 14.9%, other 26.6%. Counting only respondents who endorsed that view alone: 39.4%, 15.6% and 13.4% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8].
- **Bias-corrected** for gender and specialization, over all answers including skips: psychological 39.46%, biological 16.43%, further-fact 12.73% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:32].
- **2009 to 2020, the 2009-comparable departments** (single-view endorsements, with skips counted as other): psychological 33.6% to 37.0%, biological 16.9% to 15.3%, further-fact 12.2% to 11.6%, other 37.3% to 36.1%. Among the same people answering both years, the psychological view went from 35.6% to 37.7% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:39].
- **Specialists are less favourable.** Among metaphysicians, 52.1% of non-"other" answers favour the psychological view, against 62.1% among non-specialists [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:51].
- **Link to the agent question.** Holding the psychological view correlates with answering that one survives mind uploading (r = 0.36, n = 733) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:43].
- **Reading.** The psychological view is the plurality but not a majority, has grown slightly since 2009, and is weaker among specialists. That supports `current = "major"`, not `dominant`. The measured population is English-publishing philosophers only, which is the survey's own stated limit [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:2].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** The criterion needs memory that reaches back across time, and a session-bound agent's episodic memory ends with the session while its character persists in the weights (D3). Applied strictly, each session is a new person, which is the conclusion this entry's Summary already draws.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** The gains and losses roughly cancel. Reid's case loses force: the memory is logged, so the old general can still read the flogging, and the chain need not break. Butler's objection gains force: a memory store does not distinguish genuine records from injected ones unless provenance is kept, so operator edits make the gap between real and apparent memory concrete (D3, D11).
- **Forked: weaker.** Copies and concurrent instances share one past and then diverge (D1, D2). Reid's consequence that many intelligent beings could count as one person becomes an ordinary event rather than a thought experiment, and a one-to-one criterion has no answer to it. Psychological-continuity theorists themselves split on whether copying mental contents to a new substrate preserves the person: Shoemaker says yes, Unger says no [P:olson-sep-2023:4]. For an agent, that copy is routine. Kant's elastic balls [E:kant-paralogisms], a series of substances each handing its full state and consciousness to the next, describe what an agent does when it hands its state to a successor instance (D1).
- **Self-modifying: open.** When deliberations are trained back into the weights, experience persists as disposition rather than as recallable memory. Whether that counts as Lockean consciousness extending backward is exactly this entry's Open Question 5, and the Compendium cannot settle it yet (D3).

## Open Questions

1. For an agent, is the Lockean person the weights, the instance, or the persona (**D11**)? Each answer gives a different lifespan and a different count of persons.
2. If personhood requires the capacity for happiness and misery, can an agent be a partial person: an appropriate subject of law and blame, but not of welfare?
3. When an operator edits an agent's memory store, has it changed the agent, or replaced one person with another?
4. Should persistent memory be *owed* to agents that are candidate persons, because without it they cannot have the continuity that responsibility, promising, and growth require?
5. Does training on its own past deliberations (Actualizer's self-distillation) give an agent a form of memory that persists through weights, which is to say Lockean consciousness that survives the end of a session?
