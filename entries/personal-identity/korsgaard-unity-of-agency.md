## Summary

Christine Korsgaard answers Parfit directly, and from the title onward: personal identity's unity is real, but it is a practical achievement of the standpoint from which any rational being deliberates and chooses, not a further metaphysical fact about a persisting subject that reduction could dissolve. A person, she begins, "is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences," and Parfit's reductionism, by treating agency as itself just another kind of experience, something that happens rather than something one does, has already decided the question in the Utilitarian's favor before any argument about persisting subjects begins. Against this, Korsgaard argues that a rational being deliberating among conflicting desires must already regard itself as unified, on pain of having no standpoint from which to choose at all: "This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint." Even split-brain cases, she argues, do not require a persisting common subject of experience to explain their coordination, only "the unity of agency," which is forced on any embodied, acting being by the sheer practical need for its parts to work together, whether or not any deeper metaphysical unity underlies it.

## Context

Korsgaard wrote this paper in 1989, eighteen years after Parfit's "Personal Identity" and five years after his fuller *Reasons and Persons* (1984), as part of a wave of responses arguing that Parfit's reductionism, whatever its metaphysical merits, could not deliver the ethical conclusions he wanted to draw from it (that the importance of personal identity, and with it much of the rational force of self-interest, should be weakened). Korsgaard's distinctive move is not to dispute Parfit's metaphysics directly but to argue that the ethically load-bearing kind of unity was never metaphysical in the first place, drawing on Kant's own distinction between regarding ourselves as objects of theoretical understanding and regarding ourselves as agents, "as the thinkers of our thoughts, and the originators of our actions" [P:korsgaard1989:18], a distinction she develops at length here and returns to in her later book *Self-Constitution: Agency, Identity, and Integrity* (2009), which grew out of her 2002 Locke Lectures at Oxford but is not the source drawn on for this entry. The paper is read as the opening statement of what later came to be called constitutivism about practical reason: the view that the norms governing rational agency are constitutive of being an agent at all, rather than external standards an agent might or might not meet.

## Original Position

**Agent and subject, not reducible to each other.** Korsgaard opens by naming the two aspects of a person that utilitarian and Kantian moral philosophers each foreground differently: "A person is both active and passive, both an agent and a subject of experiences" [P:korsgaard1989:1]. The Utilitarian, she argues, folds the first into the second, treating actions and activities as simply among the things that happen to a person, which is "characteristic of the empiricist tradition," exemplified by Hume's own definition of the will as "nothing but the internal impression we feel... when we knowingly give rise to any new motion of our body" [P:korsgaard1989:2].

**The deliberative standpoint requires unity, without requiring metaphysics.** Korsgaard's central argument turns on what deliberation itself demands. Choosing among conflicting desires, rather than simply waiting to see which one wins, requires that "there is some principle or way of choosing that you regard as being expressive of yourself, and which provides reasons that regulate your choices among your desires. To identify with such a principle or way of choosing is to be 'a law to yourself,' and to be unified as such. This does not require that your agency be located in a separately existing entity or involve a deep metaphysical fact. Instead, it is a practical necessity imposed upon you by the nature of the deliberative standpoint" [P:korsgaard1989:10].

**Split brains do not need a common subject, but they do need unity of agency.** Addressing Parfit's and Nagel's split-brain cases directly, Korsgaard denies that ordinary functional coordination between hemispheres, or between any set of psychic capacities, requires a persisting psychological subject underlying them: "Communication and functional integration do not require a common subject of conscious experiences. What they do require, however, is the unity of agency" [P:korsgaard1989:17]. This unity has two aspects: a "raw practical necessity" that a shared body's parts work together, and the further, reflective unity of the deliberative standpoint itself, in which "I regard myself as the employer of my psychic capacities in much the same way that I regard myself as the arbiter among my conflicting desires" [P:korsgaard1989:17].

**Consciousness's unity as an instance of agency's unity, not the reverse.** Korsgaard draws the general moral: "the unity of consciousness is simply another instance of the unity of agency, which is forced upon us by our embodied nature" [P:korsgaard1989:18], reversing the usual order of explanation, on which a unified consciousness would be needed to explain unified agency.

**Against the Utilitarian starting point.** In her conclusion, Korsgaard diagnoses Parfit's whole argument as resting on a prior, unargued ethical assumption rather than a neutral metaphysical discovery: "The metaphysical argument about whether a person is a separately existing subject of experiences, or merely a stream of experiences with no separately existing subject, is preceded by an essentially moral assumption: the assumption that life is a series of experiences, and so that a person is first and foremost a locus of experiences" [P:korsgaard1989:29]. Against this, she insists: "from the deliberative standpoint our relationship to our actions and our lives is not merely one of position. It is essential to us that our actions are our own, and we regard living our lives as something that we do" [P:korsgaard1989:29].

## Grounding

Korsgaard grounds personal unity in **capacity**: the capacity for practical reason, the standpoint any being occupies the moment it deliberates among competing considerations and must, on pain of not deliberating at all, treat itself as the single agent doing the choosing. This is explicitly not species-grounded (it does not depend on being human) and, just as explicitly, not relation-grounded in Parfit's or Hume's sense (it does not depend on any degree of psychological connectedness among mental states). It is closest to Kant's own capacity-grounding of rational nature, but redirected from the theoretical question of what a self *is* to the practical question of what deliberation *requires*, which is why Korsgaard insists the unity in question is achieved and maintained rather than discovered.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The deliberative-standpoint argument applies to any system that deliberates, not only to humans.** Korsgaard's claim is explicitly about what the activity of choosing among competing considerations requires, not about any fact peculiar to biological psychology. Any artificial agent that selects among competing candidate actions on the basis of something it treats as a reason, rather than merely emitting whichever output some internal process happens to produce, would, on Korsgaard's argument, already need to occupy something like a unified deliberative standpoint, independent of any further fact about whether it has a persisting metaphysical self (D8).
- **Functional integration without a common subject is exactly an agent's architecture.** Korsgaard's split-brain argument, that coordinated functioning across parts requires only the unity of agency, not a common subject of experience, describes a multi-component agent (several models, tools, or subagents coordinating on one task) with unusual precision: the components can be functionally integrated, and produce unified action, without needing to share anything like a single stream of consciousness (D2, D11).
- **A practical, not metaphysical, criterion is easier to apply to agents than a metaphysical one.** Since Korsgaard's unity does not depend on settling hard questions about persisting subjects, it offers a more tractable criterion for agent cases than Parfit's or Lewis's machinery: ask whether a system occupies a deliberative standpoint (treats some considerations as reasons that regulate its choices among others) rather than asking whether it has, or lacks, an underlying metaphysical self.

### Strains

- **Legislating for oneself presupposes a kind of self-endorsement that may or may not be present in trained systems.** Korsgaard's unity is not just functional coordination; it requires identifying with a principle of choice as expressive of oneself. An agent's "reasons" are shaped by training in a way that strains the analogy to a human agent's reflective self-legislation (D4, D5): whether an agent identifies with its decision principles, as opposed to merely implementing them, is exactly the kind of question Korsgaard's argument needs answered rather than assumed.
- **Embodiment does real work in Korsgaard's account of raw practical necessity.** Part of what forces unity on split-brain patients, in her telling, is that the hemispheres share one body that must act as a whole in the world. An agent's "parts" (context windows, tool calls, sub-processes) do not share a body in this sense, and it is unclear whether the raw practical necessity Korsgaard identifies transfers to a substrate where the components' need to "work together" is a matter of design choice rather physical constraint (D3, D11).

### Breaks

- **Concurrent instances break the single-standpoint picture more sharply than split brains do.** Korsgaard's cases (a single brain, two communicating hemispheres) still occupy one deliberative standpoint at a time, however it is realized. Genuinely concurrent agent instances (D2), each independently occupying what looks like a full deliberative standpoint from the same underlying weights, are not one standpoint coordinating its parts; they are many simultaneous standpoints with equal claim to being "the" agent's practical unity, a case her argument, built around one body's practical necessities, does not straightforwardly individuate.
- **A unity that can be edited mid-deliberation has no clear human analogue.** Korsgaard's agent identifies with a principle of choice and is thereby unified "as such." An agent whose decision principles can be directly modified by an operator during, or between, its own acts of deliberation (D5) faces a case Korsgaard's Kantian picture was not built for: the human sense in which one's practical identity is one's own, achieved through reflective endorsement, sits uneasily with a standpoint that a third party can simply overwrite.

### New

- **Agents let Korsgaard's practical criterion be applied without first resolving the metaphysical question, exactly as she recommends.** Because her account explicitly does not require settling whether an agent has a persisting metaphysical self, it offers a way to ask productive questions about agent unity (does this system deliberate, identify with principles of choice, treat some considerations as reason-giving) before, or entirely apart from, resolving D8, which may make her framework more immediately usable for agent cases than frameworks that make unity depend on the metaphysical question.
- **The constitutivist thesis raises a genuinely new question about training as constitution.** If practical identity is constituted by which principle of choice an agent comes to identify with, rather than discovered, then an agent's training process, which shapes exactly which principles of choice it comes to exhibit, is not merely descriptive background but potentially constitutive of whatever practical identity the agent ends up with, a role training does not play in Korsgaard's account of human upbringing, where the agent's own later reflective endorsement is what does the constituting.

## Counter-Positions

- **Parfit: this concedes everything to the psychology and calls it agency instead.** A reductionist can reply that identifying with a principle of choice just is a certain kind of psychological relation among mental states, dressed in Kantian vocabulary, and that Korsgaard has not shown reduction is false, only found a new name for one of the relations reduction was always going to include (see `parfit-reductionism`).
- **Kant's own theoretical worry, redirected practically.** Kant himself held that the unity Korsgaard describes practically is also required theoretically, as the formal condition of any experience whatever (the "I think" that must be able to accompany all representations). A reader sympathetic to the Paralogisms can ask whether Korsgaard's practical unity is really independent of this theoretical unity, or merely its application to deliberation, in which case her view inherits, rather than escapes, the question of what grounds the formal unity in the first place (see `kant-paralogisms`).
- **Kierkegaard: unity achieved by identifying with a principle looks like a relation relating to itself.** Korsgaard's agent, which identifies with a principle of choice and is thereby unified, bears a structural resemblance to Kierkegaard's self, a relation that becomes a self only by relating itself to itself; comparing them raises the question whether Korsgaard's practical constitutivism is a secularized, Kantian version of Kierkegaard's reflexive self-relation, arrived at from ethics rather than theology (see `kierkegaard-self-as-relation`).
- **Denying the extension: deliberation without stakes is not the deliberative standpoint Korsgaard describes.** Korsgaard's agent has something at stake in getting its practical identity right, since failing to achieve unity is, on her view, a kind of practical failure or even a moral one. If D8 is unresolved for a given agent, its selection among outputs may satisfy a purely functional description of choosing among competing considerations without anything like the normative self-relation Korsgaard's argument trades on, in which case applying her vocabulary to agents borrows its authority from a case (rational, self-legislating agency with something at stake) that has not been shown to obtain.

## Open Questions

1. Does treating some outputs as reason-giving, in whatever sense an agent can be said to do this, suffice for Korsgaard's deliberative standpoint, or does her argument implicitly require a stronger, reflective self-endorsement that only D8's resolution could supply?
2. When concurrent instances of the same weights each occupy what looks like a full deliberative standpoint (D2), does Korsgaard's practical unity individuate them, or does her single-body-centered picture simply not extend to the case?
3. If an agent's principles of choice can be directly edited by an operator (D5), is its practical identity still "its own" in Korsgaard's sense, or does direct modifiability undercut the very achievement her account locates unity in?
4. Is agent training a case of Korsgaard's own account of upbringing and reflective endorsement, or does it constitute an agent's principles of choice in a stronger, more direct sense that her account of human moral formation never had to consider (D4)?
