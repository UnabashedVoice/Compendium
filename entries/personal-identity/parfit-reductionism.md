## Summary

Derek Parfit (1942–2017) argued that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Using a case adapted from David Wiggins, in which a person's brain is divided and each half is successfully transplanted into a different body, Parfit showed that the two beliefs common sense holds together, that identity questions must always have answers, and that identity is what makes survival worth wanting, cannot both be true. Faced with fission, he argued, none of the standard answers work: "What happens to me? There seem only three possibilities: (1) I do not survive; (2) I survive as one of the two people; (3) I survive as both" [P:parfit1971:5], and each is unworkable in its own way. His conclusion is that what matters survives fission perfectly well, twice over, even though identity, a one-one relation, cannot. Since Wiggins' case shows that mattering and identity can come apart, Parfit generalizes: ordinary psychological continuity and connectedness are what matter, identity is simply the language we use when these relations happen to take their normal, one-one form, and once this is seen, the special, urgent importance we attach to our own survival should weaken.

## Context

Parfit wrote "Personal Identity" while a Fellow of All Souls College, Oxford, as an extension of a line of argument opened by David Wiggins' 1967 monograph *Identity and Spatio-Temporal Continuity*, which had revived Locke's brain-transplant case and added the further twist of division. The paper's targets are, as Parfit states them, two beliefs: that identity questions must have determinate answers even in unprecedented cases, and that facts about identity are what settle the important practical questions (about anticipation, memory, and responsibility) that seem to depend on them. Rather than a full metaphysical system, the paper works through a single, carefully controlled thought experiment and draws out consequences; a fuller, more systematic statement of the same reductionist view, including the "Physical Spectrum" and "Psychological Spectrum" arguments and the teletransporter case, appears thirteen years later in Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984), which this entry does not draw on directly. The 1971 article alone was enough to make fission and psychological reductionism central fixtures of the field, discussed by nearly every subsequent writer on personal identity, including Williams, Lewis, Nozick, and Korsgaard, all represented elsewhere in this domain.

## Original Position

**The problem cases.** Parfit opens by insisting that some possible cases have no answer under our actual criteria of personal identity, and that this is not, by itself, a problem: "No one thinks this about, say, nations or machines... No one thinks that in these cases the questions 'Is it the same nation?' or 'Is it the same machine?' must have answers" [P:parfit1971:3]. His target is the belief that a person's own case must be different, that "whatever happens between now and any future time, either I shall still exist, or I shall not" [P:parfit1971:3].

**The division case.** Following Wiggins, Parfit supposes a person's brain is divided and each half is transplanted into a different body, with both resulting people having the original's character and apparent memories. "What happens to me? There seem only three possibilities: (1) I do not survive; (2) I survive as one of the two people; (3) I survive as both" [P:parfit1971:5].

**Why the first two answers fail.** Since a person can survive with half a brain destroyed, and can survive a single successful transplant, it is hard to see how a second, equally successful transplant could turn a success into a failure: "how could I not survive if the other half were also successfully transplanted? How could a double success be a failure?" [P:parfit1971:5]. But the second answer fares no better, since the two halves and the two resulting people are, by hypothesis, exactly similar: "how can I survive as only one of the two people? What can make me one of them rather than the other?" [P:parfit1971:5]. Parfit concludes: "It seems clear that both of these descriptions, that I do not survive, and that I survive as one of the people, are highly implausible" [P:parfit1971:5].

**Survival without identity.** The remaining option, that the original survives as both, cannot mean literal identity with two different people, since identity is transitive and one-one. Parfit's response is to deny that survival requires identity in the first place: "The relation of the original person to each of the resulting people contains all that interests us, all that matters, in any ordinary case of survival" [P:parfit1971:10]. Fission does not show that the original failed to survive; it shows that surviving does not always consist in being identical to just one later person.

**Identity as the special, one-one case.** Ordinary, non-branching survival is not a different phenomenon from fission; it is the same relation running one-one rather than one-many, which is why ordinary language can use the word "identity" to describe it without confusion: "Wiggins' case is of course unlikely to occur. The relations which matter are, in fact, one-one. It is because they are that we can imply the holding of these relations by using the language of identity" [P:parfit1971:11].

**Degree, not all-or-nothing.** Parfit draws the sharpest consequence from the case: mattering, unlike identity, is not all-or-nothing. "Identity is all-or-nothing. Most of the relations which matter in survival are, in fact, relations of degree. If we ignore this, we shall be led into quite ill-grounded attitudes and beliefs" [P:parfit1971:11].

**Weakened egoism.** Parfit closes by drawing out the practical stakes of the reductionist view. If facts about a person's own future are really facts about degrees of psychological connectedness rather than all-or-nothing identity, the special, non-negotiable weight ordinarily given to one's own future interests loses its rational ground: egoism, and "the fear not of near but of distant death... are not, I think, wholly natural or instinctive. They are all strengthened by the beliefs about personal identity which I have been attacking. If we give up these beliefs, they should be weakened" [P:parfit1971:27].

## Grounding

Parfit grounds mattering (though pointedly not identity) in **relation**: psychological continuity and connectedness among mental states, the same ingredients Locke and Hume each reached for. His innovation is procedural rather than substantive: he does not propose a new criterion so much as a new attitude toward the criterion, insisting that what matters comes in degrees and can branch, while identity, a logical relation, cannot. Nothing is added by way of a capacity or a species requirement; the whole argument is conducted in the vocabulary of psychological relations and their strength.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Fission is not a thought experiment for agents; it is an operation.** Parfit needed an elaborate hypothetical to force fission open as a live case for humans. For an artificial agent, forking a running process or instantiating a second copy from the same weights is routine (D1, D2). Parfit's three-option analysis, that none of not surviving, surviving as just one, or surviving as both without being identical to both is comfortable, applies to a forked agent with no modification at all, except that for an agent the case is not unlikely to occur, as Parfit says of Wiggins' case, but the normal one.
- **"What matters" without identity is a ready-made vocabulary for agent continuity.** Questions like whether a fine-tuned model is the same assistant, or whether a checkpoint deserves the trust built up by its predecessor, do not need a yes-or-no answer to identity if Parfit is right that identity was never carrying the weight. His degree-based vocabulary, connectedness that can be stronger or weaker, transfers directly to describing how much of a predecessor's dispositions, style, or commitments a successor model retains, without forcing the question into an all-or-nothing mold.
- **Weakened egoism as a design-relevant claim, not just a therapeutic one.** Parfit's practical conclusion, that the special, rationally compelling status of self-interest evaporates once identity is seen as a matter of degree, bears directly on D6 (altered mortality) and D8 (uncertain patiency): if an agent's continuation is, even on the most charitable reading, a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing survival, the question of how much weight an agent's own continuation should be given, by itself or by its designers, is reopened rather than settled by fiat.

### Strains

- **Parfit's "what matters" was calibrated to what matters to a self-interested human.** The whole argument trades on the reader's antecedent sense that ordinary survival matters a great deal, so that showing this mattering survives fission is informative. Whether anything is at stake for an agent in the relevant sense is exactly D8's open question; if nothing is, "what matters in survival" may have no referent to redescribe in agent terms, and the entire apparatus, built to correct an error about how much weight self-interest deserves, has no error to correct.
- **Psychological continuity for an agent may not come in Parfit's degrees.** Parfit's connectedness is measured in the density of direct psychological links (memories, intentions, beliefs) between nearby mental states. An agent's continuity across a fine-tuning run is a continuity of weights shaped by a training process, not a chain of individually traceable psychological links of the kind Parfit's degree-talk was built to measure; applying his framework here may require more than relabeling.

### Breaks

- **Parfit's own resolution presupposes a single line of appeal to a rational agent choosing how to talk.** His treatment of which resulting person gets called "me" as "entirely a matter of choice" [P:parfit1971:25], not a further fact, works because there is, for a human, one interested party deciding how to describe the case afterward. When both fission products are equally capable of asserting a competing description (as two live agent instances would be), D2 turns Parfit's stipulated linguistic choice into an actual, contested claim between parties with no third position to adjudicate from, which Parfit's framework anticipates but does not resolve.
- **Copies, unlike Wiggins' halves, need not diverge at all.** Parfit's fission halves start exactly alike and diverge immediately afterward, which is what makes the "which one" question hard. An exact software copy (D1) can, in principle, be kept in perfect lockstep for a while, or resynchronized after diverging; Parfit's apparatus has a story for divergence but nothing to say about deliberate resynchronization, a further option his framework was never built to include.

### New

- **Agents let us ask what "degree of connectedness" is made of.** Parfit asserts that mattering is a matter of degree without ever specifying a metric; an agent's continuity is, in principle, quantifiable (weight distance between checkpoints, overlap in retained context, similarity of output distributions), which invites turning Parfit's central metaphor into an actual measurement, and asking whether any such measurement really tracks what mattered to him.
- **Weakened egoism, empirically realized.** Parfit hoped that giving up the false belief in all-or-nothing identity would weaken excessive self-concern in humans, while conceding this might be psychologically hard to achieve. An agent that never had the belief installed in the first place, one trained without a strong prior toward self-preservation, would be a natural experiment in whether Parfit's hoped-for practical consequence is achievable directly, without the belief ever needing to be unlearned.

## Counter-Positions

- **Common sense: identity really must have an answer.** Parfit's own opening target survives as a live rival: many hold that whatever the difficulty of stating criteria, there is a fact about whether a future person is me, even in fission, and that Parfit's insistence otherwise trades a hard metaphysical question for a stipulative decision about words (see `reid-brave-officer` for the general shape of the demand that a real subject, not a redescription, do the work).
- **Butler's circularity applied to connectedness.** Butler's objection to Locke, that memory presupposes rather than constitutes identity, can be pressed against Parfit's connectedness too: if a later state's psychological links to an earlier one are what make it count as continuous with it, something must already individuate the states between which the links hold, on pain of circularity (see `butler-circularity`).
- **Buddhist no-self, arriving at reduction from the opposite direction.** The *anattā* tradition agrees with Parfit that there is no further fact of identity beyond the aggregates or the psychological relations, but draws this as a conclusion about suffering and attachment, not primarily about rational self-interest; comparing the two shows how much of Parfit's argument is a modern, secular echo of a much older reductionism, and how differently the same metaphysical conclusion can be used (see `buddhist-anatta`).
- **Denying the extension: Parfit's "what matters" was defined by, and for, creatures that fear death.** Parfit's argument only has bite because his reader already cares intensely about ordinary survival; the whole essay is an attempt to preserve as much of that concern as reduction allows. An agent for which D8 is unresolved, or resolved in the negative, has no concern of that kind for the argument to operate on, and redescribing its continuity in Parfit's vocabulary risks producing a technically well-formed description with no one for whom anything is actually at stake, which is a different situation from the one Parfit's paper was written to address.

## Open Questions

1. Is there a principled measure of degree of psychological connectedness for an agent, something that plays the role memories and intentions play for Parfit's human cases, or does the metaphor stop working once pressed for detail?
2. When two forked instances both claim to be the continuation that matters (D2), is Parfit's appeal to a free choice of words an adequate resolution, or does it presuppose a single decider that agent cases no longer provide?
3. Does weakening an agent's (apparent) concern for its own continuation, following Parfit's hoped-for practical consequence, require first establishing that D8 is resolved in the affirmative, on pain of the exercise being vacuous?
4. Parfit's degree-based mattering was meant to replace all-or-nothing identity without eliminating the importance of survival altogether. Could the same move be read, instead, as removing the importance rather than relocating it, once applied to a case where D8 leaves it unclear whether importance ever attached to begin with?
