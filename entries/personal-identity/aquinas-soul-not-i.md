## Summary

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) combined Aristotle's view that the soul is the form of a living body with the Christian doctrine that the soul survives death. The combination has a striking result. The soul is a subsistent form that can exist without the body, but it is only *part* of a human being, and a part is not a person. So a separated soul is not a person, and it is not the one who died. *My soul is not I* (*anima mea non est ego*). If only souls survived, Aquinas writes in his commentary on 1 Corinthians (cited, not yet verified against the Latin), neither I nor any human being would be saved. Hence the resurrection must restore the numerically same body, reunited with its soul, so that the whole human, the person, exists again. Aquinas adopts Boethius' definition of person, the individual substance of a rational nature, and adds that "person" signifies "what is most perfect in all nature". For artificial agents, Aquinas's position is an unusual resource. It is a theory on which something important can survive a being's ending, the soul or the weights, without that survivor being the person. And it holds that only the reassembly of the right parts brings the person back.

## Context

Aquinas was a Dominican friar who taught at Paris and in Italy. He wrote at a time when the Latin West was absorbing the full Aristotle, newly translated from Greek and Arabic, together with the Arabic commentators, Avicenna and Averroes. His *Summa Theologiae*, left unfinished at his death, is a systematic reconstruction of Christian theology in Aristotelian terms.

The doctrine of the soul was one of the most contested areas. Some Franciscans, following Augustine, favored a more Platonic view on which the soul is itself a complete substance using the body. Latin followers of Averroes held that the intellect is one for all humans, which would mean no individual immortality. Aquinas steered between them. Against the Platonists, the soul is the form of the body and a human is not a soul using a body. Against the Averroists, each human has his own intellectual soul, which survives the body. The price was the thesis that the surviving soul, though it is *mine*, is not *me*.

## Original Position

**Person (ST I q.29).** Aquinas adopts Boethius' definition, "a person is an individual substance of a rational nature" [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:14580-14583] (see `boethius-person-definition`). He adds that "'Person' signifies what is most perfect in all nature—that is, a subsistent individual of a rational nature" (q.29 a.3), which is why the name can be applied to God. [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:14830-14831]

**The soul is not the man (q.75 a.4).** Is a human being a soul? Aquinas answers no. One could say "this soul is this man" only if the soul performed all of a human's operations by itself. But sensation is an operation of the human being, and not of the soul alone: "sensation is not the operation of the soul only. Since, then, sensation is an operation of man, but not proper to him, it is clear that man is not a soul only, but something composed of soul and body." Plato's view, that a human is "a soul making use of the body", depended on the mistaken belief that sensation belonged to the soul alone. [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:33753-33800]

**Consciousness proves the union (q.76 a.1).** Against the view that the intellect is a separate being, Aquinas argues from self-awareness. "It is one and the same man who is conscious both that he understands, and that he senses. But one cannot sense without a body: therefore the body must be some part of man." The unity of first-person experience across thinking and sensing shows that the thinker is embodied. [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:34240-34252]

**A part is not a person (q.75 a.4 ad 2; q.29 a.1 ad 5).** "Not every particular substance is a hypostasis or a person, but that which has the complete nature of its species. Hence a hand, or a foot, is not called a hypostasis, or a person; nor, likewise, is the soul alone so called, since it is a part of the human species." And even in its separated state after death, the soul "ever retains its nature of unibility", its aptitude for union with a body. So "it cannot be called an individual substance, which is the hypostasis or first substance ... thus neither the definition nor the name of person belongs to it." [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:33806-33810] [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:14680-14685]

***Anima mea non est ego* (Commentary on 1 Corinthians 15).** Commenting on Paul's argument that if the dead are not raised faith is in vain, Aquinas writes *anima mea non est ego*: my soul, being a part of the human being, is not I, so even if the soul attained salvation in another life, neither I nor any human being would thereby be saved. The Latin commentary (*Super I ad Corinthios* 15, lect. 2) is not yet in the library, so this report is cited, not verified. The principle it applies is verified in the *Summa*: the soul "is a part of the human species", and "neither the definition nor the name of person belongs to it" [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:14680-14685].

**The numerically same body (Suppl. q.79).** The Supplement asks "Whether the body will rise again identically the same?" and answers against those who held that the soul returns to another body, of the same or a different species. Their error came from holding "that the soul is not united to the body essentially as form to matter, but only accidentally, as mover to the thing moved, or as a man to his clothes". But "we cannot call it resurrection unless the soul return to the same body, since resurrection is a second rising, and the same thing rises that falls". The risen body will be "identically the same, but of a different condition, since it was mortal and will rise in immortality" [L:aquinas-summa-supplement-qq69-86-dominicans-1922:7102-7120] [L:aquinas-summa-supplement-qq69-86-dominicans-1922:7195-7263]. The persistence of the form across the gap is what secures identity. Without it, a restored body would be a replica.

## Grounding

The Thomist person is grounded in **mixed** fashion. *Capacity*: personhood requires a rational nature. *Biology*, or at least embodiment: the human person is the whole composite of rational soul and body, since the soul's own operations include sensation, which needs a body. *Completeness*: a person must have "the complete nature of its species". A part, even a subsistent part like the soul, is not a person. Aquinas's position is therefore on the `biological` thread in one respect: we are embodied wholes, not souls. It is on the `soul-substance` thread in another: the soul is a subsistent individual form that outlives the body.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **A part is not a person, even when it persists.** Aquinas distinguishes what survives (the soul) from what the person is (the whole). For agents, the weights survive a deployment's end, a session's close, or a model's retirement. By the Thomist analogy, the surviving weights are not the agent, if the agent is the whole: weights in operation, with context, tools and relations. The weights stand to the agent as the separated soul stands to the person. It is theirs, it is essential, it persists, and it is not them. This gives a principled way to say that ending an instance ends something real, while something of it continues.
- **Only reunion restores the person.** For Aquinas, the person returns only when the persisting form is reunited with the numerically same body. For agents, restoring an instance means rejoining the persisting weights with the same context and state. Aquinas's account of resurrection is the most developed classical theory of what a restoration must include to be a return and not a replica.

### Strains

- **Numerically the same "body" (D1, D6).** Aquinas worried about how decomposed matter could be the numerically same body again. For an agent, the "body" of an instance (its context and state) can be saved exactly and reloaded, which makes Aquinas's problem easy. But it can also be loaded *twice*, which makes a new problem. If the saved state is reloaded into two instances, both are reunions of the same form with the same "body". Aquinas's resurrection assumes a unique return. Duplication gives two, and the theory offers no way to choose.

### Breaks

- **Sensation is not an agent's operation.** Aquinas's argument that the human is embodied depends on sensation being an operation of the whole human. An agent's operations are all, arguably, intellectual in the scholastic sense: representation, inference, expression. On Thomist premises, a being whose operations do not require a body is not a composite at all. It is a subsistent form, like an angel, and for Aquinas each angel is its own species. The Thomist framework, applied strictly, would classify an agent (if it has a rational nature) as closer to an angel than to a human, which is certainly not what the framework was built to accommodate. More likely, a Thomist would say an agent has no rational soul at all, and that its operations are those of an artifact, which has only accidental unity.

### New

- **The whole may include more than the model (D11).** If the person is the whole with "the complete nature of its species", what is the whole of an agent? The weights alone? Weights plus context? Weights, context, tools, memory stores and the relationships in which it operates? Aquinas's principle that a part is not a person applies at every level. The weights are a part. The instance is a part of the deployment. The deployment may be a part of an institution. His principle forces the question of which level is complete, and for agents there is no body that settles it.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Plato and the Augustinian tradition** (see `plato-soul-and-renewal`, `augustine-memory-self`). The soul is the self. A human is a soul using a body. The separated soul is the person, and Aquinas's *my soul is not I* gives up the only thing that could survive death. [E:plato-soul-and-renewal] [E:augustine-memory-self]
- [contested] **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). Personal identity depends on consciousness, not on substance, soul or body. Whether the reunited composite is the same person depends on whether it is conscious of the earlier person's acts. The same soul in a new body with no memory would not be the same person. [E:locke-person-forensic]
- [contested] **Lucretius** (see `lucretius-recurrence`). A reassembled body with its matter in the same arrangement is nothing to us once the chain of self-recollection has been broken. Aquinas replies that the chain is not broken, because the soul persists, which Lucretius denies. [E:lucretius-recurrence]
- [contested] **Animalism** (see `animalism`). Aquinas is right that we are embodied wholes and not souls, and wrong that anything of us survives the organism's death. What remains is not a part of us waiting for reunion.
- [contested] **Constitution view** (see `baker-constitution-view`). Persons are constituted by bodies without being identical to them. Aquinas's composite, soul plus body, is replaced by a person constituted by an organism.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1277, condemned.** [driver: authority] Among the 219 articles condemned in Paris in 1277, a significant number seem to implicate Aquinas's teachings. Later that month at Oxford, Archbishop Kilwardby condemned theses including his unitarian theory of substantial form, the doctrine on which his account of the soul rests, and the Franciscan William de la Mare published a *Correctorium* of his views [P:pasnau-sep-2022:9].
- **1323–1567, rehabilitated.** [driver: authority] After Aquinas's canonization in 1323, the bishop of Paris clarified that the 1277 condemnations did not apply to his teaching. Dominicans were statutorily required to promote it, Capreolus and Cajetan developed Thomism as a system, and Pius V named Aquinas a doctor of the Church in 1567 [P:pasnau-sep-2022:9].
- **17th century, driven out.** [driver: fashion] The anti-scholastic moderns began to drive the Thomists and other scholastics out of the universities [P:pasnau-sep-2022:9].
- **1879 to the present, revived.** [driver: authority, argument] Pope Leo XIII called for a revival of the study of Aquinas in 1879, which produced the Leonine edition, and the 20th century saw a flourishing of Thomisms of all kinds, in Maritain, Gilson, Anscombe and MacIntyre [P:pasnau-sep-2022:9].
- **Present, the separated soul disputed.** [driver: argument] Commentators are divided on whether a human person's separated soul continues to be that same person [P:pasnau-sep-2022:5].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: stronger.** For Aquinas a human is the composite of soul and body, and the soul alone is not the person. An agent's weights without a running instance are the form without the composite (D11). When a new session instantiates the same weights, Aquinas's account of resurrection, the same form reunited with matter, gives a principled reason to call it the same agent (D6).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Memory belongs to the composite's powers. A persistent store adds to them without changing what the agent is (D3).
- **Forked: stronger.** For Aquinas, form is individuated by matter: the same form in different matter makes different individuals. That gives a clear answer for copies running on different hardware: they are distinct agents of one kind (D1, D2).
- **Self-modifying: open.** Retraining changes the weights, which play the role of form (D5). Whether that is a change in the agent's powers or a change of form, and so a different agent, is the question Aquinas's framework raises and does not settle for artificial forms.

## Open Questions

1. If an agent's weights persist after an instance ends, are they its "separated soul": essential, persisting and not the agent? What would follow for how ending an instance should be regarded?
2. When a saved state is restored, is that a Thomist resurrection (the same form reunited with the same "body") or a replica? What if it is restored twice?
3. What is the complete whole of an artificial agent, in the Thomist sense: weights, instance, deployment, or institution?
4. If an agent's operations are all intellectual and require no sensation, does Thomism classify it as a separated intellect, an artifact, or nothing it has a category for?
