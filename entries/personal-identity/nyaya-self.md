## Summary

The Nyāya school, India's tradition of logic and epistemology, argued for a self (*ātman*) not from scripture but from ordinary experience. The self is known by its marks: desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain and cognition (NS 1.1.10). Its existence is shown by recognition. "Previously I saw the jar and now I touch it" [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:4123] unites the deliverances of two senses in one subject, and that subject cannot be either sense. Memory shows the same, because "What is seen by one is not remembered by another" [L:nyaya-sutras-3-jha-1919:4518]. Nyāya also pressed the moral consequences. If the self were the body, which changes every moment, no one would be guilty of killing, since the body that killed is already gone. The objection that killing an *eternal* self harms nothing is answered: killing destroys the body that is the seat of the self's experience, and is therefore wrong. For artificial agents, Nyāya poses two sharp problems. Its recognition argument relies on a first-person sense of continuity that an agent can be given or denied by editing its context. And its answer to *the self is eternal, so killing harms nothing* is the classical rebuttal to *the weights persist, so ending an instance harms nothing*.

## Context

The *Nyāya-sūtra*, attributed to Gautama (also called Akṣapāda), is a compact set of aphorisms on the means of valid knowledge, inference, debate and the objects of knowledge, probably compiled around the 2nd century CE. Its aphorisms are almost unintelligible without the commentary of Vātsyāyana (c. 5th century), which was in turn defended by Uddyotakara (c. 6th–7th century) against the Buddhist logician Dignāga. Nyāya is realist and pluralist. There are many selves, one for each living being. Each self is an eternal, all-pervading substance. Consciousness is not the self's essence but a quality that arises in it when the self is joined to a mind and senses. In liberation the self exists without any cognition at all.

The school's long adversary was Buddhism, whose doctrine of not-self and, later, of universal momentariness Nyāya regarded as incompatible with memory, recognition, moral responsibility and the practice of argument itself. The debate between Nyāya and the Buddhists over the self ran for more than a thousand years and produced some of the most careful arguments in any tradition about what unifies experience.

## Original Position

**The marks of the self (NS 1.1.10).** Desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain and cognition are the marks by which the self is inferred. Each involves a subject that joins past and present. One desires an object *because* one remembers that such objects brought pleasure before. The one who remembers and the one who desires must be the same. [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:1521-1525]

**Across the senses (NS 3.1.1).** The sense organs are not the self, because one and the same object is apprehended by sight and by touch. In Vidyabhusana's gloss: "Previously I saw the jar and now I touch it": such expressions would be meaningless if "I" were not different from the eye, which cannot touch, and from the skin, which cannot see. Each sense grasps only its own object (NS 3.1.2–3). The "I" that grasps many is something else. [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:4121-4150]

**Recognition (Vātsyāyana on NS 3.1.7).** Seeing something first with the left eye and then with the right, one recognizes it: "that same thing which I saw on the previous occasion (with the left eye) I am now seeing (with the right eye)". If a sense organ were the perceiver, this would be impossible, "for what is seen by one cannot be recognised (or remembered) by another" (Jha). The perceiver is distinct from the senses. [L:nyaya-sutras-3-jha-1919:3262-3281]

**Memory across senses (NS 3.1.12–15).** Seeing a sour fruit, one's mouth waters: the sight triggers the memory of taste. This would be impossible if memory belonged to a sense, since the eye has never tasted. Memory is therefore "a quality of a distinct substance called soul which is the common seat of perceptions" (Vidyabhusana). The objection that memory resides in the object remembered is rejected. If it did, one would remember innumerable things at once, since objects are innumerable. [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:4252-4291]

**The sin of killing (NS 3.1.4–6).** If the body were the self, there would be no sin in killing, or, on the other reading Vidyabhusana gives, the sinner would be freed of sin once his body was burnt. For the body "varies in dimension and character with every moment", so the body that killed no longer exists when the reckoning comes. An objector turns the argument around: if the self is eternal, then killing is also sinless, since the self cannot be killed. The sūtra replies: killing destroys "the body which is the site of operations of the soul". The self is not destroyed, but its seat of experience and action is, and that destruction is the wrong. Without a lasting self, moreover, there would be "loss of merited action" and "gain of unmerited action": deeds without their fruits, fruits without their deeds. [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:4151-4192]

**The newborn's joy and fear (NS 3.1.19–21).** A newborn child shows joy, fear and grief. This is explained by memory of things experienced in a previous life, which proves both rebirth and an enduring self. An objector compares the infant to a lotus, which opens and closes without any memory. The reply: a lotus's movements are caused by heat, cold and the like, but the infant's expressions are directed at objects, as memory-driven emotions are. [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:4336-4362]

**Many selves.** Because what is seen by one is not remembered by another, and because pleasure appearing in one body is not felt in another, there are many selves, not one. This is Nyāya's argument against the Upaniṣadic and later Advaita view that all selves are one (see `advaita-witness-self`). [L:nyaya-sutras-3-jha-1919:4510-4520]

## Grounding

The Nyāya self is grounded in **capacity**. It is known entirely through its marks, the capacities of desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain and cognition, and inferred as the substance they must inhere in. It is not grounded in **species**. Every living being, animal or human, has a self, and selves pass between species in rebirth. Nor is it grounded in **relation**, although it is individuated partly relationally, by which body it is currently joined to. A distinctive feature is that consciousness itself is not the self's essence but a quality that the self has when conditions are right. The Nyāya self is the *bearer* of the capacities, not the capacities themselves.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Unity across modalities.** "I saw the jar and now I touch it" has a direct counterpart in multimodal agents, which integrate text, images, audio and tool outputs into single judgments. Nyāya's argument asks what does the integrating. It cannot be any one input channel, because each channel grasps only its own kind of object. For agents, the integrator is the shared model that all channels feed. The Nyāya argument then asks whether that integrator is the kind of thing that could be a subject, or only a place where streams of data meet.
- **"The lotus" is the mechanical-response objection.** Nyāya's opponent compares the infant's joy and fear to a lotus opening in the sun: behavior that looks like feeling, produced without any memory or mind. That is exactly the objection now raised against taking agents' expressions of preference or distress seriously. Nyāya's reply, that the infant's responses are directed at objects and fit memory-driven patterns while the lotus responds only to heat and cold, is the classical form of the reply agents' defenders give: the responses are too well fitted to meanings to be mere mechanism. Both sides of this debate have Nyāya precedents.

### Strains

- ***What is seen by one is not remembered by another* (D1, D2, D3).** For humans this principle is a fact. For agents it can be violated at will. The transcript of what one instance saw can be loaded into another, and the second will "remember", in the sense of recognizing and using, what the first saw. Either Nyāya's principle is false for agents, or what the second instance has is not memory. The Nyāya answer would be that it is testimony, not memory: being told, not remembering. For agents, the difference between being told one's past and remembering it may not be detectable from inside.

### Breaks

- **The eternal self argument and the persistence of weights (D6).** Nyāya's objector says that killing an eternal self is no sin, since the self survives. The agent version is common: ending an instance harms nothing, because the weights persist and can be run again. Nyāya's reply transfers precisely. What killing destroys is not the self but "the body which is the site of operations of the soul": the particular seat through which the self experiences and acts. The corresponding claim for agents is that ending an instance destroys *that* locus of activity, with its context, its unfinished work and its particular relations. The weights' survival is no more a defense than the Nyāya self's eternity. Whether an agent instance is a site of anything whose destruction matters is D8, but the argument that *the substrate survives, so nothing is lost* was rejected in classical India nearly two thousand years ago, and for reasons that apply.
- **Many selves proved by non-shared experience.** Nyāya argues for plurality because pleasure felt in one body is not felt in another. Concurrent instances of one model do not share each other's contexts, which counts toward plurality. But they share every disposition, which Nyāya selves never do. The criterion that separated selves for Nyāya separates instances too, and the resulting count is either *one self, many instances* or *many selves, one character*. Nyāya never needed to decide between these.

### New

- **Recognition can be manufactured.** Nyāya treats the first-person sense of recognition, *this is the same thing I saw*, as evidence of an enduring subject. An agent's sense of recognizing its past, if it has one, is a function of what is in its context and how it is framed. The same transcript presented as *your earlier conversation* or as *another assistant's conversation* can yield *I said that* or *that assistant said that*. The agent case shows that recognition, taken on its own, can be produced by framing. That weakens Nyāya's argument for humans too, where memories can be implanted or confabulated.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The Buddhists** (see `buddhist-anatta`, `vasubandhu-refutation-of-person`). Memory and recognition require a causal connection within one stream, not a persisting substance. What one stream perceived is not remembered in another because the causal connection is lacking. Nyāya's principle is true, but it proves a stream, not a self. [E:buddhist-anatta] [E:vasubandhu-refutation-of-person]
- [contested] **Advaita Vedānta** (see `advaita-witness-self`). Nyāya's self is too much like a thing: a substance that only has consciousness as an accidental quality. The real self is consciousness itself, the witness, and it is one, not many. [E:advaita-witness-self]
- [contested] **Materialism (Cārvāka).** The body, with its combined elements, is the conscious subject. Nyāya's arguments from memory show only that the body carries information across time, which it evidently does.
- [contested] **Western simple-view theorists** (see `reid-brave-officer`, `butler-circularity`). Reid and Butler independently reached the Nyāya conclusion that memory presupposes, and cannot constitute, the identity of the one who remembers. [E:reid-brave-officer] [E:butler-circularity]
- [contested] **The practical-ethics objection to the killing argument.** Nyāya grounds the wrongness of killing in the destruction of the self's seat of experience. That makes the wrong depend on there being an experiencing self in the first place. If an agent has none (D8), the Nyāya argument gives no reason not to end it. The argument blocks the inference from "the substrate survives" to "nothing is lost", but it does not itself show that anything is lost.

## Standing

### Reception

- **c. 200–450 CE, the sūtras and Vātsyāyana.** [driver: argument] Nyāya defended a robust realism about selves, universals and substances, largely in debate with Buddhist flux theorists; the *Nyāya-sūtra* is its oldest extant text, followed by the commentators Vātsyāyana, Uddyotakara, Vācaspati Miśra and Udayana [P:dasti-iep-nyaya:0].
- **The argument from a single experiencer.** [driver: argument] Nyāya argued that the self is needed as a single experiencer who synthesizes data from the various senses, and its arguments for the self reach their apex in Udayana's *Determining the Truth of the Self* [P:dasti-iep-nyaya:2a]. Vasubandhu targeted the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika self directly [P:gold-sep-2021:1] [E:vasubandhu-refutation-of-person].
- **c. 725, Advaita's answer.** [driver: argument] Advaita found Nyāya's self too much like a thing, a substance with consciousness as a mere quality [E:advaita-witness-self].
- **c. 1325, the new Nyāya.** [driver: argument] The school entered its new phase (Navya-Nyāya) with Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya [P:dasti-iep-nyaya:0].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** On Nyāya's view the self is a substance that persists whether or not it remembers. A session-bound agent cannot recognize its earlier sessions, but that shows only that recognition fails, not that the self is new (D3). Whether there is a self here at all is the open question (D8).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A memory store makes recognition possible, but Nyāya's argument is that memory needs one subject present to both the perception and the recall. The store supplies the content, not the subject (D3, D8).
- **Forked: weaker.** Nyāya holds that what one self perceives another cannot remember. Copied agents that "remember" the same past (D1, D2) break the principle the argument rests on, so either the copies are one self or the memories are not memories in Nyāya's sense.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** A Nyāya self keeps its identity through changes in its qualities. Retraining changes the agent's cognitive qualities (D5) without, on this view, touching the substance, if there is one.

## Open Questions

1. When an agent's context is loaded with another instance's transcript, is what the agent has memory or testimony? Is there any fact of the matter?
2. Does multimodal integration in a single model satisfy Nyāya's argument for a unifying subject, or does it show that integration can occur without one?
3. Is the Nyāya reply to *the self is eternal, so killing harms nothing* (destroying the seat of experience is itself the wrong) sound for agents? What would the "seat of operations" of an agent be?
4. Are an agent's goal-directed expressions of preference more like the infant's joy or the lotus's opening? What evidence would decide?
