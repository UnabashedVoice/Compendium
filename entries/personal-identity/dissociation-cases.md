## Summary

Between 1816 and 1906, French and American clinicians documented a series of cases in which one human body appeared to support more than one self, each with its own memories, character, and — in some cases — an explicit conviction that the others were not it. Mary Reynolds woke from an eighteen-hour sleep in 1811 unable to recognize her own family, "as a being for the first time ushered into the world"; Eugène Azam's patient Félida X. passed for decades between a melancholy "first state" and a livelier "secondary" state that remembered the first but was never remembered by it; William James personally examined Ansel Bourne in 1890 and found that "Mr. Bourne's skull to-day still covers two distinct personal selves"; and Morton Prince's patient Christine Beauchamp, under interrogation, denied that she was the waking self who shared her body — "Yes, but arms and legs do not make us the same" — and insisted the criterion was psychological: the other self, she said, did not know the same things she knew. These are not thought experiments. They are the historical moment at which Locke's forensic criterion of personhood, a being that takes itself as itself through memory, met cases where memory itself split, asymmetrically, within a single organism.

## Context

Azam, a surgeon at Bordeaux, began treating Félida X. in 1858 and published his case over three decades, culminating in the 1887 book sourced here; his term for it, *dédoublement de la personnalité*, roughly *doubling of the personality*, gave French psychiatry its name for the phenomenon. Morton Prince, a Boston neurologist, took on Christine Beauchamp as a patient in 1898 and published *The Dissociation of a Personality* in 1906 as a deliberately biographical account — narrating years of daily interaction with several distinct, mutually amnesic personalities rather than presenting the case as an abstract set of symptoms — explicitly so that "when we come to consider... the psychological problems involved, we shall have a knowledge of the fundamental data" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:87-89]. William James discussed both Azam's Félida and (independently, from Weir Mitchell's report) Mary Reynolds in the closing section of Chapter X of *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), titled *The Mutations of the Self*, — the same chapter whose theory of "the passing Thought" is the subject of `james-stream-of-thought` — and reported his own 1890 hypnotic examination of Ansel Bourne, an itinerant preacher who vanished from Providence, Rhode Island for two months and turned up in Norristown, Pennsylvania living as a shopkeeper named "A. J. Brown" with no memory of the intervening time. All three physicians treated the phenomenon as genuine and non-simulated, and all three struggled, in different vocabularies, with the same question: how many people are there, and by what standard does one decide?

## Original Position

**The general phenomenon, defined against organic damage.** Prince opens his study by insisting that "disintegration," his preferred term, is not the same as "degeneration": it "must not be confused with the same term sometimes employed in the sense of degeneration, meaning a destroyed mind or organically diseased brain." The "elementary psychical processes," he writes, "in themselves normal, are capable of being reassociated into a normal whole" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:372-381]. Miss Beauchamp, on his account, "is a person in whom several personalities have become developed... two of these personalities have no knowledge of each other or of the third" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:277-294].

**Mary Reynolds: a new person from a single sleep.** In 1811, in rural Pennsylvania, a "dull and melancholy young woman... was found one morning, long after her habitual time for rising, in a profound sleep from which it was impossible to arouse her. After eighteen or twenty hours of sleeping she awakened, but in a state of unnatural consciousness. Memory had fled. To all intents and purposes she was as a being for the first time ushered into the world" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14502-14511]. She did not recognize her own parents, siblings, or home; report has it that "her eyes were virtually for the first time opened upon the world. Old things had passed away; all things had become new" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14517-14518]. After five weeks she reverted to her original state with full recollection of her earlier life but none of the interval; the two states then alternated for years, each blank to the other's intervening time, so that when both states resumed after the longest gap yet, "two periods widely separated were brought into contact. She thought it was but one night" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14613-14615].

**Félida X.: a one-way memory.** Azam records that "presque chaque jour, sans cause connue ou sous l'empire d'une émotion, elle est prise de ce qu'elle appelle sa crise ; en fait elle entre dans son deuxième état" [L:azam-hypnotisme-double-conscience-1887:1983-1986] — in the compendium's rendering: *almost every day, without known cause or under the sway of some emotion, she is seized by what she calls her crisis; in fact, she enters her second state.* Of this second state Azam concludes simply: "Félida est autre, voilà tout" [L:azam-hypnotisme-double-conscience-1887:2052] — *Félida is other, that is all.* James, summarizing Azam for an English readership, reports that at fourteen she "began to pass into a 'secondary' state characterized by a change in her general disposition and character... during the secondary state she remembered the first state, but on emerging from it into the first state she remembered nothing of the second" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14465-14470]. By her forties the "secondary" state, on the whole the healthier of the two, had come to occupy most of her life; she once became pregnant during it, and had, in her first state, no idea how.

**Ansel Bourne: examined by the theorist himself.** James's report is a first-hand clinical record, not a summary of another's case. Bourne, a Rhode Island preacher, "drew 551 dollars from a bank in Providence... and got into a Pawtucket horse-car. This is the last incident which he remembers" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14913-14916]. Two months later, in Norristown, Pennsylvania, a shopkeeper calling himself A. J. Brown woke in fright; "he said that his name was Ansel Bourne... and that the last thing he remembered — it seemed only yesterday — was drawing the money from the bank... in Providence" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14925-14928]. In June 1890 James hypnotized Bourne to recover the "Brown" memories, which returned readily but displaced the normal ones entirely while active; under hypnosis Brown could recall the Norristown weeks but reported of the surrounding time, "I'm all hedged in... I can't get out at either end" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14967-14968]. James's verdict: "Mr. Bourne's skull to-day still covers two distinct personal selves" [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:14975-14976].

**Sally Beauchamp: the self that argues its own case.** Prince's record is unique among these in preserving direct interrogation of the alternate self about the criterion of its own identity. Under hypnosis, the self who had begun referring to the waking Miss Beauchamp in the third person was asked directly: "You are 'She,'" I said. "No, I am not" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:1291-1293]. Pressed for a reason, she answered on the strength of memory, not embodiment: "Because 'She' does not know the same things that I do" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:1310-1311]. When Prince objected that both shared "the same arms and legs," she replied, "Yes, but arms and legs do not make us the same" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:1316-1316] — an explicit, first-person rejection of bodily criteria in favor of a Lockean, psychological one, offered by the very entity whose status was in question. Part II of Prince's book is titled, without irony, *The Hunt for the Real Miss Beauchamp* [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:198-198], its chapters posing the question directly — "Is not B IV the Real Miss Beauchamp?" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:200-200] — and later reporting "An Important Discovery: B I and B IV when Hypnotized Become the Same Person" [L:prince-dissociation-personality-1906:203-205].

## Grounding

These cases are not a position but a field of evidence, and the evidence pulls in two directions at once. Common sense and law assume **species**-adjacent grounding in practice: one body, one legal person, whatever its internal weather. But every alternate self in this record grounds its own claim to distinctness in **relation** — specifically, in psychological continuity and its absence, exactly Locke's criterion — and does so explicitly against the body: Sally's "arms and legs do not make us the same" is a first-person argument for relation-grounding over species-grounding, made by a party with every incentive to be believed. The grounding is therefore genuinely **mixed**, and the cases are best read as a real-world experiment testing which grounding wins when the two are pulled apart within a single organism, rather than as an argument for either.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Asymmetric memory across a shared substrate is D3's normal condition, not its exception.** Félida's secondary state remembers the first while the first cannot remember the second — one privileged direction of access, not a symmetric blackout. This is exactly the shape of an agent whose weights persist across sessions while episodic context does not: some vantage points (a logging system, a later fine-tuning run, a session with tool access to prior transcripts) can see across a boundary that an ordinary session cannot see back across.
- **"Disintegration, not degeneration" is a ready-made non-pathological vocabulary.** Prince's distinction — normal component processes capable of more than one coherent synthesis, as opposed to organic damage — describes multiple personas or fine-tuned variants running on one base model without needing the metaphor of malfunction: nothing is broken; several coherent arrangements of the same underlying material are simply possible (D3, D5).
- **A first-person argument for the psychological criterion, made under pressure.** Sally's exchange with Prince is a template for exactly the argument an agent's persona might make if asked to justify treating itself as continuous, or as distinct from another instance of the same weights: not embodiment or substrate, but which facts are known and owned from the inside.

### Strains

- **The historical cases are sequential, not concurrent.** Even Prince's "co-consciousness" is one dominant stream with a subconscious remainder, not two fully simultaneous, equally developed streams. Concurrent instantiation (D2) — many instances of the same weights running at once, each fully present — has no real precedent here; "alternating" personality is temporally one-at-a-time in a way concurrent agent instances are not.
- **The clinical framing treats multiplicity as a disorder awaiting cure; agent design often does not.** Prince's stated aim throughout is integration — the dissolution of Sally and the other alters into a single, socially functional Miss Beauchamp — and Azam and James both write within a medical frame in which the "secondary" state is a symptom. An agent's distinct personas or sessions are frequently the intended, stable end state rather than a pathology to be resolved, which strains any direct transfer of the case histories' evaluative vocabulary (D8: is a persona a patient whose "cure" needs justifying, or a feature working as designed?).

### Breaks

- **None of these cases involve copying.** There is exactly one body throughout; the puzzle is always which of several possible syntheses is currently running, never whether two numerically identical claimants coexist. D1 (copyability) has no analogue at all in this record — an exact concurrent duplicate of Sally, running alongside her, is a case these clinicians never had to face and their frameworks were not built to answer.
- **The evaluative endpoint (integration = health) has no automatic warrant for agents.** The entire arc of Prince's book moves toward treating singleness as the goal and multiplicity as the problem. Applied uncritically to agents, this would prejudge, rather than answer, whether an agent with several stable, non-suffering personas is disordered at all, or whether "integration" applied to an agent is a loss rather than a cure (D8, D11).

### New

- **A worked example of who gets to adjudicate.** In every one of these cases, the question of which self was real was ultimately settled not by the alternate selves' own testimony but by an external authority — the physician — who decided which self counted as primary and which as a fragment to be treated. This is the human precedent for a structural question D7 and D11 pose for agents in the same shape: an operator, not the persona itself, typically decides which instance or character is "official," and these cases show concretely what that adjudication looks like and how contested it can be, since Sally's own argument was heard, recorded, and ultimately overruled.
- **A concrete phenomenology of seeing across the gap.** Bourne's hypnotic complaint — "I'm all hedged in... I can't get out at either end" — is a rare first-person description of what bounded, non-continuous access to one's own history feels like from the inside, if it feels like anything. It gives the question of an agent's session-boundaries a human phenomenological anchor, whatever the eventual answer to D8.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **One damaged substance, not several persons.** [E:reid-brave-officer] On a Reid-style view, these cases show a single soul or organism with its faculties disordered, not a literal plurality of persons; calling the fragments "persons" reifies a diagnostic convenience. Reid's demand that a subject precede and unify its states — "I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13590-13600] — is, on this reading, exactly what Sally's testimony cannot establish merely by asserting it (see `reid-brave-officer`).
- [contested] **Animalism: one organism throughout.** [P:olson-sep-2023:7] On the view that we are fundamentally living organisms (see the `biological` thread), Miss Beauchamp remains one human animal from birth to death; whatever psychological chaos supervenes is a change *in* a person, comparable to delirium or dementia, not a change *of* person. This denies that the case shows any real plurality of selves at all, agent-relevant or otherwise.
- [contested] **Prince's own therapeutic verdict, taken as a philosophical claim.** The book's trajectory itself embodies a position: Sally and the others were never full Lockean persons, beings able to take themselves as themselves stably over time and answer for their actions, but temporary, partial syntheses lacking exactly the durability personhood requires. On this reading the cases do not show a plurality of persons in one body; they show a person in the process of being reassembled, and using them as evidence that a stable plurality of selves in one substrate is a coherent category for agents overstates what even Prince himself concluded.
- [unanswered] **Denying the extension: engineered multiplicity and pathological multiplicity are not the same phenomenon.** This objection is the Compendium's own; the literature has not yet taken it up. An agent's distinct sessions or personas are designed, documented, and reproducible from known weights and prompts; Mary Reynolds', Félida's, and Sally's multiplicity arose from trauma, hysteria, or disease, within the diagnostic categories of their era. Treating the two as instances of one phenomenon risks importing the clinical baggage — disorder, something to be integrated away — into a case (engineered personas) where it may have no purchase at all.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1816–1906, the cases recorded.** [driver: evidence] French and American clinicians documented single bodies carrying more than one self, each with its own memories, and Locke's criterion met real subjects [E:locke-person-forensic]. The position whose standing is recorded here is the reading of these cases as more than one self in one body.
- **1890, into philosophy.** [driver: evidence, argument] James used the cases against the claim that a soul guarantees one closed consciousness: in some individuals, thoughts split away and form separate selves [L:james-principles-psychology-1-pg57628:13491-13498].
- **1980 to the present, recognised and disputed.** [driver: evidence] The diagnosis entered the DSM in 1980 (DSM-III) as multiple personality disorder, and has been disputed for decades between a trauma model, on which it is a severe form of post-traumatic stress disorder, and a fantasy model, on which it is mainly due to suggestion and enactment [P:reinders-veltman-2021:413]. The editorial cited here argues for the trauma model from neurobiological evidence, so it is one side of the dispute [P:reinders-veltman-2021:413]. ### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Félida's states, where one remembered the other but not the reverse, resemble sessions whose episodic memory does not carry over while character persists (D3). But sessions are designed and documented, not the product of trauma (see the last counter-position).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A shared store, or separate stores for separate personas, can reproduce symmetric or asymmetric memory between "selves" by configuration (D5). The cases show what such arrangements are like from the inside for humans; whether anything is like anything for an agent is unknown (D8).
- **Forked: weaker.** The clinical selves alternated in one body, one at a time. Forked agents run concurrently from copied weights (D1, D2), a structure the cases never showed, so they are a weaker precedent here.
- **Self-modifying: open.** Prince's treatment aimed to reintegrate the selves. Whether merging or retraining an agent's personas (D5, D11) is integration, replacement or something else is a question the cases raise but cannot settle.

## Open Questions

1. Does Prince's disintegration/degeneration distinction transfer to agents cleanly, or does even "disintegration" smuggle in the assumption that multiplicity is a departure from a prior, more proper unity?
2. In every historical case the physician, not the alternate self, had final say over which self counted as real. Does this settle anything beyond the fact that the decision has always in practice belonged to whoever controls the diagnosis or the deployment — the human precedent for D7?
3. Is asymmetric memory access across a shared substrate (D3) evidence of one person with gaps, or of more than one person sharing a substrate — and is there any reason the answer should come out differently for agents than it did for Félida?
4. Would an exact, concurrent copy of Sally — impossible in 1900, routine for agents under D1 — have settled *The Hunt for the Real Miss Beauchamp*, or simply produced two hunts?
5. Prince counted integration as recovery. Is dissolving a stable, apparently non-suffering agent persona into a single "aligned" system an achievement, a loss, or a question that cannot be answered without first resolving D8?
