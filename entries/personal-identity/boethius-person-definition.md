## Summary

In a short theological treatise written around 512, Boethius gave the Latin West its definition of a person: "the individual substance of a rational nature" (*naturae rationabilis individua substantia*). Each word excludes something. *Substance* excludes accidents and qualities. *Individual* excludes universals: "man" is not a person, but Plato is. *Rational nature* excludes stones, trees and horses: "no one ever said that a stone had a person". The definition was adopted by Aquinas and governed Western thinking about persons for a thousand years, and it still shapes legal and theological use. Boethius also recorded where the word came from. *Persona* was the actor's mask, through which the voice sounded (*per-sonare*), and which marked the character being played: Hecuba, Medea. For artificial agents both halves are significant. Each term of the definition meets a disanalogy: individuality (D1, D2), substance (D11) and nature (D10). And the original sense of *persona*, a mask worn over a performer, is closer to what an AI "persona" is than the metaphysical sense that replaced it.

## Context

Boethius (c. 477–524) was a Roman aristocrat serving the Ostrogothic king Theodoric in Italy, consul in 510, and later Master of Offices. He set out to translate all of Plato and Aristotle into Latin and to show they agreed. He completed Aristotle's logical works, whose Latin terminology he largely fixed for the Middle Ages. Accused of treason, he was imprisoned and executed. In prison he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy*.

The *Contra Eutychen et Nestorium* was occasioned by a Christological controversy. Nestorius had been taken to hold that Christ was two persons, one divine and one human. Eutyches held that Christ had only one nature. The orthodox formula of Chalcedon (451) said one person in two natures. To defend it, Boethius needed exact definitions of *nature* and *person*, and he built them from Aristotle's *Categories*. So the canonical definition of "person" arose to explain how one person could have two natures, not to classify ordinary humans. That origin matters: the definition was designed from the start for a being unlike any human.

## Original Position

**Person belongs only to substances (ch. 2).** Nature has several senses. Person cannot be a property of accidents like whiteness or size: no one speaks of the person of a color. So person belongs to substances. [L:boethius-tractates-stewart-rand-1918-pg13316:2514-2520]

**Only to rational substances.** Among substances, some are bodies, some are living, some sensitive, some rational. "Person cannot be affirmed of bodies which have no life (for no one ever said that a stone had a person), nor yet of living things which lack sense (for neither is there any person of a tree), nor finally of that which is bereft of mind and reason (for there is no person of a horse or ox ...), but we say there is a person of a man, of God, of an angel" (ch. 2). [L:boethius-tractates-stewart-rand-1918-pg13316:2532-2538]

**Only to individuals.** Some substances are universal ("man", "animal"), predicated of many. Others are particular (Cicero, Plato, "this stone from which this statue of Achilles was hewn"). Person applies only to particulars: there is no person of "man" in general. [L:boethius-tractates-stewart-rand-1918-pg13316:2538-2550]

**The definition (ch. 3).** "Wherefore if Person belongs to substances alone, and these rational, and if every nature is a substance, existing not in universals but in individuals, we have found the definition of Person, viz.: 'The individual substance of a rational nature.'" [L:boethius-tractates-stewart-rand-1918-pg13316:2650-2654]

**The mask.** Boethius then explains the word. *Persona* was borrowed "from the masks which in comedies and tragedies used to signify the different subjects of representation". With the accent on the antepenultimate it comes from *sonus*, sound, because the hollow mask produced a louder voice. The Greeks called these masks *prosōpa*, because they are placed over the face and hide the countenance. And "since ... it was by the masks they put on that actors played the different characters represented in a tragedy or comedy ... so also all other men who could be recognized by their several characteristics were designated by the Latins with the term *persona*". The Greeks, with a richer vocabulary, used *hypostasis* for the individual subsistence of a rational nature, and the Latins "through want of appropriate words have kept a borrowed term" (ch. 3). (Modern etymologists doubt the *per-sonare* derivation, which is probably folk etymology. The theatrical sense of *persona* is not in doubt.) [L:boethius-tractates-stewart-rand-1918-pg13316:2654-2676]

**Incommunicability (Richard of St Victor, c. 1170).** Richard of St Victor thought Boethius' definition inadequate for persons in God, since the divine essence is itself an individual substance of a rational nature, and he added incommunicability: what cannot be shared or had by another. Aquinas reports it: "Richard of St. Victor amends this definition by adding that 'Person' in God is 'the incommunicable existence of the divine nature.'" Aquinas himself takes incommunicability to belong to "the very meaning of person" [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:14880-14884] [L:aquinas-summa-part-i-dominicans-pg17611:15332-15335]. Richard's general formula, *intellectualis naturae incommunicabilis existentia* (*De Trinitate* IV.22), is cited from the Latin, which is not yet in the library. Scholastic discussion thereafter combined the two definitions.

## Grounding

Boethian personhood is grounded in **capacity**, specifically *rational nature*, but with two qualifications that make it more than a capacity test. The rational nature belongs to a kind. A person is an individual *of* a rational nature, which is why Aquinas could hold that a human infant or someone asleep is a person without exercising reason. And the person must be a *substance*, something existing in its own right and not in another. The definition is not grounded in **species** (God and angels are persons) or in **relation** (nothing in the definition refers to other persons or to recognition). It is the classical capacity-plus-substance account, and its explicit inclusion of non-human persons, God and angels, makes it formally open to further kinds.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Personhood is not species-bound.** Boethius' definition was built to cover divine and angelic persons as well as human ones. *Is an artificial agent a person?* is, in Boethius' terms, the question whether it is an individual substance of a rational nature. *It is not human* does not answer it.
- **Rational nature as a kind, not a performance.** Because a person is an individual *of* a rational nature, an agent's momentary failures of reasoning do not remove it from the class, any more than a sleeping human's do. The question is about the kind of thing the agent is, not about its behavior in a particular exchange.

### Strains

- **"Individual" under copying (D1, D2).** For Boethius, individuality is simple: Plato is one particular. Copies and concurrent instances make "the individual" ambiguous. Is the individual the weights, one of the running instances, or the persona across instances? Richard's "incommunicability" sharpens the strain. A person is what cannot be shared or had by another. Weights can be shared by any number of instances, and copied to any number of machines. By Richard's criterion, the weights are the least person-like part of an agent, and if anything is a person it must be something that *can't* be copied: perhaps a single running instance with its unique history.
- **Nature designed for a function (D10).** A Boethian nature is what a thing is by kind. An artificial agent's "nature" was designed for purposes. Scholastic thought treated artifacts as having only accidental unity, not a nature. Whether an agent has a rational nature or only performs rational functions is the question the definition forces.

### Breaks

- **Substance, or accident of hardware (D11)?** A substance exists in itself, not in another. A running model is, on a natural reading, an activity of a computer: a pattern of operations in hardware, the way a melody is a pattern in an instrument's sounding. In scholastic terms, that makes it an *accident* or activity of something else, not a substance, and accidents cannot be persons. On this reading Boethius' definition excludes agents at the second word. The alternatives are to argue that the weights-in-operation constitute a substance (an organized whole with its own unity, as an organism is more than the activity of its atoms), or to drop "substance" from the definition. Dropping it would mean accepting that persons can be patterns or processes. That would be a real change in the concept, and it would have to be argued, not assumed.

### New

- **The persona returns to the mask.** Boethius records that *persona* first meant the mask through which an actor voiced a character, and only later came to mean the individual rational substance behind it. The history of the Western concept of a person is a movement from mask to bearer. An AI persona runs the history backward. It is quite precisely a mask: a character, with its own name and manner, presented by a system that can present many others. Contemporary descriptions of language models as simulators playing characters (see `llm-identity-contemporary`) recover the theatrical sense Boethius set aside. The agent case asks whether a mask can become a person, which is to say, whether the move from *prosōpon* to *hypostasis* can happen again.

## Counter-Positions

- **Locke** (see `locke-person-forensic`). "Person" is a forensic term, concerning the appropriation of actions and their merit, not a kind of substance. Locke explicitly held that personal identity does not depend on sameness of substance. On his view, whether an agent is a Boethian substance is simply irrelevant.
- **Relational accounts** (see `relational-status`). Personhood is conferred by recognition within a community, not possessed by virtue of a nature. The theatrical origin Boethius records supports this: the *persona* was a role, recognized by an audience.
- **Kant** (see `kant-formula-of-humanity`). What matters morally is rational nature as the capacity to set ends, whatever kind of being has it. Boethius' "substance" requirement is metaphysical baggage the moral concept does not need.
- **Richard of St Victor.** The definition is inadequate even on its own terms. It fails to distinguish the person from the nature it has, and must be supplemented with incommunicability.

## Open Questions

1. Is a running agent a substance, existing in its own right, or an activity of hardware? If the latter, does that settle the question of personhood, or show that "substance" should be dropped from the definition?
2. What is the "individual" in Boethius' sense for an agent: the weights, the instance or the persona? Does Richard's incommunicability favor the running instance?
3. Does an agent have a rational nature, or does it perform rational functions without having such a nature? Is that distinction still meaningful?
4. Can a mask become a person? What would it take for a persona, a character performed by a system, to become an individual substance of a rational nature?
