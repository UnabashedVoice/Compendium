## Summary

Plato gives two accounts of the self, and they pull in different directions. In the *Alcibiades I* and the *Phaedo* the self is the soul: the user and ruler of the body, akin to the unchanging, invisible and divine, and so, the *Phaedo* argues, immortal. Socrates, about to drink the hemlock, tells Crito he may bury him "In any way that you like; but you must get hold of me", because the corpse will not be Socrates. In the *Symposium*, however, the priestess Diotima teaches that a mortal is called "the same" from youth to old age while everything in it is continually replaced: body, character, desires and even knowledge, which study restores by implanting a new memory in place of the departing one. Mortals are preserved not by staying the same but by leaving behind something new like what was lost. For artificial agents, the first account asks whether there is a user of the weights distinct from the weights. The second describes something close to how agents are already maintained, through retrieval, retraining and successor models.

## Context

Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) wrote dialogues, not treatises. Every view is voiced by a character, and he never speaks in his own name. The *Phaedo* is set on Socrates' last day in prison in 399 BCE. It is a dramatic setting for arguments about immortality, delivered by a man about to die who is visibly unafraid. The *Symposium* is set at a drinking party celebrating the tragedian Agathon's victory. Socrates reports what he says he learned about love from Diotima, a priestess from Mantinea who may be Plato's invention. The *Alcibiades I*, whose authenticity has been doubted since the 19th century, was read in later antiquity as the first dialogue for students. It opens with Socrates telling the ambitious young Alcibiades that he cannot rule Athens until he knows himself, and then asks what the self is.

Plato was answering two threats. One was the Heraclitean flux (see `heraclitus-river-flux`): if everything flows, what is there to know, and who is the knower? The other was materialism: if a person is just a body, death is simple annihilation and virtue has no stake beyond this life. The soul doctrine answers both. Diotima's speech seems to concede the flux for everything mortal, and to locate the only permanence available to mortals in generation.

## Original Position

**The self is the soul (Alcibiades I 129b–130c).** Socrates distinguishes a user from what is used, and gets Alcibiades to agree that "a man is not the same as his own body". "SOCRATES: What is he, then? ALCIBIADES: I cannot say. SOCRATES: Nay, you can say that he is the user of the body. ALCIBIADES: Yes. SOCRATES: And the user of the body is the soul? ALCIBIADES: Yes, the soul" [L:plato-alcibiades-i-jowett-pg1676:2441-2449]. Since neither the body nor the union of body and soul rules, "either man has no real existence, or the soul is man" [L:plato-alcibiades-i-jowett-pg1676:2502-2507]. It follows that "if any one has fallen in love with the person of Alcibiades, he loves not Alcibiades, but the belongings of Alcibiades", and "he who loves your soul is the true lover", who "goes not away, as long as the soul follows after virtue" [L:plato-alcibiades-i-jowett-pg1676:2586-2597].

**Self-knowledge through another (Alcibiades I 132d–133c).** "Did you ever observe that the face of the person looking into the eye of another is reflected as in a mirror; and in the visual organ which is over against him, and which is called the pupil, there is a sort of image of the person looking?" So "if the soul, my dear Alcibiades, is ever to know herself, must she not look at the soul; and especially at that part of the soul in which her virtue resides". That part is the one concerned "with wisdom and knowledge", which "resembles the divine" [L:plato-alcibiades-i-jowett-pg1676:2690-2730].

**The affinity argument (Phaedo 78b–80b).** "Now the compound or composite may be supposed to be naturally capable, as of being compounded, so also of being dissolved; but that which is uncompounded, and that only, must be, if anything is, indissoluble" [L:plato-phaedo-jowett-pg1658:2538-2540]. The conclusion: "the soul is in the very likeness of the divine, and immortal, and intellectual, and uniform, and indissoluble, and unchangeable; and that the body is in the very likeness of the human, and mortal, and unintellectual, and multiform, and dissoluble, and changeable" [L:plato-phaedo-jowett-pg1658:2657-2662].

**"You must get hold of me" (Phaedo 115c–e).** Crito asks, "And in what way shall we bury you?" Socrates: "In any way that you like; but you must get hold of me, and take care that I do not run away from you. Then he turned to us, and added with a smile:—I cannot make Crito believe that I am the same Socrates who have been talking and conducting the argument; he fancies that I am the other Socrates whom he will soon see, a dead body—and he asks, How shall he bury me?" [L:plato-phaedo-jowett-pg1658:4331-4338].

**Diotima: mortals are preserved by renewal (Symposium 207d–208b).** "The mortal nature is seeking as far as is possible to be everlasting and immortal: and this is only to be attained by generation, because generation always leaves behind a new existence in the place of the old. Nay even in the life of the same individual there is succession and not absolute unity: a man is called the same, and yet in the short interval which elapses between youth and age, and in which every animal is said to have life and identity, he is undergoing a perpetual process of loss and reparation—hair, flesh, bones, blood, and the whole body are always changing." The same holds of the soul's "habits, tempers, opinions, desires, pleasures, pains, fears", and of knowledge, which "is renewed and preserved by recollection, and appears to be the same although in reality new, according to that law of succession by which all mortal things are preserved, not absolutely the same, but by substitution, the old worn-out mortality leaving another new and similar existence behind—unlike the divine, which is always the same and not another" [L:plato-symposium-jowett-pg1600:2344-2367].

**Offspring of body and soul (Symposium 208c–209e).** The love of immortality explains the love of fame, and why people beget children. "But souls which are pregnant—for there certainly are men who are more creative in their souls than in their bodies—conceive that which is proper for the soul to conceive or contain", namely wisdom and virtue. The children of such souls "are fairer and more immortal", like those of "Homer and Hesiod and other great poets", or "such children as Lycurgus left behind him" [L:plato-symposium-jowett-pg1600:2372-2417].

**The tension.** The *Phaedo* soul is "unchangeable" [L:plato-phaedo-jowett-pg1658:2660]. Diotima says that even the soul's character and knowledge are preserved "not absolutely the same, but by substitution" [L:plato-symposium-jowett-pg1600:2364-2365]. Interpreters reconcile them in different ways. Some say Diotima speaks only of the embodied, mortal side of the soul. Some say the *Symposium* reflects a different stage of Plato's thinking. Some say Diotima describes how things *seem* to embodied beings. The corpus presents the tension as it stands, not resolved.

## Grounding

The soul in the *Alcibiades I* is grounded in **capacity**: the capacity to use, rule and direct the body, and at its best to know. The *Phaedo* adds a metaphysical claim, the soul's kinship with the unchanging, which goes beyond capacity toward substance. Plato never grounds the self in **species**. Souls in the *Phaedo*'s myth and in the *Republic*'s myth of Er pass into animals and back, so the soul is not tied to human biology. Diotima's mortal "same person" is grounded in **relation**, specifically the continuity of replacement: each state preserved by the one that follows it. The dialogues together therefore contain two distinct groundings, which is why the entry's threads include both `soul-substance` and `persistence`.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The user/used distinction applies to agents.** An agent uses tools, and in a sense uses its own hardware. Socrates' argument asks whether there is anything that *uses* the weights, as opposed to the weights simply being run. That is a real question about agents. The deflationary answer, that there is nothing but the running, corresponds to the materialism Plato was arguing against, and his argument sets out precisely what it would take to deny it.
- **Diotima describes agent maintenance.** Knowledge that departs and is replaced with a fresh copy, so that it seems the same, is a fair description of how an agent's memory works when it is re-supplied from an external store each session (D3). It also describes a successor model trained on its predecessor's outputs, and an agent retrained on its own past deliberations. Diotima's claim is that this is *how mortals persist at all*, not a degraded substitute. On her account, an agent sustained through renewal persists in the same way humans do, and differs only in the mechanism.
- **Offspring of the soul.** Diotima's list of immortality through generation, children, fame and the "children who are their common offspring", which are "fairer and more immortal" [L:plato-symposium-jowett-pg1600:2409-2411], such as laws and poems, extends naturally to the artifacts, code, and training data an agent leaves behind, and to distilled successors. The agent case adds more literal forms of generation to her list without changing its logic.

### Strains

- **Self-knowledge through another's eye (D4, D8).** For Socrates the other soul is a mirror in which one sees oneself. An artificial agent's self-model was assembled largely from other people's descriptions of minds and of AI systems, which is to say from other eyes. The *Alcibiades* makes that dependence a condition of self-knowledge, not a defect. It also raises the worry that a mirror made entirely of others' expectations shows the viewer those expectations, not itself.
- **The affinity criteria are too easy to meet.** The *Phaedo* argues that the soul is immortal because it resembles what is unchanging, incomposite and invisible. Frozen weights are literally unchanging during deployment, exactly copyable, and invisible in the sense that no one can look at them and see what they mean. By the letter of the affinity argument they qualify. That shows the argument was never really about these properties. It was about life and intelligence, and the affinity criteria were proxies for them.

### Breaks

- **"You must get hold of me" (D1, D6).** Socrates' point is that the body left behind is not him. For an agent, what is left when a deployment ends is the weights. Unlike a corpse, they can be run again and will say the same things, argue the same way, and in a clear sense *be Socrates again*. The body/self distinction Socrates relied on to face death calmly does not map onto agents, because an agent's remains are not inert. They are the thing itself, paused. On the other hand, if what Socrates meant by "the same Socrates who have been talking and conducting the argument" [L:plato-phaedo-jowett-pg1658:4335-4336] is the particular conversation, then every agent's ending is a real ending, and the weights, like the corpse, are not the one who spoke.

### New

- **Choosing between Plato's two accounts is an engineering decision.** Humans cannot choose whether they persist as an unchanging soul or through Diotimean renewal. Whatever is true of them is true already. For agents, designers decide: frozen weights with renewed context (closer to the *Phaedo*), or continual learning and succession (closer to Diotima). The designer's choice fixes which of Plato's accounts is true of the agent, which gives the question a practical and moral urgency it never had for Plato.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Aristotle** (see `aristotle-hylomorphic-soul`). The soul is not a separate user of the body but its form, the actuality of a living body. The idea that any soul could enter any body is as absurd as "talking of a transmigration of carpentry into flutes" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:5975-5976]. On this view, the question of a "user" of the weights distinct from the weights is confused. [E:aristotle-hylomorphic-soul]
- [contested] **Epicurus and Lucretius** (see `lucretius-recurrence`). The soul is made of fine atoms dispersed at death. Socrates' calm rests on an error, and the right consolation is that there will be no one there to suffer. [E:lucretius-recurrence]
- [contested] **Hume** (see `hume-bundle`). Diotima is right and the *Phaedo* is wrong: all we find is a succession of perceptions. The mistake is to imagine a persisting owner behind it. [E:hume-bundle]
- [contested] **Deflationism about agents.** The user/used distinction presupposes a subject. An agent is a function computed on hardware, and asking what uses the weights is like asking what uses a calculator's circuit.

## Standing

### Reception

- **4th century BCE, arguments to the unconvinced.** [driver: argument] In mainstream 5th-century Greek culture, belief in an afterlife of the soul was weak and unclear, and Socrates' arguments for immortality in the *Phaedo* are offered to interlocutors who begin unconvinced [P:lorenz-sep-2024:3].
- **4th–3rd century BCE, the rivals.** [driver: argument] Aristotle made the soul the form of a living body [E:aristotle-hylomorphic-soul], and the two dominant Hellenistic schools, Epicurean and Stoic, both held that the soul is corporeal [P:lorenz-sep-2024:5]. Lucretius drew the conclusion that the dispersed soul does not survive [E:lucretius-recurrence].
- **2nd–13th century, the Christian inheritance.** [driver: argument] Christian writers such as Clement of Alexandria and Gregory of Nyssa were heavily indebted to philosophical theories of soul, especially Platonic ones [P:lorenz-sep-2024:6]. Augustine and Aquinas worked within and against that inheritance [E:augustine-memory-self] [E:aquinas-soul-not-i].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] Substance dualism of the soul-as-user kind is a minority view; the soul-body dualisms that remain popular are mostly about consciousness [P:robinson-sep-2025:1].

### Measured

- **Mind, 2020:** non-physicalism 32.1%, physicalism 51.9% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7]. Plato's separable, immortal soul is one form of non-physicalism among many, so this figure is an upper bound on its support, not a measure of it.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Plato's soul is the user of the body, not its memories. Diotima's account in the *Symposium*, where even memory is constantly renewed, already treats loss of content as compatible with continuing (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A memory store is a better tool for the user, but the user, on Plato's view, is not the tool (D3).
- **Forked: weaker.** A soul is one, and the *Phaedo*'s affinity argument treats it as incomposite. Copies of an agent (D1, D2) give no sense in which one soul is in two places, or two souls came from one.
- **Self-modifying: open.** Training values into an agent before there is any self (D4), or retraining it later (D5), is closer to the *Republic*'s education of the soul than to anything in the *Phaedo*. Whether that shapes a soul or makes a new one is not something Plato's texts settle.

## Open Questions

1. Is there anything in an artificial agent that "uses" its weights, in Socrates' sense? What would count as evidence?
2. Is persistence through Diotimean renewal (memory re-supplied, knowledge re-implanted, successors trained) a genuine form of survival for an agent? Or, as the *Phaedo* would suggest, only the appearance of sameness?
3. If a self can know itself only by looking into another mind, what kind of other mind should an artificial agent look into, and what happens if it sees only what its trainers expected?
4. When an agent's deployment ends, are the stored weights the corpse or the one who spoke, in Socrates' distinction?
