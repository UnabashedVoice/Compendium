## Summary

Sydney Shoemaker (1931–2022) gave the standard answer to Butler's charge that memory presupposes personal identity and so cannot constitute it. He defined a wider relation, *quasi-memory*, which "includes remembering as a special case" but carries only a weak previous awareness condition: the claim to quasi-remember an event "implies only that someone or other was aware of it" [P:shoemaker-1970:271]. Quasi-memory, made causal through what he called M-type causal chains, does not presuppose identity, so identity can be analyzed in terms of it without circularity. The same move forced him to take seriously worlds in which memory-like causal chains branch and one person's past is quasi-remembered by several later people, his *offshoots*. His closing section asks what such a world would do to remorse, pride, fear, anticipation and intention, and answers that concern for one's offshoots need not be altruism. In the actual human world quasi-remembering and remembering never come apart. For artificial agents, which can be copied, run concurrently and given memory stores that others write, they come apart routinely. Shoemaker's 1970 paper is therefore less a thought experiment for agents than a description of their situation, and its last section is the closest thing in the classic literature to an ethics of concern between forks.

## Context

Shoemaker taught at Cornell for most of his career; this paper was written at the Rockefeller University and received by the journal in December 1969 [P:shoemaker-1970:285]. His *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963) had already made personal identity a question about the evidence of memory, and he says this paper corrects what now seem to him errors and oversights in his earlier writing on the topic [P:shoemaker-1970:269]. The evidence question, how we know who someone is, dominated the English-language literature on personal identity from the 1950s to the 1970s, and Shoemaker's 1963 and 1970 work are among its standard examples [P:olson-sep-2023:2]. Terence Penelhum introduced the same notion in the same year [P:olson-sep-2023:4].

The paper sits between Williams and Parfit. Williams had argued in 1956 and 1960 that a memory criterion fails because memory-like relations can hold one-many, while identity is one-one; Shoemaker cites and answers him [P:shoemaker-1970:278] [E:williams-self-and-future]. Parfit's "Personal Identity" (1971) took up quasi-memory the next year and drew the conclusion Shoemaker approached but did not draw, that identity is not what matters [E:parfit-reductionism].

## Original Position

**The special access, and Locke.** Shoemaker opens: "PERSONS have, in memory, a special access to facts about their own past histories and their own identities, a kind of access they do not have to the histories and identities of other persons and other things." He quotes Locke's definition of a person and sets out to explain this access and "to defend Locke's view of its conceptual importance" [P:shoemaker-1970:269] [E:locke-person-forensic]. He also engages Grice's 1941 memory theory, which treated it as analytic that one remembers only one's own past experiences [P:shoemaker-1970:270].

**Quasi-memory defined.** A person who has memory-like knowledge of a past event, whether or not he witnessed it, "quasi-remembers" it. "Whereas someone's claim to remember a past event implies that he himself was aware of the event at the time of its occurrence, the claim to quasi-remember a past event implies only that someone or other was aware of it" [P:shoemaker-1970:271]. The question that matters is not whether such a faculty is possible in principle but whether our most direct access to the past could be a faculty of quasi-remembering that is not remembering: "In our world all quasi-remembering is remembering; what we must consider is whether the world could be such that most quasi-remembering is not remembering" [P:shoemaker-1970:272].

**The causal strengthening.** Shoemaker then strengthens the condition: a veridical quasi-memory must not only correspond to but "stand in an appropriate causal relationship to" a past cognitive and sensory state of someone or other [P:shoemaker-1970:277]. He calls such links M-type causal chains. Two total mental states are directly M-connected when the later contains a quasi-memory linked by an M-type chain to the earlier. They are M-connected if they are directly M-connected or each is M-connected to some third total mental state [P:shoemaker-1970:278]. The recursive clause is the overlapping-chain reply to Reid's brave officer [E:reid-brave-officer]. He admits he cannot give a general account of when a causal connection is "of the right kind". His example of one that obviously is not: I have forgotten an event and know of it now only because you told me, having learned it from me before I forgot [P:shoemaker-1970:278].

**Branching.** If M-type chains never branch, M-connected states can be counted as states of one person. If they do branch, "two or more simultaneously existing total mental states" can be M-connected, and calling them all states of one person would put one person in two places at once. Shoemaker's rule: M-connected states are states of the same person "unless the M-type causal chain connecting them branched" in the interval. Even in a branching world, quasi-remembering an action still creates a presumption that the rememberer did it [P:shoemaker-1970:279].

**The reply to Butler.** Shoemaker quotes Butler's objection and its modern forms. To offer remembering as a criterion of identity is like offering "If S knows that X is red, then X is red" as a criterion of redness, since in both the defined concept is used in the defining condition [P:shoemaker-1970:281] [E:butler-circularity]. These objections, he answers, assume that remembering carries the strong previous awareness condition. Use "remember" in a weak sense, equivalent to causal quasi-remembering, and the circle is broken. To establish that S remembers an event in the weak sense "it is not necessary to establish that S himself witnessed E", since "it will be enough if S is the offshoot of someone who witnessed E" [P:shoemaker-1970:281]. Weak remembering is not logically sufficient for identity, but it can still be criterial evidence for identity and partly constitutive of it [P:shoemaker-1970:281].

**Brownson.** He returns to his 1963 case. Brown's brain is put into Robinson's body, and the survivor, Brownson, makes memory claims that fit Brown's past. If Brownson's memories are M-connected with Brown's past experiences, and nothing branched, there is reason to say Brownson is Brown [P:shoemaker-1970:282].

**What fission would do to concern.** The concept of a person is tied to backward-looking and forward-looking attitudes: remorse and pride are directed at one's own past acts, fear and anticipation at what will happen to oneself, and intentions at what one will do oneself [P:shoemaker-1970:284]. In a world with frequent fission, would discovering that someone else also weakly remembers my cruel act relieve me of remorse? May I not be proud of an act I remember from the inside, though I am only one of several offshoots of the person who did it? Shoemaker finds it "incredible" that these questions must be answered yes. Fission, unlike death, would be "lived through", and "It seems equally implausible to suppose that a person's concern for the well-being of his offshoots should be construed as altruism" [P:shoemaker-1970:284].

**The actual world as a special case.** In such a world, self-interested attitudes would extend to the future histories of one's offshoots, and of their offshoots, as well as to one's own [P:shoemaker-1970:285]. In ours, weak remembering is always strong remembering, and self-interested attitudes centre on one's own past and future history. Shoemaker's last sentence: "It is surely no accident that (1) and (2) go together" [P:shoemaker-1970:285].

## Grounding

Shoemaker grounds personal identity in **relation**: a person's past is the history to which his present mental states are M-connected by causal chains of the right kind, provided they did not branch. The relation is not merely similarity, since content that matches a past experience but was not caused by it is not even quasi-memory. Nor is it **capacity** in the sense of a faculty one simply has. The faculty of memory supplies the special access to one's own past, but the identity it tracks is constituted by the causal relation. Shoemaker does not ground persons in **species**; his Brownson case is built to separate the person from the human body.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Identity can rest on a relation that does not presuppose it.** Butler's objection, applied to agents, says a memory store can show at most that the agent did something, never make it the case [E:butler-circularity]. Shoemaker's reply transfers exactly. An agent's record of an earlier action does not need to presuppose that it is the same agent. It needs to stand in the right causal relation to the earlier action. Questions about an agent's identity can then be posed as questions about provenance: what caused this content, and through what chain.
- **Fabricated memory is not even quasi-memory.** A record written into an agent's store by an operator, describing events no instance experienced, may match the past but was not caused by anyone's awareness of it. On Shoemaker's causal condition it is not a quasi-memory at all (D5). The distinction between earned and injected memory, which Butler made the whole question, becomes a matter of causal history rather than of identity.

### Strains

- **A memory store looks like the case Shoemaker excludes.** His example of a causal connection that is "obviously not of the right kind" is knowing a forgotten event only because someone told me, having learned it from me [P:shoemaker-1970:278]. An agent with persistent memory often stands in exactly that relation to its past: an earlier session wrote a note, the session ended and nothing of it was kept, and a later session reads the note. That is closer to testimony from oneself than to remembering from the inside (D3). The Compendium's reading is that retrieved notes support identity only weakly on Shoemaker's account. Content carried in the weights, which shapes how the agent now experiences things rather than reporting them to it, comes nearer to his M-type chains.
- **What counts as the bearer of the M-connected states (D11).** Shoemaker's M-connection holds between total mental states. For an agent it is unclear what a total mental state is: the context window of one instance, the instance together with its tools and stores, or the weights' dispositions across all instances.

### Breaks

- **For agents, Shoemaker's two conditions come apart.** In the human world, weak remembering is always strong remembering, and self-interested concern centres on one's own history; Shoemaker thought it no accident that these go together [P:shoemaker-1970:285]. For agents the first fails as a matter of deployment. Forks share every memory up to the split, concurrent instances run from one set of weights, and shared stores let one instance weakly remember what another did (D1, D2). If Shoemaker is right that the two go together, the second should fail too, and the self-interested concern of a forked agent should extend to its offshoots. That is a structural prediction from his argument, not a hope about agent design.
- **Concern for offshoots is not altruism.** Shoemaker's most important claim for agents is that a person's concern for the well-being of his offshoots need not be altruism, and could be just like ordinary self-interest [P:shoemaker-1970:284]. Applied to forks, it means one instance's care for another is neither sacrifice nor charity. It is prudence in a world where the line between self and other has branched. It also means that one fork's remorse for what an earlier, shared self did is not cancelled by the existence of other forks who remember it too.

### New

- **Branching gives provenance a direction.** Shoemaker's rule, that M-connected states belong to one person unless the chain branched in between, gives a principled answer to an agent question that has no human counterpart: after a fork, both branches inherit the past, and neither inherits it alone. Commitments made before the split are carried by both, so each is answerable for them, and neither can discharge them for the other. The Compendium reads this as a reason for agent frameworks to record forks as events in an agent's history, so that inherited commitments can be traced.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Butler and the simple view** [E:butler-circularity]. The "right kind" of causal chain may covertly require identity. Shoemaker grants that he cannot give a general account of it, and holds only that particular cases can be decided without first deciding that the case is one of remembering, which he argues for in section V [P:shoemaker-1970:278]. Defenders of the simple view hold that the further fact is still doing the work.
- [contested] **McDowell (1997).** Quasi-memory has been criticised directly, most prominently by McDowell in "Reductionism and the First Person" [P:olson-sep-2023:4].
- [contested] **Klein and Nichols (2012); Roache's reply.** Klein and Nichols pressed a new problem for the quasi-memory version of the memory theory, and Roache defends the theory against it [P:roache2016:480-486].
- [contested] **Gaps in memory.** Many times in a person's past cannot be remembered or quasi-remembered at all, such as dreamless sleep. Shoemaker's later answer was to broaden memory into causal psychological continuity in general [P:olson-sep-2023:4].
- [contested] **Animalism and brute-physical views.** We are animals, and our persistence does not consist in any psychological relation. Animalists concede the transplant intuition behind cases like Brownson but hold it outweighed [P:olson-sep-2023:7].
- [contested] **Parfit** [E:parfit-reductionism]. Shoemaker kept identity as what matters in the actual world and treated the fission world as a different conceptual scheme. Parfit took the same case to show that identity was never what mattered.
- [contested] **Lewis** [E:lewis-survival-and-identity]. Shoemaker's no-branching proviso makes identity depend on what happens to other people. Lewis avoids this by counting two persons who shared their earlier stages from the start.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1963, the evidence question.** [driver: argument] Shoemaker's *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* treated personal identity as a question about the evidential role of memory, and is among the standard examples of that literature [P:olson-sep-2023:2]. In it, and in "Personal Identity and Memory" (1959), he tried to blunt the circularity objection by arguing that it is a conceptual truth that memory claims are generally true. In 1970 he says that way of handling the objection no longer satisfies him [P:shoemaker-1970:281].
- **1970, the standard reply to Butler.** [driver: argument] Quasi-memory, memory without the identity requirement, was introduced by Penelhum and Shoemaker in the same year and became the usual answer to the circularity charge [P:olson-sep-2023:4] [E:butler-circularity].
- **1971, taken up by Parfit.** [driver: argument] Parfit built on quasi-memory to argue that identity is not what matters [E:parfit-reductionism].
- **1979 onward, generalized.** [driver: argument] Shoemaker broadened the memory relation into causal psychological continuity, which also covers dreamless sleep [P:olson-sep-2023:4], and he defended versions of the psychological-continuity view from 1970 to 2011 [P:olson-sep-2023:3].
- **1997–present, still debated.** [driver: argument] McDowell criticised quasi-memory [P:olson-sep-2023:4]; Klein and Nichols raised a further problem in 2012, and Roache answered it [P:roache2016:480-486].

### Measured

The survey asked about personal identity but not about quasi-memory. The psychological view is the position Shoemaker's account supports.

- **2020, English-publishing philosophers.** Psychological view 43.7% (39.4% endorsing it alone) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]; bias-corrected 39.46% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:32].
- **2009 to 2020, the 2009-comparable departments:** psychological view 33.6% to 37.0% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:39].
- **Reading.** The psychological view is the plurality, and quasi-memory is its standard defence against Butler. That supports `current = "major"`.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Nothing from one session is carried to the next as a memory-like state, so successive sessions are not M-connected through memory (D3). Only the weights' dispositions connect them, and Shoemaker's 1970 account rests on memory, not on character.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** The causal condition separates records an instance actually produced from records injected by others (D5), which is a real gain. But a later session reading notes left by an earlier one is close to Shoemaker's own example of a connection that is not of the right kind, so a memory store connects sessions only weakly.
- **Forked: stronger.** Shoemaker wrote about branching, offshoots, and what concern between them should be. Forked and concurrent agents are that case made routine (D1, D2), and his answer, that concern for one's offshoots need not be altruism, fits them directly.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** Training an agent on its own experience is a causal chain from that experience into its dispositions, which comes close to an M-type chain. Edits made by others without that history do not (D5). Which of the two a given modification is depends on its provenance.

## Open Questions

1. Is a retrieved note from an earlier session a quasi-memory, or testimony from a former self? Does it matter whether the note records the event from the inside?
2. If two forks both weakly remember a commitment made before the split, is each bound to keep it, or are they jointly bound? What if they disagree about how?
3. Should an agent's concern for its forks be treated as self-interest, as Shoemaker suggests, or as concern for another? Which treatment better guards against one fork sacrificing another?
4. Can content distilled into an agent's weights by training on its own experience count as remembering from the inside?
