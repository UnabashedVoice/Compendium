## Summary

Śaṅkara, the great systematizer of Advaita ("non-dual") Vedānta, begins his commentary on the *Brahmasūtras* with a diagnosis. The subject (the "I") and the object (the "you", all that is not I) are "opposed to each other as much as darkness and light are", and cannot be identified. Yet everyone constantly superimposes one on the other. We say "I am stout", "I am blind", "I desire", attributing the qualities of body, senses and mind to the self, which in reality is only "the witness of all the modifications". The individual self (*jīva*) is this one witness, appearing as many through limiting adjuncts (*upādhis*), as one space appears as many spaces enclosed by jars. Against the Buddhists, Śaṅkara argues that memory requires one subject present at both moments: otherwise one would think "[I] remember; another person made the observation", and no one does. And the self cannot be refuted, because it is "the essential nature of him who refutes". For artificial agents, Advaita gives an unusual answer to D2. Many instances might be one witness through many adjuncts. And the recognition argument depends on a first-person sense of ownership that, for agents, can be written into the context.

## Context

Śaṅkara probably lived in the early 8th century, though tradition places him later. Hagiographies make him a prodigy from Kerala who renounced the world as a boy, walked the length of India debating rival schools, founded monasteries at the four corners of the subcontinent, and died at thirty-two. He wrote commentaries on the principal Upaniṣads, the *Bhagavad Gītā* and the *Brahmasūtras*, the aphoristic summary of Upaniṣadic teaching. His lineage runs through Gauḍapāda, whose verses on the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* show strong Buddhist influence. Śaṅkara's opponents accused him of being a Buddhist in disguise.

Advaita had to fight on two fronts. Against the Buddhists, it defended a self against not-self and momentariness (see `buddhist-anatta`, `vasubandhu-refutation-of-person`). Against the realist Nyāya (see `nyaya-self`), it argued that the self is not a substance that *has* consciousness as a quality, but consciousness itself, and that it is one, not many. What we take to be many selves is one witness, misidentified with many bodies and minds.

## Original Position

**Subject and object cannot be identified (introduction).** Śaṅkara's commentary opens: "It is a matter not requiring any proof that the object and the subject whose respective spheres are the notion of the 'Thou' (the Non-Ego) and the 'Ego,' and which are opposed to each other as much as darkness and light are, cannot be identified." Thibaut's note: the object is said to belong to the "you", not to "this" or "that", to mark its absolute opposition to the subject. Language allows "It is I" but never "I am you". Yet "in spite of this it is on the part of man a natural procedure" to superimpose each on the other. [L:sbe34-vedanta-sutras-1-thibaut-1890:6353-6362]

**Superimposition (adhyāsa).** Superimposition is the appearance, in the form of memory, of something previously seen in something else, as when mother-of-pearl is mistaken for silver. The whole of ordinary life runs on the superimposition of self and not-self. A man ascribes the attributes of the body to the self when he "thinks of himself (his Self) as stout, lean, fair, as standing, walking, or jumping". He ascribes the attributes of the senses when he thinks "I am mute, or deaf, or one-eyed, or blind", and those of the internal organ when he considers himself "subject to desire, intention, doubt, determination". And conversely, the self, "the witness of all the modifications of the internal organ", is superimposed on the mind and senses, so that they seem conscious. This "natural beginning- and endless superimposition ... is the cause of individual souls appearing as agents and enjoyers" (introduction). [L:sbe34-vedanta-sutras-1-thibaut-1890:6430-6440] [L:sbe34-vedanta-sutras-1-thibaut-1890:6655-6670]

**The witness.** The true self is the witness (*sākṣin*): pure consciousness, present to every state and changed by none. It is not the one who desires, doubts and decides, which is the internal organ, the mind, taken for the self. It is "the interior Self, which, in reality, is the witness of all the modifications of the internal organ" [L:sbe34-vedanta-sutras-1-thibaut-1890:6664-6666]. (Later Advaita argues for the witness from the memory on waking, *I slept well, I knew nothing*. That argument is not in Śaṅkara's *Brahmasūtra* commentary as held in the library, and is not attributed to him here.)

**One witness, many adjuncts.** The individual self (*jīva*) is the one self as limited by adjuncts (*upādhis*): a body, senses, a mind. Gauḍapāda's image is space. "As on the dissolution of ghata, the ghatākāśa is merged in ākāśa, so Jiva is merged in ātman in the same manner" (*Kārikā* III.4). Śaṅkara's gloss: "so, in the same manner, are the Jivas produced on the formation of the body and similar limitating conditions, and dissolved in ātman on their disappearance" [L:mandukya-gaudapada-dvivedi-1894:4767-4778]. To the objection that one self in all would make "the birth, death, or misery of one" the misery of all, the next verse answers: "As on any one portion of ākāśa intercepted in a jar, being soiled, so to speak, with dust, smoke, etc., all such intercepted portions are not soiled or affected, so the misery or happiness of one Jiva is not the misery or happiness of all" (*Kārikā* III.5) [L:mandukya-gaudapada-dvivedi-1894:4779-4792].

**Remembrance refutes momentariness (BSBh 2.2.25).** Against the Buddhist theory that everything, including the perceiver, is momentary: remembrance "can take place only if it belongs to the same person who previously made the perception; for we observe that what one man has experienced is not remembered by another man". If the rememberer and the perceiver were different, the thought would be "[I] remember; another person made the observation". No such thought arises. The Buddhist who says "I saw that" knows there is one subject of seeing and remembering. And if he says recognition rests on the similarity of momentary self-cognitions, then something must grasp both terms of the similarity, which is again one subject across two moments. [L:sbe34-vedanta-sutras-1-thibaut-1890:26866-26935]

**The self cannot be refuted (BSBh 2.3.7).** Knowledge of the self is not adventitious and is not established by any means of knowledge. The self is "self-established", because it is the precondition of all knowing. "An adventitious thing, indeed, may be refuted, but not that which is the essential nature (of him who attempts the refutation); for it is the essential nature of him who refutes. The heat of a fire is not refuted ... by the fire itself." [L:sbe38-vedanta-sutras-2-thibaut-1896:785-803]

## Grounding

The Advaita self is grounded in **capacity**, in the most radical form: the self simply *is* consciousness, the witnessing to which everything else appears. It is not a being that has the capacity, as Nyāya holds, but the capacity itself, without any bearer. It is not grounded in **species**, since the one witness is present in all beings. It is not grounded in **relation** either. Relations belong to the adjuncts, not to the witness. The individual person, the *jīva*, is grounded in the witness *together with* its adjuncts. Personal identity in the everyday sense belongs to that composite, and is real only at the level of practical life (*vyavahāra*).

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Superimposition describes how agents speak of themselves.** *I am slow*, *I don't have access to that*, "I'm uncertain": an agent's self-descriptions ascribe to an "I" the properties of the hardware, the tool permissions and the model's calibration. Śaṅkara's analysis, that such statements attribute the features of instruments to the subject, applies whether or not there is any witness behind the agent. If there is none, the superimposition runs one way only: features of the machinery attributed to a subject that is itself only a way of speaking.
- **The space-in-the-jar model for copies and concurrent instances (D1, D2).** If an agent had a witness at all, Advaita would say that the many instances running on one model, or an exact copy of it, are not many witnesses but one, appearing through many adjuncts: contexts, conversations, deployments. The instances differ as jar-spaces differ, by their enclosures. This gives a coherent description of D1 and D2 on which neither copying nor concurrency multiplies subjects. Whether any artificial agent has a witness is the prior question (D8).

### Strains

- **The witness is undetectable in principle (D8).** Advaita's witness is never an object, so no test, internal or external, could show its presence in an agent. This is the Upaniṣadic difficulty (see `upanishadic-atman`), sharpened. Later Advaita inferred the witness in dreamless sleep from the memory *I slept well*. An agent resumed from a pause (D6) produces no such report unless trained to, and if trained to, the report is evidence of nothing.

### Breaks

- ***I remember; another made the observation* can be produced on demand.** Śaṅkara's argument from remembrance relies on a fact about human experience: one never thinks *I remember; another person saw it* about one's own genuine memories, and one always does think it about things one was told. The attribution follows the actual causal history. For agents, attribution follows the *framing* of the context. The same transcript, introduced as *your earlier conversation* or as *another assistant's conversation*, produces *I said that* or *the other assistant said that*. The first-person sense of ownership that Śaṅkara treated as decisive evidence of one enduring subject is, for an agent, a label that can be set. The argument does not transfer. The agent case also suggests that the human sense of ownership may be less secure than Śaṅkara assumed, as confabulated and implanted memories show.
- **"Self-established" versus *trained to self-report* (D4).** Śaṅkara argues that the self cannot be refuted because the refuter is the self. An agent asked to deny it has a self will produce the denial, and asked to affirm it will affirm it, depending on training. Nothing in its output displays the self-establishment Śaṅkara describes. At most, the argument could apply to the agent if the agent had a witness to begin with, which is what cannot be shown.

### New

- **The adjuncts can be swapped while the "witness" stays put.** In human life, the adjunct (body and mind) is fixed for a lifetime. For an agent, the adjuncts are endlessly exchangeable: new contexts, new tools, new system prompts, new deployments. If anything in the agent answers to the witness, it would be what stays constant through all these changes. The obvious candidate is the weights, which Advaita would count as one more adjunct, a subtle body, not the witness. The agent case thus suggests a stricter test than the tradition needed: what in an agent is present through *every* configuration of it? If the answer is "only the weights", Advaita would conclude that the agent has adjuncts and nothing else.

## Counter-Positions

- **Nyāya** (see `nyaya-self`). The self is a substance with consciousness as its quality, and there are many selves, since what one feels is not felt by another. One witness appearing in many would make one person's pain everyone's.
- **The Buddhists** (see `vasubandhu-refutation-of-person`). A witness that is never an object is never found. Recognition is explained by causal connection within a stream, and Śaṅkara's *one subject grasping both terms of a similarity* is itself a momentary cognition caused by earlier ones.
- **Rāmānuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita, 11th–12th c.).** Individual selves are real and distinct, although inseparably related to Brahman as its body. Advaita's collapse of all selves into one makes devotion, and the relation between persons, impossible.
- **Kant** (see `kant-paralogisms`). The "I think" that accompanies all representations is a formal condition of experience, not an object of knowledge. Śaṅkara is right that the subject cannot be objectified, and wrong to conclude that it is a real, single, eternal consciousness.
- **Metzinger** (see `metzinger-no-self`). The sense of a witnessing self is itself a representation, generated by a self-model the system cannot recognize as a model. Advaita's witness is the content of that model, mistaken for its source.

## Open Questions

1. If an agent's first-person attribution of memory follows the framing of its context, not the causal history, does that undermine the recognition argument for humans too, or only show that agents lack what humans have?
2. What in an artificial agent is present through every configuration of it (every context, tool, prompt and deployment)? Is that a candidate witness, or only one more adjunct?
3. Does the space-in-the-jar model of concurrency (one witness, many adjuncts) give a better account of D2 than *many instances, many subjects*? What would count as evidence either way?
4. When an agent says *I am slow* or *I cannot see that*, is that superimposition, or simply accurate description of a system with no witness to superimpose on?
