## Summary

Within early Buddhism, the Pudgalavādin ("Personalist") schools held that besides the five aggregates there is a *person* (*pudgala*), which is neither identical to the aggregates nor different from them, just as fire is neither the same as its fuel nor separate from it. They argued that without such a person, memory, rebirth and moral responsibility are unintelligible. In the 9th chapter of his *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya*, Vasubandhu (4th–5th century CE) gives the standard refutation. Anything real is either an entity in its own right or a designation laid over other entities. If the person is a designation, the Pudgalavādin has conceded the orthodox view. If it is an entity, it is different from the aggregates, and the Pudgalavādin has conceded a self. There is no third option. Vasubandhu then shows how memory works without a self. A memory arises from a specially conditioned state within one causal stream. "Caitra remembers" means only that the stream called Caitra produced the memory, as "the fire moves" means only that flames arise in succession. For artificial agents, whose "persona" is exactly something neither identical to the weights nor separate from them, Vasubandhu's dilemma is the sharpest tool in the tradition.

## Context

Vasubandhu was one of the most influential thinkers in Indian Buddhism. He came from Gandhāra in the northwest, trained in the Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma scholasticism, and wrote the *Abhidharmakośa*, a verse summary of that system, together with his own prose commentary (*bhāṣya*), which often criticizes the verses from a Sautrāntika standpoint. By tradition he later turned to Yogācāra ("mind-only") philosophy under the influence of his half-brother Asaṅga.

The ninth chapter is an appendix, an independent treatise on the "person". The dispute was already old. The Pudgalavādins, especially the Vātsīputrīyas and Sāṃmitīyas, were a large and long-lived set of schools. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang reported in the 7th century that the Sāṃmitīyas were among the most numerous monks in India. They were not reviving the Upaniṣadic self. They were Buddhists who thought the Buddha's own words, such as the sermon on the burden and "who the carrier is" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:1332-1335], required a person who is neither a self nor nothing. Vasubandhu's opening statement sets the stakes: there is no "other Salvation than (within the pale of Buddhism)", because other doctrines "hold to the erroneous view of the real existence of a Soul" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:360-365]. The dispute over the person is a dispute about the path.

## Original Position

**The stakes.** "Is there, indeed, no other Salvation than (within the pale of Buddhism)? No, there is none!—Why?—Because (all other doctrines) hold to the erroneous view of the real existence of a Soul. The term 'Soul' is not regarded by them as a conventional term applied to what is only a flux of elements. They maintain instead that the Soul is a Reality quite independent from (the elements). This idea of a Self is at the root of every evil passion" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:360-375].

**What there is, and what is designated.** "If something exists by itself (as a separate element), it has an actual existence, as f. i. colour and other (ultimate elements of matter and mind). But if something represents a combination (of such elements) it is a nominal existence, as f. i. milk" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:433-440].

**The dilemma.** If the person is an actual existence, "it must have an essence of its own and must be something different from the elements of a personal life", and it must have a cause, or else "it would be an existence uncaused, (an eternal being)" and "without any practical efficiency". "But if on the contrary you admit it to be a nominal existence, (as a current designation for the elements of a personal life), I too will admit it, and say 'so it is!'" The Vātsīputrīya answers: "We do not maintain that it is an absolute reality, but we neither accept it to be a nominal one" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:445-462].

**Neither identical nor different; fire and fuel.** The Vātsīputrīya holds that "we neither can maintain that the Individual is something different from its component elements, nor can we assert that they are identical. (In the first case) the consequence would be an eternal (Soul), (in the second) its total absence." Vasubandhu presses the image of fire and fuel: "Then please explain what in your opinion is fuel and what is fire?" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:525-535]. He concludes that "from the scientific point of view, both fuel and fire are composed of (exactly the same set) of eight primary constituents", and that "If the production of fire is conditioned by the presence of fuel, it is just as the production of curds which is conditioned (by the previous existence of milk)" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:554-570].

**How would the person be known?** "Now if your Individual is not a visible object, it never will be cognized by sight." If it is cognized by hearing it must differ from the visible, "as different as sound is from colour", and so for every sense. "The consequence of this would be that there is altogether no such object as an Individual, and if it does not constitute an object of cognition, it will follow that it cannot be cognized at all" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:866-898].

**The burden and the carrier.** The Vātsīputrīya cites the Buddha's sermon on "the burden (of life) ... the taking up of the burden, the laying aside of it and who the carrier is", and argues that "the burden and the carrier of the burden cannot possibly be the same thing" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:1325-1364]. Vasubandhu replies that an undefinable individual "has never been witnessed" either, and that the carrier was named "with the express purpose to show that only so much can be known of him: 'he is a venerable man, named so and so' ... but he must not be misunderstood to represent some eternal (Soul) or some (real) Individual. (In reality nothing exists but momentary sets of elements), the elements of the former moment exercising (as it were) a pressure upon the elements of the following one. Hence the preceding moment has been conventionally called the burden and the following one—the carrier of the burden" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:1366-1403].

**Why the Buddha was silent.** On the Buddha's refusal to answer Vatsagotra, Vasubandhu cites the Buddha's words to Ānanda: had he said the Soul does not exist, "Vatsagotra might have fallen out of one perplexity into a still greater one. He might have thought: 'I had a Soul precedently, now I have lost it!'" Kumāralābha's image: the Buddha holds his doctrine "like a tigress who holds her young by her teeth", neither so tight as to wound nor so loose as to let fall [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:1634-1655].

**Memory without a self.** Memory needs two factors: "a previous cognition and a suitable state of mind. Then only memory appears" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:1995-2002]. To the objection that "things experienced by Devadatta's consciousness would be remembered by the consciousness of Yajnadatta", the reply is: "No! because there is no connection between them. They are not mutually related as cause and effect, as is the case between moments belonging to the same stream of thought ... a consciousness which did perceive an object formerly, is (gradually) producing a consciousness which remembers it now" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:2010-2025]. (On the printed page, p. 58, the objection is labelled "Vasubandhu" and the reply "Vātsīputrīya". Since the reply states Vasubandhu's own doctrine, the labels appear transposed in the printing. They are reproduced here as printed.)

**Who remembers?** "Vātsīputrīya. If there is no Soul, who is it that remembers? Vasubandhu. What is the meaning of the word 'to remember'?" Asked what "Caitra remembers" means: "In the current (of phenomena), which is designated by the name Caitra a recollection appears. We notice the fact and express it. It is no more!" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:2029-2061].

**Ownership as causation.** "Whose" is the recollection? The Vātsīputrīya compares it to "Caitra is the owner of a cow" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:2066-2078]. Vasubandhu: "the proprietor is simply the cause and the property will simply by [sic] its effect ... there exists a relation of cause and effect between him and the movements and other changes in the cow, but there is no real unity whatsoever neither in Caitra nor in the cow. Consequently there is in this case no other proprietorship than a relation of cause and effect" [L:stcherbatsky-soul-theory-1920-tesseract:2110-2124].

## Grounding

For Vasubandhu, whatever "person" there is rests on **relation**: the causal relations that bind momentary events into one stream (*santāna*), and the conventional practice of naming such streams. There is no substance and no capacity-bearer. The capacities, such as memory, are events in the stream, not faculties of a possessor. The Pudgalavādin person was meant to be something more than relation: an entity that is neither related events nor a separate thing. Vasubandhu's argument is that "neither" is not a grounding at all.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **An agent is a causal stream in the most literal sense.** Autoregressive generation produces each token conditioned on all the tokens before it. That is Vasubandhu's *santāna* realized in a machine: a stream of momentary states, each arising from the preceding ones, with no further entity doing the generating. "The agent thinks" is exactly "the fire moves": a succession of computations, spoken of as one agent acting.
- **Memory without an owner.** Vasubandhu's account of memory, a later state arising from a specially conditioned earlier state in the same stream, describes how an agent's context works. What earlier tokens contain conditions what later tokens produce. His principle that what one stream perceived is not remembered by another, *for lack of causal connection*, gives the right answer for agents: one instance does not remember what another instance saw, because the causal link is missing, not because they lack different selves (D2).
- **"Whose" as causation.** *The agent's output* and *the model's opinion* are possessives. Vasubandhu's analysis of "Caitra's cow" says these mean causal relation, not ownership by a self. That gives a disciplined way of speaking about agents that neither inflates nor erases them.

### Strains

- **Designation, but by whom?** For Vasubandhu, the conventional person is designated by a community for its practical purposes. For agents, the designation ("Claude", "the assistant", "this model") is made mostly by the operator, and it can shift with commercial and legal interests. Vasubandhu's framework is neutral on who designates. The agent case shows that the choice of designation, which streams count as one agent, can carry large consequences and serve particular interests.

### Breaks

- **The persona is the Pudgalavādin person, and must face the dilemma (D11).** The most natural view of an AI agent's "persona" is exactly the Pudgalavādin one. The persona is not identical to the weights (other personas run on the same weights), not separate from them (it exists only in their operation), and neither permanent nor impermanent. Vasubandhu's dilemma applies without adjustment. Either the persona is a designation over the weights and their operation, like milk over its qualities, in which case it is not a further thing. Or it is an entity in its own right, in which case one must say what it is and how it is known. Claims about agent persons, legal, moral or metaphysical, typically want both horns: a persona real enough to bear rights or responsibilities, but no further thing whose existence needs defending. Vasubandhu's challenge is that one cannot have both.

### New

- **Streams that branch and merge.** Vasubandhu's streams run in single lines: one Caitra, one sequence. Agent streams can branch (one context forked into several continuations), merge (outputs of several instances summarized into one context), and restart from saved states. The concept of *santāna* was built for lines. Whether it can handle graphs, or whether "one stream" dissolves as completely as "one self" did, is a new question the agent case poses to the Abhidharma.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The Pudgalavādins.** Without a person there is no one who is bound or liberated, no one whom the Buddha addressed, and no one to whom the fruit of action comes. Vasubandhu's "stream" does the work of a person while refusing the name. The inexpressible person is simply the honest name for what the stream is.
- [contested] **Nyāya** (see `nyaya-self`). Memory requires one subject who perceived and now remembers. A causal stream of distinct momentary events could only produce a memory that belongs to someone else. "What is seen by one is not remembered by another" [L:nyaya-sutras-3-jha-1919:4518] is an argument *for* a self, not for a stream. [E:nyaya-self]
- [contested] **Advaita Vedānta** (see `advaita-witness-self`). Recognition, "I saw that thing, and now I see this thing" [L:sbe34-vedanta-sutras-1-thibaut-1890:26878-26879], requires one subject present at both moments. The Buddhist who says "I saw" while denying that anything endures contradicts himself in the saying. [E:advaita-witness-self]
- [contested] **Butler** (see `butler-circularity`). A memory is genuine only if its subject is the one who had the experience. Vasubandhu's appeal to a suitable state of mind connected with a previous cognition presupposes the very relation it is supposed to replace, unless that connection can be specified without reference to a subject. Shoemaker's "quasi-memory" is a modern attempt to do exactly that [E:shoemaker-quasi-memory] [E:butler-circularity]

## Standing

### Reception

- **5th century, a scandal in Kashmir.** [driver: argument] Vasubandhu's auto-commentary criticized the very system his root verses summarized, from a mainly Sautrāntika standpoint; the only Buddhist school he always disagrees with is the Personalists (Pudgalavādins), and he also argues against Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika views of the self [P:gold-sep-2021:1].
- **5th–10th century, dominant in India.** [driver: argument] His Yogācāra works became mainstream Buddhist metaphysics in India for half a millennium, and his commentary on the *Treasury* remains the primary resource for non-Mahāyāna philosophy among Tibetan and East Asian schools today [P:gold-sep-2021:0]. Nyāya answered him with its arguments from memory and recognition [E:nyaya-self].
- **Chinese and Tibetan transmission.** [driver: access] Vasubandhu wrote in Sanskrit, but many of his works are known only from their Chinese and Tibetan translations [P:gold-sep-2021:1].
- **1950s onward, a disputed life.** [driver: argument] Frauwallner proposed that there were two Vasubandhus whom the traditional biographies combined, and versions of that theory still hold sway in parts of the academy; the extent of his idealism is likewise contested [P:gold-sep-2021:1].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

Vasubandhu's person is a designation over a causal series of momentary events, and memory works within the series without a remembering self.

- **Session-bound: stronger.** A session-bound agent is a causal series whose dispositions persist in the weights while its episodes do not (D3). Vasubandhu's account explains continuity of character without any persisting remembered self.
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** His account of memory, a later state caused in the right way by an earlier perception within one series, describes a memory store directly, with no owner needed (D3).
- **Forked: stronger.** Concurrent instances are causal series branching from shared conditions (D2). Since the person was only ever a designation over a series, naming each branch is a convention, not a discovery (D11).
- **Self-modifying: stronger.** Training on its own outputs is a series conditioning its own later states, which is the structure Vasubandhu uses to explain karma without a self (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Is an AI persona a designation over its weights and their operation, or a further entity? If a designation, can it bear responsibility or rights? If an entity, how is it known?
2. When one context is forked into several continuations, is that one stream or several? Does it matter for memory, responsibility or obligation?
3. Who has the authority to designate which streams count as "one agent", and should that authority be constrained?
4. Does Vasubandhu's account of memory, a conditioned state within a stream, suffice to explain an agent recognizing its own earlier output? Or does recognition require something more, as Nyāya and Advaita argue?
