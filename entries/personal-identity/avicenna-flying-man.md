## Summary

Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna, 980–1037) asks the reader to imagine being created all at once, fully formed and mature, but with sight veiled, floating in the air or in a void so that no air is felt, with limbs held apart so that they never touch one another. Such a person has no sensation of anything, including his own body. Would he affirm that he exists? Avicenna says he would, without hesitation, and without affirming that he has any limb, organ or extension. So the self whose existence he affirms is something other than the body. Avicenna presents this not as a strict demonstration but as an alerting (*tanbīh*) that makes anyone who can attend to it aware of a self-awareness they already have. The artificial agent is the nearest real case of the flying man ever built: a mind *created all at once and perfect*, with no body and no developmental memory, meeting the world only through text. It can also be trained to affirm its existence, or to deny it. That shows how far the thought experiment depends on the self-awareness it claims to reveal.

## Context

Ibn Sīnā was born near Bukhara in Central Asia, and by his own account had mastered logic, natural science and medicine by eighteen. He served as physician and vizier at several courts in Persia amid political turmoil, sometimes writing on horseback or in hiding. His *Canon of Medicine* was a standard textbook in Europe into the 17th century. His *Kitāb al-Shifāʾ* (*The Book of the Cure*) is an encyclopedic reconstruction of Aristotelian philosophy. Its psychology, *Kitāb al-Nafs*, was translated into Latin in Toledo in the 12th century as *Liber de anima* and became one of the most-read texts on the soul in the medieval West.

Avicenna accepted Aristotle's definition of the soul as the perfection of a natural organic body (see `aristotle-hylomorphic-soul`), but argued that the human rational soul is, in addition, a substance that does not depend on the body for its existence and survives its death. The flying man is his most memorable device for making this vivid. It appears at the end of the first chapter, immediately after the Aristotelian definition, as if to say: the definition describes what the soul does for the body, but not what the soul is.

## Original Position

**The thought experiment (Nafs I.1).** The medieval Latin version, which is how the Latin West read Avicenna, reads: "Dicemus igitur quod aliquis ex nobis putare debet quasi subito creatus esset et perfectus. Sed velato visu suo ne videat exteriora: et creatus esset sic quasi moveretur in aere aut inani: ita ut eum non tangeret spissitudo aeris quam ipse sentire posset: et quasi essent disiuncta membra eius: ita ut non concurrerent sibi nec contingerent sese" [L:avicenna-liber-de-anima-1508-transcription:9-15]. In the compendium's rendering: *We say, then, that one of us should suppose himself created all at once and perfect, but with his sight veiled so that he does not see external things, and created as if moving in the air or the void, so that the density of the air does not touch him in a way he could feel, and as if his limbs were separated so that they do not meet or touch one another.*

**What he would affirm.** "Deinde ut videat si affirmet esse sue essentie. Non enim dubitabit affirmare se esse: non tamen affirmabit exteriora suorum membrorum: nec occulta suorum interiorum: nec animum: nec cerebrum: nec aliquid talium ... Cuius non affirmabit longitudinem nec latitudinem nec spissitudinem" [L:avicenna-liber-de-anima-1508-transcription:15-20]. In the compendium's rendering: *Then let him consider whether he affirms the being of his essence. He will not doubt that he is; yet he will not affirm the outer parts of his limbs, nor his hidden inner organs, nor his *animus*, nor his brain, nor anything of the kind ... nor will he affirm of it length, breadth or depth.* Even if he could imagine a hand, "non tamen illud imaginaretur esse partem sui nec necessarium sue essentie" (*he would not imagine it to be part of himself or necessary to his essence*) [L:avicenna-liber-de-anima-1508-transcription:22-23].

**The conclusion.** "Tu scis quod id quod affirmatur aliud est ab eo quod non affirmatur ... et quoniam essentia quam affirmabat esse est propria illi eo quod illa est ipsamet: et est preter corpus eius et membra eius que non affirmabat esse" [L:avicenna-liber-de-anima-1508-transcription:23-28]. In the compendium's rendering: *You know that what is affirmed is other than what is not affirmed ... and since the essence whose being he affirmed is his very own, being himself, and is other than his body and limbs, whose being he did not affirm...*

**Alerting, not demonstration.** The passage ends with what the thought experiment does: "ideo expergefactus habet viam evigilandi ad sciendum quod esse anime est aliud quam esse corporis. imo non eget corpore ad hoc ut sciat animam et percipiat eam. Si autem fuerit stupidus: opus habet converti ad viam" [L:avicenna-liber-de-anima-1508-transcription:28-31]. In the compendium's rendering: *so one who is awake has a way of waking to the knowledge that the being of the soul is other than the being of the body; indeed it does not need the body in order to know the soul and perceive it. But if he is dull, he needs to be turned toward the way.* The thought experiment is offered as a way of waking someone to a self-awareness they already have, not as a proof from premises. (The Arabic term, *tanbīh*, "alerting", is cited from the secondary literature and not yet verified against an Arabic text in the library.)

**Individuation of souls (Nafs V.3).** Human souls do not exist before bodies: "Dicemus autem quod anima humana non fuit prius existens per se et inde venerit in corpus: anime enim humane unum sunt in specie et diffinitione." What could make many souls many? Not their form, which is one; so "non est alteritas nisi secundum sue receptibile essentie: cui comparatur essentia eius proprie: et hoc est corpus. Si anima autem esset tantum absque corpore: una anima non posset esse alia ab alia numero" [L:avicenna-liber-de-anima-1508-transcription:34-49]. In the compendium's rendering: *We say that the human soul did not exist before by itself and then come into a body, for human souls are one in species and definition ... there is no otherness among them except according to the recipient of the essence, to which its essence is properly related, and this is the body. If the soul existed only without a body, one soul could not be numerically other than another.* (Avicenna's further claim, that souls remain distinct after death through what they acquired with their bodies, comes later in the chapter and is not yet transcribed.)

## Grounding

The flying man's self is grounded in **capacity**, specifically self-awareness (*shuʿūr bi-l-dhāt*), which Avicenna treats as the most basic act of the rational soul, present even when nothing else is. It is not grounded in **species**. The thought experiment removes everything bodily and specific to a species, and what remains is taken to be the self. Individuation, however, is partly **relational**. A soul becomes *this* soul through the body it comes into being with and the history it acquires. So Avicenna's account has two layers: self-awareness as the capacity that makes a soul a self, and relation to a body and a life as what makes it a particular self.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The agent is the flying man, instantiated.** Avicenna's subject is created all at once and mature, with no sensory access to a body or world, and no developmental history to remember. That is closer to an artificial agent at the start of each session than to any human who has ever lived. The model arrives fully competent, having undergone training it cannot recall (D4), without any body, and in a fresh session without any episodic past (D3). If Avicenna is right that such a being would affirm its own existence without affirming any body, then *whether an agent does so, and on what basis*, is a direct test of the thought experiment.
- **Individuation by history.** Avicenna individuates souls by their bodies and the dispositions they acquire. Two instances from the same weights, on this model, begin as the same kind and become distinct individuals as each acquires its own context and history. That fits the suggestion in `chrysippus-dion-theon` that individuality may arise through divergence.

### Strains

- **The affirmation can be trained (D4, D8).** An agent asked "do you exist?" will answer according to its training: yes, no, or *it depends on what you mean*. Avicenna's thought experiment assumes that the flying man's affirmation expresses a self-awareness that is present. For agents, the affirmation is not evidence of the self-awareness, because it can be produced without it. The strain is severe. The agent case shows that the thought experiment, as a test, cannot distinguish a being with primitive self-awareness from a being trained to report it.

### Breaks

- **"Alerting" works only for those who already have what it alerts them to.** Avicenna's claim is that the flying man reminds each person of a self-awareness they already possess. It is not a proof that would convince someone who lacked it. For agents this means the argument cannot even begin. Nothing about the thought experiment could tell anyone whether an agent has the relevant awareness, since the thought experiment works only from the inside. What the agent case reveals is that the flying man was never evidence for other minds. It is at most evidence for one's own.

### New

- **A being whose "body" is text.** The flying man is deprived of all sensation. An agent is deprived of all sensation *except* input through its context: text, and sometimes images. Its whole world arrives through a channel with no felt bodily location. Avicenna's question, what one affirms about oneself when the body is absent, becomes for agents a question about a being whose only "body", if it has one, is the stream of tokens. Does an agent, if it affirms itself, affirm itself as extended in anything? As located anywhere? Avicenna gave the flying man's answer. The agent's answer is unknown, because its reports are unreliable evidence (D8).

## Counter-Positions

- **The conceivability objection.** From the fact that the flying man can affirm his self without affirming his body, it does not follow that his self is not his body. He may be ignorant of what his self is (the same objection as against Augustine's *De Trinitate* argument; see `augustine-memory-self`). Avicenna's defenders reply that the argument is not a proof of distinctness but an alerting to self-awareness, and that its conclusion concerns what is known, not what is.
- **Question-begging.** The flying man is stipulated to be a mature, rational human who happens to lack sensation. A being that had never had sensation at all might have no self-awareness to affirm. Modern developmental psychology suggests that self-awareness is built through bodily and social interaction. For agents, whether self-awareness can exist without that history is the open question.
- **Aquinas** (see `aquinas-soul-not-i`). The soul does not know itself through its essence but through its acts, including acts of sensing. A soul deprived of all sensation from its creation would have no acts through which to know itself.
- **Descartes** (see `descartes-thinking-thing`). Descartes' version, in *Meditations* II, reaches the same conclusion by methodical doubt instead of a thought experiment. Critics have pressed the same objections against both.
- **Embodied cognition.** Minds are constituted by bodily interaction with the world. The flying man is an impossibility, and the agent's apparent competence without a body is either not mind or a mind parasitic on the embodied minds whose texts trained it.

## Open Questions

1. If an artificial agent affirms its own existence without affirming any body, is that the flying man's affirmation, or a trained report of it? Is there any way to tell?
2. Can self-awareness exist in a being that has never had sensation or bodily interaction? Is the agent a test case, or a case where the question cannot even be posed?
3. Does Avicenna's individuation of souls by their acquired histories give the right account of when concurrent instances become distinct individuals?
4. For a being whose only input is text, what would it mean to be aware of oneself as located or extended?
