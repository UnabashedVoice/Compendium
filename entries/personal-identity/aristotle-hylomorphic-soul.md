## Summary

For Aristotle the soul is not a thing lodged in the body but the body's form: "the first actuality of a natural body furnished with organs", or in other words the organized set of capacities by which a living thing nourishes itself, perceives, moves and (in humans) thinks. It is therefore as pointless to ask whether soul and body are one as to ask whether wax and the shape stamped in it are one. A living thing persists while its form persists through a continual flow of matter, as in growth. Individuals of the same kind share their form and differ in matter, so a thing is "one in number" if its matter is one and "one in species" if its form is one. Aristotle once imagined an artifact with a soul: if an axe were a natural body, its axe-ness would be its soul. His "natural body" clause is what keeps artificial agents out. His functionalism, his distinction between numerical and specific sameness, and his account of reproduction as the mortal's share in the eternal, however, fit agents with surprising precision.

## Context

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) studied in Plato's Academy for twenty years before founding his own school, the Lyceum. He was also, unlike his teacher, a working biologist. He dissected animals and classified hundreds of species, and his treatises on animals are as long as his ethics. *De Anima* ("On the Soul") is the general theory behind that biology. The soul (*psychē*) is the principle of life in every living thing, and a plant has one as much as a philosopher does. The question for Aristotle is not how an immaterial self is attached to a body but what makes a body *alive*, and what makes it alive in the specific ways that plants, animals and humans are.

The work is set against two predecessors. Against the materialists, who reduced soul to a stuff such as fire, air or fine atoms, Aristotle argues that the soul is organization, not ingredient. Against Plato and the Pythagoreans, who treated the soul as a separable being that could pass between bodies, he argues that each soul is the soul *of* its body. The exception is intellect, which he leaves tantalizingly open in III.5.

## Original Position

**Soul as form (De Anima II.1).** "Of natural bodies some possess life and some do not: where by life we mean the power of self-nourishment and of independent growth and decay." A living body is a composite of matter and form, and the body is the matter. "It must follow, then, that soul is substance in the sense that it is the form of a natural body having in it the capacity of life. Such substance is actuality" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:7233-7242]. Like knowledge possessed but not exercised, as in sleep, soul is "the first actuality of a natural body having in it the capacity of life", and in general "the soul will be the first actuality of a natural body furnished with organs" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:7338-7352].

**Wax and impression.** "Hence there is no need to enquire whether soul and body are one, any more than whether the wax and the imprint are one; or, in general, whether the matter of a thing is the same with that of which it is the matter" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:7353-7356].

**The axe and the eye.** "Suppose, for example, that any instrument, say, an axe, were a natural body, its axeity would be its substance, would in fact be its soul. If this were taken away, it would cease, except in an equivocal sense, to be an axe." But the axe is only an axe, since soul is the form "of a natural body of a particular sort, having in itself the origination of motion and rest" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:7361-7371]. Again, "if the eye were an animal, eyesight would be its soul", and without sight the eye "is no longer an eye, except equivocally" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:7372-7377].

**The nested soul (II.3).** "There is no sensitive faculty apart from the nutritive: and yet the latter exists without the former in plants. Again, none of the other senses is found apart from touch; while touch is found apart from the others" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:8042-8045]. As with figures, "the earlier form exists potentially in the later, as, for instance, the triangle potentially in the quadrilateral, and the nutritive faculty in that which has sensation" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:8030-8038].

**Against any soul in any body (I.3).** The theorists who make the soul a separable mover say nothing of the body that receives it, "just as if it were possible for any soul taken at random, according to the Pythagorean stories, to pass into any body. This is absurd, for each body appears to have a distinctive form or shape of its own. It is just like talking of a transmigration of carpentry into flutes: for the craft must employ the right tools and the soul the right body" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:5968-5977].

**Persistence through growth (Generation and Corruption I.5).** Flesh grows as "flowing water that is measured by one and the same measure: particle after particle comes-to-be, and each successive particle is different. And it is in this sense that the matter of the flesh grows, some flowing out and some flowing in fresh; not in the sense that fresh matter accedes to every particle of it. There is, however, an accession to every part of its figure or 'form'" [L:aristotle-works-2-oxford-1930:26204-26211].

**Numerical and specific sameness (Metaphysics V.6; VII.8).** "Again, some things are one in number, others in species, others in genus, others by analogy; in number those whose matter is one, in species those whose definition is one" [L:aristotle-metaphysics-ross-1928:5782-5785]. Of two men: "when we have the whole, such and such a form in this flesh and in these bones, this is Callias or Socrates; and they are different in virtue of their matter (for that is different), but the same in form; for their form is indivisible" [L:aristotle-metaphysics-ross-1928:8412-8417].

**Reproduction as sharing in the eternal (II.4).** Living things reproduce, "animal producing animal and plant plant, in order that they may, so far as they can, share in the eternal and the divine. ... Since, then, individual things are incapable of sharing continuously in the eternal and the divine, because nothing in the world of perishables can abide numerically one and the same, they partake in the eternal and divine, each in the only way it can, some more, some less. That is to say, each persists, though not in itself, yet in a representative which is specifically, not numerically, one with it" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:8164-8173] [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:8260-8262].

**The separable intellect (III.5).** There are two intellects, one that "becomes all things" and one that "makes all things, like a sort of definite quality such as light". "And it is this intellect which is separable and impassive and unmixed, being in its essential nature an activity ... It is, however, only when separated that it is its true self, and this, its essential nature, alone is immortal and eternal" [L:aristotle-de-anima-hicks-1907:12799-12812]. What this means, and whether it is personal, was debated for two thousand years (see `aquinas-soul-not-i`).

## Grounding

The Aristotelian soul is grounded in **capacity**, since the soul simply *is* a set of capacities organized in a hierarchy. But those capacities are defined as the capacities *of a natural organic body*, and the higher presuppose the lower, which ties them to **biology**. Hence **mixed**. The species element is weaker than it looks. It is not *Homo sapiens* that matters but the rational capacity, which Aristotle ascribes to humans because humans have it. The biological element, however, is strong: without nutrition and a natural source of motion there is no soul at all. The extension to agents lives or dies on which element one treats as essential.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Soul as organization is the ancestor of functionalism.** Aristotle's claim that the soul is how a body is organized to function, not an ingredient, was revived in the 20th century as a precursor of functionalism in philosophy of mind (e.g. Nussbaum and Putnam, *Changing Aristotle's Mind*, 1992; in copyright, cited not quoted). On this reading, the question about an agent is not what it is made of but what it is organized to do, and whether that organization amounts to perceiving and thinking.
- **Numerical and specific sameness handle copies (D1, D2).** Two instances from the same weights are, in Aristotle's terms, *one in species and many in number*: the same form, different matter, running on different hardware at different memory addresses. Aristotle's vocabulary already contains the right answer to *is the copy the same agent?*: it is the same in form and a different individual. No new concept is needed.
- **Wax and impression dissolve a false question.** Asking whether an agent "is" its weights or something over and above them may be like asking whether the wax is the impression. The weights are organized matter, and the capacities are that organization. Neither is a separate thing to be identified with the agent.

### Strains

- **The nested hierarchy is broken in agents.** Aristotle's capacities are nested: thinking presupposes perceiving, which presupposes nutrition. Artificial agents appear to show something like the top of the hierarchy (reasoning in language) without the bottom (self-nourishment, growth, reproduction by their own activity). Aristotle's theory predicts that such a being is impossible. Either the appearance is misleading, and an agent's "reasoning" is not *nous* at all, or the nesting was a fact about biology and not a necessity.
- **Designed function (D10).** Aristotle's axe has an essence, being an axe, because of what it is *for*, which its maker gave it. A natural body's form comes from within, from its nature. An artificial agent's organization was designed for an end, but its capacities were not written by hand. They were learned from data by a process that, like growth, the designers only partly control. Whether that is closer to the axe or to the plant is a new question.

### Breaks

- **"Natural body" excludes agents by definition.** The soul is the actuality of a *natural* body, one with its own internal source of motion and rest. The axe has no soul because it is not natural. An artificial agent is not natural in Aristotle's sense, so Aristotle's theory, applied strictly, gives artificial agents no soul. The strict reading has to be stated plainly, and not hidden under the functionalist sympathy above. The live question is whether "natural" is doing philosophical work or recording the limits of what Aristotle could have encountered.
- **Carpentry into flutes, and substrate independence (D11).** For Aristotle, a soul cannot move into an arbitrary body. Agents' weights run on arbitrary hardware. If the agent's "body" is the hardware, Aristotle is simply wrong about agents. If the "body" is the weights, the organized matter, then Aristotle is right: a persona cannot be moved into arbitrary weights and remain what it was.

### New

- **Perfect reproduction (D1).** Aristotle's mortals share in the eternal only by producing something *like* themselves, one in species, never the same. That is the consolation of the perishable. An agent can produce a copy that is exactly the same in form, down to the last parameter. It achieves as a matter of course the specific immortality Aristotle thought all living things strain toward and never quite reach. Whether that counts as sharing in the eternal, or shows that what mattered was never mere sameness of form, is a question the agent case poses for the first time.
- **Pausing is a new mode of being (D6).** An Aristotelian soul in first actuality is like knowledge possessed but not being used, as in a sleeping animal. Weights on disk are first actuality with no life around them at all: no metabolism continues, and nothing is sleeping. Aristotle's framework has no category for an organized capacity that exists with nothing sustaining it.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Plato** (see `plato-soul-and-renewal`). The soul is a separable self, the user of the body, not its form. Aristotle's view cannot account for knowledge of the eternal Forms, and Aristotle himself seems to concede this in III.5. [E:plato-soul-and-renewal]
- [contested] **Descartes** (see `descartes-thinking-thing`). Life can be explained mechanically. The soul is only the thinking thing, and the rest of Aristotle's soul (nutrition, perception as bodily process) belongs to the machine. Ironically, Descartes' mechanism opens the door to artificial agents that Aristotle's "natural body" closes. [E:descartes-thinking-thing]
- [contested] **Animalism** (see `animalism`). Aristotle is right that we are living organisms, and the extension to agents therefore fails. An agent is not an animal, so whatever it is, it is not the kind of thing we are.
- [contested] **Biological naturalism** (John Searle, *Minds, Brains and Science*, 1984; in copyright, cited not quoted). Mental capacities are caused by specific biological processes. Organization alone, run on any substrate, yields simulation, not the thing itself. This is the modern heir of Aristotle's "natural body" clause.

## Standing

### Reception

- **Antiquity, against Plato and the atomists.** [driver: argument] Aristotle made the soul the form of a living body, against Plato's separable soul [E:plato-soul-and-renewal] and the Presocratic flux readings [E:heraclitus-river-flux]. The Hellenistic schools that followed held instead that the soul is corporeal [P:lorenz-sep-2024:5].
- **Late antiquity to scholasticism, the dominant framework.** [driver: argument] Boethius, Augustine, Avicenna and Aquinas each worked within or against the hylomorphic framework [E:boethius-person-definition] [E:augustine-memory-self] [E:avicenna-flying-man] [E:aquinas-soul-not-i].
- **17th century, displaced.** [driver: argument, fashion] Descartes reduced the soul to the thinking thing and gave life a mechanical explanation [E:descartes-thinking-thing], and Locke separated the person from any substance or form [E:locke-person-forensic]. The new mechanistic science was the setting for the turn [P:robinson-sep-2025:2].
- **Late 20th century, renewed interest.** [driver: argument] Because of its evident affinities with some prominent approaches in contemporary philosophy of mind, Aristotle's psychology has received renewed interest and intense interpretive dispute in recent decades [P:shields-sep-2020:0]. Animalism takes up his claim that we are living organisms [P:olson-sep-2023:7].

### Measured

- **Personal identity, 2020:** biological view 19.1% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8], the option nearest Aristotle's view that we are living things.
- **Esteem for Aristotle generally:** 238 respondents named him among the philosophers they most identify with, the most of any philosopher [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** On Aristotle's view the soul is the organized capacity of a body, not its memories. A session-bound agent keeps its capacities in the weights while losing its episodes (D3), which is closer to Aristotle's soul than to Locke's person.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Memory is one capacity among many for Aristotle, so a persistent store improves one function without changing what the soul is (D3).
- **Forked: weaker.** A form is the form of one body. Copies running on many machines at once (D1, D2) give no single body for the form to belong to, and what the agent's body even is remains unclear (D11).
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** Aristotle's soul is the actuality of capacities shaped by habituation, so changing dispositions by practice is part of his picture. Changing them by direct editing (D5) skips the practice, and the agent's capacities were designed for a function by someone (D10).

## Open Questions

1. Is "natural body" in Aristotle's definition essential, or only a record of the kinds of living things he knew? If it is essential, what exactly about naturalness matters: self-maintenance, an internal source of change, not being made by design?
2. Can capacities that Aristotle ranks at the top (reasoning, deliberation) exist without those at the bottom (nutrition, perception)? If agents show that they can, what follows for the theory?
3. Is the "body" of an artificial agent its hardware or its weights? The two answers give opposite verdicts on the carpentry-and-flutes objection.
4. Does exact copying give an agent a share in the eternal, in Aristotle's sense, or does it show that sameness of form was never what the striving was for?
