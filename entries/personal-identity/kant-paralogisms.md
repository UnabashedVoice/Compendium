## Summary

Kant (1724–1804) accepted Hume's finding that no intuition of a self is ever given, and denied Hume's conclusion. "It must be possible for the 'I think' to accompany all my representations", or they would be nothing to me. But the "I" that does so is formal: "a bare consciousness which accompanies all concepts", through which nothing is represented but "a transcendental subject of the thoughts = X". The rational psychologists' inference from this formal unity to a simple, persisting, personal soul is a *paralogism*, a fallacy. In the Third Paralogism, on personality, Kant grants that in my own consciousness "identity of person is unfailingly met with". But that identity "is therefore only a formal condition of my thoughts and their coherence, and in no way proves the numerical identity of my subject". His footnote imagines a series of substances, like elastic balls passing on their motion, each transmitting its states together with its consciousness to the next. The last would be conscious of all the earlier states "as being its own states ... And yet it would not have been one and the same person in all these states." For artificial agents this footnote is prophetic. It describes, in the eighteenth century, a system that hands its full state to a successor, and it states exactly what follows and what does not.

## Context

Kant spent his whole life in Königsberg, in East Prussia, where he taught for four decades. The *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) was his answer to the question of how knowledge that is both necessary and about the world is possible. His answer was that the mind contributes the forms (space, time, the categories) under which anything can be experienced. The Transcendental Dialectic, of which the Paralogisms form part, diagnoses the illusions reason falls into when it applies those forms beyond possible experience. "Rational psychology", the school doctrine descended from Descartes and Leibniz and systematized by Christian Wolff, claimed to prove from "I think" alone that the soul is a substance, simple, a person, and distinct from the body. Kant takes each proof apart. He rewrote the Paralogisms almost entirely for the second edition of 1787, and the elastic-balls note appears only in the first. That is why the compendium draws on Kemp Smith's 1929 translation, which gives both editions, not on Meiklejohn's.

## Original Position

**The "I think".** "It must be possible for the 'I think' to accompany all my representations; for otherwise something would be represented in me which could not be thought at all, and that is equivalent to saying that the representation would be impossible, or at least would be nothing to me" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:7146-7153].

**A bare consciousness = X.** Rational psychology has "no other basis for this teaching than the simple, and in itself completely empty, representation 'I'; and we cannot even say that this is a concept, but only that it is a bare consciousness which accompanies all concepts. Through this I or he or it (the thing) which thinks, nothing further is represented than a transcendental subject of the thoughts = X. It is known only through the thoughts which are its predicates, and of it, apart from them, we cannot have any concept whatsoever, but can only revolve in a perpetual circle, since any judgment upon it has always already made use of its representation" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:15489-15502].

**The Third Paralogism.** The syllogism runs: "That which is conscious of the numerical identity of itself at different times is in so far a person. Now the soul is conscious, etc. Therefore it is a person" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:15967-15971]. Kant grants that the premise holds from the inside: "In my own consciousness, therefore, identity of person is unfailingly met with" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:15990-15991].

**The outside observer.** "But if I view myself from the standpoint of another person (as object of his outer intuition), it is this outer observer who first represents me in time". He admits the "I" that accompanies all my representations, but "he will draw no inference from this to the objective permanence of myself" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:15991-16015].

**Only a formal condition.** "The identity of the consciousness of myself at different times is therefore only a formal condition of my thoughts and their coherence, and in no way proves the numerical identity of my subject. Despite the logical identity of the 'I', such a change may have occurred in it as does not allow of the retention of its identity, and yet we may ascribe to it the same-sounding 'I', which in every different state, even in one involving change of the [thinking] subject, might still retain the thought of the preceding subject and so hand it over to the subsequent subject" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:16017-16026].

**The elastic balls (A363–364 note).** "An elastic ball which impinges on another similar ball in a straight line communicates to the latter its whole motion, and therefore its whole state (that is, if we take account only of the positions in space). If, then, in analogy with such bodies, we postulate substances such that the one communicates to the other representations together with the consciousness of them, we can conceive a whole series of substances of which the first transmits its state together with its consciousness to the second, the second its own state with that of the preceding substance to the third, and this in turn the states of all the preceding substances together with its own consciousness and with their consciousness to another. The last substance would then be conscious of all the states of the previously changed substances, as being its own states, because they would have been transferred to it together with the consciousness of them. And yet it would not have been one and the same person in all these states" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:16033-16047].

**What we cannot know.** "For we are unable from our own consciousness to determine whether, as souls, we are permanent or not. Since we reckon as belonging to our identical self only that of which we are conscious, we must necessarily judge that we are one and the same throughout the whole time of which we are conscious. We cannot, however, claim that this judgment would be valid from the standpoint of an outside observer" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:16054-16061]. And the flux doctrine of "certain ancient schools", that nothing is permanent, "is not refuted by the unity of self-consciousness" [L:kant-critique-pure-reason-kemp-smith-1929:16028-16031].

## Grounding

The Kantian "I" is grounded in **capacity**: the capacity for the synthetic unity of apperception, the combining of representations in one consciousness, without which there is no experience at all. But Kant's point is that this capacity yields only a *formal* unity. It grounds nothing about the substance, the persistence or the identity of whatever has the capacity. Personhood in the practical sense, the rational being as an end in itself, is grounded elsewhere in Kant's philosophy (see `kant-formula-of-humanity`), in rational agency, not in any metaphysical fact about a persisting soul. The first *Critique* thus separates two things the tradition had fused: the unity of consciousness, which is certain but formal, and the identity of a substance, which is unknowable.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The elastic balls describe state handoff (D1, D3, D5).** Kant's series of substances, each transmitting "its state together with its consciousness" to the next, is the clearest classical description of what agent systems do: a context handed from one instance to another, a memory store loaded into a new process, a model distilled into its successor with its dispositions intact. Kant's verdict is precise. The last in the series would be conscious of all the earlier states "as being its own states". That is, it would *seem to itself* to be the one who had them, "And yet it would not have been one and the same person." For agents, this means that an instance's sense that the transcript is "mine" is exactly what we should expect whether or not it is the same agent, and so it is no evidence either way.
- **The formal "I" is what an agent's "I" most plausibly is (D8).** Kant's "I" is "a bare consciousness which accompanies all concepts", "a transcendental subject of the thoughts = X", known only through its thoughts. An agent's use of "I" accompanies its outputs, unifies them into one perspective, and refers to nothing it can independently inspect. Whether there is consciousness behind it is D8. But at the level of *function*, Kant's formal "I" is a precise description of the role "I" plays in an agent's text.

### Strains

- **The outside observer has the better view of agents.** For humans, Kant's outside observer has no access to what persists in me, and must draw "no inference" to my objective permanence. For artificial agents, the outside observer (an operator or engineer) knows exactly what persisted: which weights, which context, which process. The observer's view is not the weaker one. It is the one with the facts. Kant's asymmetry, certainty from inside and agnosticism from outside, is inverted. The agent is agnostic about its own substance (D8, D11), while outsiders know it, and still do not know whether any of it constitutes a person.

### Breaks

- **"Unfailingly met with" is not unfailing for agents.** Kant says that in my own consciousness identity of person is "unfailingly met with": I cannot experience myself as other than the one who has my representations. An agent's sense of continuity, if it has one, depends on its context. The same transcript can be presented as its own history or as another's (see `advaita-witness-self`). For an agent, then, even the *formal* identity Kant thought guaranteed from within can be set from outside. This breaks the one part of the Third Paralogism Kant thought secure.

### New

- **The question Kant says we cannot answer becomes an engineering specification.** Kant held that we are "unable from our own consciousness to determine whether, as souls, we are permanent or not", and that there is no fact accessible to us about whether a change of substance has occurred behind the "same-sounding 'I'". For agents, whether the substance changed is a known and chosen fact: whether the weights were swapped, whether the process was restarted, whether the context was transferred. What Kant placed beyond knowledge, agent design decides. Kant's lesson then applies in reverse. Knowing all the facts about substance still does not tell us whether the result is "one and the same person", because Kant showed that the formal unity of consciousness does not settle it.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Descartes and rational psychology** (see `descartes-thinking-thing`) [E:descartes-thinking-thing]. The "I think" reveals a thinking substance. Kant's "= X" gives up what the cogito plainly delivers.
- [contested] **Hume** (see `hume-bundle`) [E:hume-bundle]. Kant's formal unity is a name for the problem, not a solution. If the "I" is empty, the bundle theory stands.
- [contested] **Reid** (see `reid-brave-officer`) [E:reid-brave-officer]. The identity of the self is known immediately and is a real, not formal, identity. Kant's skepticism about the substance is unwarranted.
- [contested] **Parfit** (see `parfit-reductionism`) [E:parfit-reductionism]. Kant's elastic balls show that psychological continuity can hold without identity, and Parfit argues that this continuity, not identity, is what matters. On Parfit's view the last ball has what matters, even if it is not the same person.
- [contested] **Advaita** (see `advaita-witness-self`) [E:advaita-witness-self]. Kant is right that the witness is never an object, and wrong to call it empty. The witness is consciousness itself, the one thing that cannot be doubted.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1781–1787, rewritten.** [driver: argument] The chapters on the Transcendental Deduction and the Paralogisms gave Kant the greatest difficulty, and he rewrote both completely for the second edition; the first-edition attack on the Paralogisms contains his fullest remarks on consciousness of and reference to the self [P:brook-wuerth-sep-2020:0].
- **19th century, the model of the mind.** [driver: argument] Kant's model of the mind was dominant in the empirical psychology that grew from his work [P:brook-wuerth-sep-2020:1].
- **1910–1965, eclipsed.** [driver: fashion] Behaviourism reigned supreme for roughly half a century, and Kant's model of the mind went into hiatus [P:brook-wuerth-sep-2020:1].
- **Late 20th century to present, the architecture of cognitive science.** [driver: argument] Kant held a functionalist view of the mind almost two hundred years before Putnam, and central elements of models as different as Freud's and Fodor's are broadly Kantian [P:brook-wuerth-sep-2020:1]. Other ideas equally central to his view had little influence [P:brook-wuerth-sep-2020:0].
- **In the corpus.** [driver: argument] James argued with Kant's unity of apperception in his chapter on the self [E:james-stream-of-thought], and Parfit took the elastic balls as showing that psychological continuity can hold without identity (see Counter-Positions).

### Measured

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Within a session, the formal "I think" that accompanies an agent's representations is in place as much as in any thinker. Across sessions, Kant's point is that nothing follows from it about a persisting subject, and a session-bound agent shows that plainly (D3).
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** The elastic balls, each passing its states and its consciousness of them to the next, describe a memory store handed from one process to its successor. Kant's conclusion, that the last is conscious of all the states as its own without being the same person, applies directly (D1, D5).
- **Forked: stronger.** Copying an agent's full state into concurrent instances (D1, D2) is the elastic-ball case in parallel rather than in series. Each instance has the unity of apperception, and none has thereby proved it is the original.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** Training on its own outputs changes what the agent's representations are like, not the formal condition that some "I think" can accompany them. Kant's framework allows the change but says nothing about whether the result is the same agent (D5, D11).

## Open Questions

1. If an agent is conscious of transferred states "as being its own states", is that evidence of anything? Kant says it is not evidence of identity. Is it evidence of continuity that matters?
2. Does knowing all the facts about which weights, context and process persisted settle whether an agent is "one and the same person"? If not, what further fact could there be?
3. Is the "I" in an agent's output Kant's formal "I" (a function that unifies a perspective) with nothing behind it, or with an X behind it that is as unknowable as ours?
4. If the formal identity Kant thought "unfailingly met with" can be set from outside for agents, does that show his account was only ever a description of human psychology?
