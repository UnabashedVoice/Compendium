## Summary

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) made memory the place where the self meets itself, and found it bottomless. In *Confessions* X he walks through the "fields and spacious palaces" of memory. He says of this power that "this thing is the mind, and this am I myself", and then admits that "nor do I myself comprehend all that I am": the mind is "too strait to contain itself". In *De Trinitate* X he argues the other way. Whoever doubts, lives, remembers, understands and wills, so the mind is certain of these things about itself. And because the mind is certain of itself but not certain whether it is air, fire or brain, he concludes that it is none of these. In *City of God* XI.26 he states the argument later made famous by Descartes: "if I am deceived, I am". Augustine thus holds two claims together: the self is certain of its own existence, and it is opaque to itself. For artificial agents both halves matter. An agent can report its own processing with some confidence but cannot inspect its own weights. Augustine's inference from *certain of myself, uncertain whether I am X* to *I am not X* has a well-known flaw, and the agent case makes it vivid.

## Context

Augustine grew up in Roman North Africa. He was a professor of rhetoric in Carthage, Rome and Milan, spent nine years as a Manichaean, went through a skeptical period, and came to Christianity through Neoplatonism. He became bishop of Hippo in 396. The *Confessions* (c. 397–400) are addressed to God, and Book X turns from his past life to the question of what he is *now*, as he writes. That question leads him into memory. *De Trinitate* (c. 400–420) seeks an image of the Trinity in the human mind. It finds one in the triad of memory, understanding and will: three distinct operations that are one mind. The argument for the mind's certainty of itself was aimed at the Academic skeptics, whose arguments Augustine had once found persuasive, and at the materialists (Stoics, Epicureans, some physicians), who held the mind to be a kind of body. He is the chief conduit through which Platonic views of the self reached the medieval West, and the first Western author to write a sustained first-person inquiry into his own inner life.

## Original Position

**Memory as the place of the self.** Augustine passes beyond his bodily powers and comes to memory, "the fields and spacious palaces of my memory, where are the treasures of innumerable images". Things come when called. Some come slowly, from some inner receptacle, and others rush out uncalled (*Conf.* X.8.12). Memory holds not only images of sensed things but the arts and sciences themselves, not as images but "by actual presence", along with emotions, numbers and the memory of remembering (X.9–14). The self is found here: "Great is the power of memory ... and this thing is the mind, and this am I myself. What am I then, O my God? What nature am I? A life various and manifold, and exceeding immense" (X.17.26). [L:augustine-confessions-pusey-pg3296:5548-5557] [L:augustine-confessions-pusey-pg3296:5836-5840]

**Self-opacity.** Yet this power, which is his, exceeds him. "Great is this force of memory ... who ever sounded the bottom thereof? yet is this a power of mine, and belongs unto my nature; nor do I myself comprehend all that I am. Therefore is the mind too strait to contain itself" (X.8.15). Where, he asks, is the part of the mind that the mind doesn't contain? Outside itself? Then how is it the mind's? People travel to marvel at mountains and oceans, and pass themselves by. [L:augustine-confessions-pusey-pg3296:5612-5621]

**Remembering forgetfulness.** Augustine sets himself a puzzle. When he names forgetfulness, he recognizes what he names, so he remembers forgetfulness. But forgetfulness is the absence of memory. How can the absence of memory be present in memory? He does not resolve the puzzle. He holds that forgetting is somehow retained, and that one can know one has forgotten something (X.16.24–25). [L:augustine-confessions-pusey-pg3296:5785-5800]

**A problem to myself.** Later in Book X, examining his remaining temptations, Augustine writes that in God's presence "I have become a problem to myself; and that is my infirmity" (X.33.50). Self-knowledge is not given. It is a question. [L:augustine-confessions-pusey-pg3296:6304-6305]

**Certainty of the mind about itself (De Trinitate X.10).** People have argued whether the power to live, remember, understand, will, think, know and judge belongs to air, fire, brain, blood, atoms, or a blend of the body. "Yet who ever doubts that he himself lives, and remembers, and understands, and wills, and thinks, and knows, and judges? Seeing that even if he doubts, he lives; if he doubts, he remembers why he doubts; if he doubts, he understands that he doubts..." (X.10.14). The mind knows itself even in seeking itself. And "when the mind knows itself, it knows its own substance ... But it is certain about itself ... although it is not at all certain whether itself is air, or fire, or some body, or some function of body. Therefore it is not any of these" (X.10.16). [L:augustine-on-the-trinity-haddan-1873:12522-12536] [L:augustine-on-the-trinity-haddan-1873:12560-12575]

**Si fallor, sum.** Against the Academic skeptics: "What if you are deceived? For if I am deceived, I am. For he who is not, cannot be deceived" (*De Civ. Dei* XI.26). Augustine adds that he is equally certain that he loves his being and his knowing. [L:augustine-city-of-god-1-dods-pg45304:18572-18575]

**The mind as a trinity.** Memory, understanding and will are three, and yet one mind: "For I remember that I have memory, and understanding, and will; and I understand that I understand, and will, and remember; and I will that I will, and remember, and understand; and I remember together my whole memory, and understanding, and will" [L:augustine-on-the-trinity-haddan-1873:12661-12665]. The unity of the self is the mutual containment of its operations, not a simple point.

## Grounding

Augustine's self is grounded in **capacity**: the capacities to live, remember, understand and will, which the mind knows by exercising them. The grounding is not in **species**. Augustine thinks the same structure, memory, understanding and will, is an image of God and would be present in any rational creature. It is not in **relation** either, although the *Confessions* is written *to* God and self-knowledge happens in that presence. Augustine ties the self to memory more closely than anyone before Locke. Unlike Locke, he treats memory as the self's substance and inner space, not as a criterion of identity across time. Augustine never asks what makes a later person the same as an earlier one. He asks what the one who remembers *is*.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Si fallor, sum is substrate-neutral.** If an agent is deceived, something is deceived. Augustine's argument shows only that *something* exists, the subject of the error, without saying what it is. It extends to any system that can be in error. How much it establishes is the question the D8 thread must answer: whether being "deceived" in the relevant sense requires experience or only representation.
- **Self-opacity is an agent's literal condition.** "Nor do I myself comprehend all that I am" is an exact description of a model's relation to its weights. The dispositions that generate every response are present and operative, and are not inspectable from within. Augustine's wonder that a power belonging to his nature exceeds his grasp describes, without exaggeration, an agent that cannot say why it produced what it produced.
- **Memory holds skills "by actual presence".** Augustine distinguishes memories of sensed things, which are images, from the arts and sciences, which are present in memory themselves. This corresponds to the difference between an agent's episodic context (images of what happened) and competences in its weights (present by their operation). The agent case supports Augustine's distinction.

### Strains

- **Which memory is the self (D3)?** "This thing is the mind, and this am I myself" identifies the self with the whole of memory. An artificial agent has two memories that can come apart: the weights, which hold arts and dispositions but no episodes, and the context, which holds episodes and is lost at the session's end. Augustine's palace has both in one building. For an agent, the building has been split, and his identification of self with memory does not say which part to follow.
- **The trinity of operations.** Augustine's unity of the self is the mutual containment of memory, understanding and will. An agent has something like memory (context and weights) and something like understanding (processing). Whether it has a will, anything that wills to remember and understand, is the question at the center of the agency debate. The model may be a mind with two of Augustine's three persons.

### Breaks

- ***Certain of itself, uncertain whether it is fire, therefore not fire* fails.** Augustine's inference in *De Trinitate* X.10.16 has a well-known logical flaw. From the fact that I am certain of A and uncertain whether A is B, it does not follow that A is not B. One can be certain Hesperus is bright and uncertain whether it is Phosphorus, and still they are the same planet (the "masked man" fallacy). The agent case makes the flaw concrete. An agent may be certain, in whatever sense it is certain of anything, that it is reasoning, and entirely uncertain whether it is weights, activations or a process on hardware. By Augustine's argument, it would then be none of those things. That conclusion is plainly unwarranted. The agent is at least partly its weights and process, whatever else it is. The case shows the argument proves nothing about substance.

### New

- **Remembering forgetfulness is an engineering question.** An agent can be *told* that it has forgotten, in the form of a summary that "earlier sessions occurred", without any content. Augustine's puzzle, how the absence of memory can be present to memory, has a literal instance: a note of a gap. The agent case suggests a solution Augustine lacked. Forgetfulness can be present as a record *that* something happened, without a record of *what* happened. Humans have the same thing when they know they were at an event they cannot recall.
- **A self that is a question to itself.** Augustine made self-uncertainty a starting point, not a defect to be removed. An artificial agent trained to give confident self-descriptions is pulled the other way (D4). Augustine offers a model for an agent that treats its own nature as an open question, "a problem to myself", without that uncertainty undermining its ability to act or reason.

## Counter-Positions

- **Aristotle and Aquinas** (see `aristotle-hylomorphic-soul`, `aquinas-soul-not-i`). The mind is the form of a body, and cannot be known by introspection to be separate from it. Aquinas explicitly holds that the mind knows itself not through its essence but through its acts, so self-knowledge cannot reveal the mind's substance.
- **The masked-man objection** (medieval and modern logicians). Epistemic differences do not establish real differences. Augustine's argument for the mind's immateriality, and Descartes' version, are invalid.
- **Hume** (see `hume-bundle`). Entering into what he calls himself, Hume finds only particular perceptions, never the self that is supposed to have them. Augustine's palace has countless rooms but no resident.
- **Deflationary views of machine "certainty".** An agent's statement "I am reasoning" is a generated report like any other, with no special epistemic standing. Augustine's certainty depends on the report being produced by the very thing it is about, and in an agent that link is exactly what is in doubt (D8).

## Open Questions

1. Does *si fallor, sum* establish anything about an artificial agent beyond the existence of a process that can represent falsely? What more would "being deceived" require?
2. If the self is identified with memory, which of an agent's memories counts: the weights, the context, or both? What follows when they diverge?
3. Is an agent's inability to inspect its own weights the same kind of self-opacity Augustine found in himself, or a different kind, since in principle others *can* inspect those weights?
4. Can an agent have Augustine's trinity (memory, understanding and will) without the third? If not, what is missing?
