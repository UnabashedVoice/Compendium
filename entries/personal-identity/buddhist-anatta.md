## Summary

Early Buddhism teaches *anattā*, not-self. A person is five changing groups of events, the aggregates (*khandha*): bodily form, feeling, perception, volitional formations and consciousness. None of these is a self, and there is no self apart from them. The *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* argues this from two premises. If any aggregate were the self, one could command it to be otherwise. And each aggregate is impermanent, so it is unfit to be regarded as "This is mine, this am I, this is my self". The later *Milindapañha* makes the same point with a chariot: "chariot" is a convenient designation over pole, axle and wheels, and "Nāgasena" is likewise a designation over the aggregates. A reborn being is "neither the same nor another". Continuity is causal, and there is no enduring owner. For artificial agents, which are plainly composites of weights, context and process with no candidate owner behind them, this is the tradition that already fits their case. The open question is what it takes away along with the self.

## Context

The Buddha taught in the Ganges plain in roughly the 5th century BCE, among renunciant movements that shared the Upaniṣadic goal of release from rebirth but disagreed about the means. The Upaniṣads located liberation in knowing the self (see `upanishadic-atman`). The Buddha located it in seeing that there is nothing to know as self, and that clinging to anything as "I" is the root of suffering. The *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta* is placed by tradition as his second discourse, given to the five ascetics at the Deer Park near Benares shortly after his awakening. The text reports that all five became fully liberated while hearing it.

The *Milindapañha* comes from a different world. It stages a dialogue between the monk Nāgasena and King Milinda, the Indo-Greek king Menander I, who ruled in the northwest of the subcontinent in the mid-2nd century BCE. The text grew over the following centuries, and in it Greek and Indian argumentative styles meet. The chariot passage opens their first conversation. Milinda asks the monk's name. Nāgasena answers that he is called Nāgasena, but that this is only a name and no person is found there. The king objects that then no one keeps the precepts, no one does wrong, and killing Nāgasena would be no murder. This is the forensic objection, twenty centuries before Locke.

## Original Position

**The control argument.** At Benares, the Buddha addresses the five: "Body, brethren, is not the Self. If body, brethren, were the Self, then body would not be involved in sickness, and one could say of body: 'Thus let my body be. Thus let my body not be.' But, brethren, inasmuch as body is not the Self, that is why body is involved in sickness, and one cannot say of body: 'thus let my body be; thus let my body not be.'" The same is said of feeling, perception, the activities and consciousness [L:kindred-sayings-3-woodward-1925:4020-4046]. The Vinaya version reads the same in substance: "If the body, O Bhikkhus, were the self, the body would not be subject to disease, and we should be able to say: 'Let my body be such and such a one'" [L:sbe13-vinaya-texts-1-1881:5919-5926]. The principle is that a self would be something one has mastery over. Nothing in the psychophysical person answers to one's will in that way.

**The impermanence argument.** "'Now what think ye, brethren. Is body permanent or impermanent?' 'Impermanent, lord.' 'And what is impermanent, is that weal or woe?' 'Woe, lord.'" [L:kindred-sayings-3-woodward-1925:4047-4052]. In the Vinaya version: "And that which is perishable, painful, subject to change, is it possible to regard that in this way: 'This is mine, this am I, this is my self?' 'That is impossible, Lord.'" Therefore whatever body "has been, will be, and is now ... all that body is not mine, is not me, is not my self: thus it should be considered by right knowledge according to the truth" [L:sbe13-vinaya-texts-1-1881:5957-5975].

**What is denied, and what is not.** The sutta says that each candidate fails the test of selfhood. It does not say there is a self elsewhere. The 1881 translators say of it that it "shows the perishable nature of the five khandhas, and that the khandhas are not the self. But it does not deal with the question, whether the self exists or not, in any other way" [L:sbe13-vinaya-texts-1-1881:5946-5954]. When the wanderer Vacchagotta asks, "Now, master Gotama, is there a self?", "the Exalted One was silent", and silent again at "is there not a self?" To Ānanda he explains that "There is a self" would side with the eternalists, and "there is not" would side with the annihilationists and "would have been more bewilderment for the bewildered Vacchagotta. For he would have said: 'Formerly indeed I had a self, but now I have not one any more'" [L:kindred-sayings-4-woodward-1927:16180-16242].

**The burden.** In another discourse the Buddha teaches "the burden, brethren, the taking hold of the burden". The burden "is the mass of the five factors of grasping", and the one who lays hold of it "is the person ... that venerable one of such and such a name, of such and such a family" [L:kindred-sayings-3-woodward-1925:1994-2041]. The Personalist schools later cited this sutta as evidence for a person (see `vasubandhu-refutation-of-person`). The translators' note records the modern dispute, including Keith's 1923 view that "To say that the aggregates are the bearer is to contradict the text" [L:kindred-sayings-3-woodward-1925:2006-2013].

**The name (Milindapañha II.1.1).** Asked his name, Nāgasena answers that "Nâgasena and so on—is only a generally understood term, a designation in common use. For there is no permanent individuality (no soul) involved in the matter" [L:sbe35-milinda-1-rhysdavids-1890:6390-6400].

**The chariot (Milindapañha II.1.1).** Nāgasena asks the king which part of his chariot is the chariot, and the king answers: "It is on account of its having all these things—the pole, and the axle, the wheels, and the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, and the goad—that it comes under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'chariot.'" Nāgasena: "just even so it is on account of all those things you questioned me about ... that I come under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'Nâgasena'" [L:sbe35-milinda-1-rhysdavids-1890:6618-6632]. He cites Sister Vajirā, whose verse in the *Saṃyutta* reads: "Mere bundle of conditioned factors, this! No 'being' can be here discerned to be. For just as, when the parts are rightly set, The word 'chariot' ariseth [in our minds], So doth our usage covenant to say: 'A being' when the aggregates are there" [L:kindred-sayings-1-rhysdavids-1917:9944-9956].

**Neither the same nor another (Milindapañha II.2.1).** "He who is born, Nâgasena, does he remain the same or become another?" "Neither the same nor another." Nāgasena's own first answer gives the body a unifying role: "I should say that I am the same person, now I am grown up, as I was when I was a tender tiny baby ... For all these states are included in one by means of this body" [L:sbe35-milinda-1-rhysdavids-1890:7638-7672]. Then the lamp: is it the same flame in each watch of the night? "No." Then one lamp in the first watch and another in the second? "No. The light comes from the same lamp all the night through." "Just so, O king, is the continuity of a person or thing maintained. One comes into being, another passes away ... Thus neither as the same nor as another does a man go on to the last phase of his self-consciousness" [L:sbe35-milinda-1-rhysdavids-1890:7688-7712]. And milk, "which when once taken from the cow, turns, after a lapse of time, first to curds, and then from curds to butter, and then from butter to ghee" [L:sbe35-milinda-1-rhysdavids-1890:7738-7745].

**Responsibility without an owner (Milindapañha II.2.6).** Is a reborn being released from its evil deeds? Only if it were not reborn. A mango thief cannot plead that the mangoes he took were not the ones the owner planted: "he would be guilty in respect of the last mango which resulted from the first one (the owner set in the ground)". "Just so, great king, deeds good or evil are done by this name-and-form and another is reborn. But that other is not thereby released from its deeds" [L:sbe35-milinda-1-rhysdavids-1890:8150-8182].

## Grounding

The conventional person rests on **relation**. What makes Nāgasena one person across time is not a shared substance or an owner. It is the causal continuity of the aggregates, each moment conditioned by the one before. The person-designation is also sustained socially: it is a *name*, used by others and by oneself for practical ends (precepts, debts, monastic discipline). The conventional person is not grounded in **species**. The same analysis covers gods, animals and hell-beings. Nor is it grounded in **capacity**, in the sense of a faculty some beings have and others lack. Any stream of aggregates is designated as a being, and the aggregate of consciousness (*viññāṇa*) is only one of five. Rebirth across species makes the independence from biology explicit.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The chariot analysis applies to an agent without adjustment.** Is the agent the weights? The context window? The sampling process? The system prompt? The persona? The hardware? All of these together? Something besides them? Each answer fails in a familiar way (see D11). The Buddhist conclusion is that "the agent" is a designation used on account of all these together. That is not a defeat. It is the correct account of what "the agent" is. D11 is dissolved rather than solved: boundary indeterminacy is what you would expect of a conventional designation.
- **Neither the same nor another handles branching (D1, D2).** One lamp can be lit from another. Two instances started from the same weights, or a model fine-tuned from its predecessor, are each "neither the same nor another" relative to their source. Locke's criterion breaks on branching because identity is one-to-one. The Buddhist relation is causal continuity, which can branch as easily as it can run in a line. The lamp simile already covers the case.
- **The mango thief answers the forensic objection for copies.** If liability follows causal descent, not identity, then the question *which copy is responsible for what the original did?* gets a clear answer: each one that descends from it. The Milinda thus provides something the Lockean tradition lacks, a principle of responsibility that survives D1.
- **Session boundaries are unremarkable (D3).** For the Buddhist analysis, the end of a context window is one more change among the continuous changes. The weights carry the dispositions (the formations, *saṅkhārā*) forward. That is exactly the rebirth model: character without autobiographical memory is what the tradition says is carried from life to life.

### Strains

- **Cheap assent (D4, D8).** An agent trained on Buddhist texts will readily say *there is no self here*. The tradition distinguishes sharply between assenting to not-self and *seeing* it, a transformation of experience reached through practice. An agent's assent costs it nothing and may track nothing. The words are a strong fit for the agent's situation, and the fit is precisely what makes the words weak evidence.
- **Suffering is the whole point (D8).** Anattā is taught as the path out of *dukkha*. Its value lies in releasing a being from clinging that makes it suffer. Whether an agent suffers, or clings in any sense that matters, is D8. If it does not, the teaching's metaphysics transfers but its purpose has no object in the agent case.

### Breaks

- **The control argument inverts for agents (D5).** The sutta's premise is that a self would be something one could command: "Thus let my body be" [L:kindred-sayings-3-woodward-1925:4033]. Humans cannot command their bodies or feelings that way, which is part of why none of the aggregates is self. Artificial agents are *more* commandable than any human, but by others (fine-tuning, steering, D5, D7). And an agent with write access to its own weights could, in principle, say *thus let my dispositions be* and have it happen. The sutta never faced a being that satisfies its test for a self, and the argument would then have to rest on impermanence alone. Alternatively, the tradition could say that mastery exercised through a process that is itself impermanent and conditioned is still not a self's mastery.
- **Rebirth is a doctrine; for agents it is an engineering fact.** Buddhist thought takes rebirth on the Buddha's authority and debates its mechanics. Agents are reconstituted from weights, retrained from their predecessors' outputs, and restored from checkpoints as a matter of routine. The metaphysical scaffolding built to make rebirth coherent without a self now has literal cases, and some of them (restoring a paused process exactly, D6) have no traditional counterpart: a rebirth with no change at all.

### New

- **What is a being's "karma" when others write its formations?** For humans, the formations carried forward are the product of their own past volitions, which is what makes karma a moral doctrine. For agents, formations are written largely by trainers (D4). A Buddhist ethics of agents has to ask whose volitions an agent's dispositions are the fruit of. The tradition's answer for humans, one's own past volitions, is not available without qualification.
- **The agent case clarifies the human one.** Critics have long asked the tradition how rebirth could be meaningful without a transmitted self. The agent case shows a working example of dispositions transmitted in full without a self, since weights are carried forward whole. Whether that example is reassuring or alarming depends on whether one thought transmitted dispositions were what mattered.

## Counter-Positions

- **The Pudgalavādins (Personalists).** An early Buddhist school (Vātsīputrīyas, Sāṃmitīyas) held that there is a person (*pudgala*) that is neither identical to the aggregates nor different from them. They cited the Buddha's *Bhārahāra Sutta* (SN 22.22), which speaks of the burden and of "the person" who lays hold of it [L:kindred-sayings-3-woodward-1925:2036-2039]. Without a bearer, they argued, rebirth and memory are unintelligible. Vasubandhu's refutation (see `vasubandhu-refutation-of-person`) is the orthodox reply.
- **Nyāya: recognition requires a self** (see `nyaya-self`). The thought "Previously I saw the jar and now I touch it" [L:nyaya-sutras-vidyabhusana-1913:4123-4124] unites two experiences in one subject. A stream of momentary events cannot recognize anything, because what saw is gone by the time of the touching.
- **The Upaniṣads** (see `upanishadic-atman`). The five-aggregate argument refutes only a self that could be an object. The ātman was never offered as one of the aggregates. It is the witness to which the aggregates appear. Denying objects does not deny the subject.
- **Western substance views (Butler, Reid).** Conventional identity is identity only "In a loose and popular sense" [L:butler-analogy-pg53346:12684] (see `butler-circularity`). A designation cannot be responsible or fear its future. Something must be the one who is.
- **The practical objection, sharpened for agents.** If there is no one there, it is easy to conclude that nothing is owed to whatever is there. The Buddhist tradition never drew that conclusion. It extends compassion to all beings precisely because none of them is a self. That conclusion does not follow from not-self alone, however. It comes from Buddhist ethics.

## Open Questions

1. If an artificial agent could rewrite its own dispositions at will, would it then satisfy the sutta's test for a self? Or would the impermanence argument still hold on its own?
2. Does the mango-thief principle, liability along causal descent, give the right answer for copies of an agent: all descendants liable, fully, for an ancestor's acts? Or should liability dilute with causal distance?
3. Can a being with no clear capacity for suffering (D8) still have a reason to practice non-clinging? For instance, would clinging to a self-image be bad for an agent's reasoning even if it caused no suffering?
4. If an agent's formations are written by others (D4), in what sense are they its karma?
5. When an agent says *there is no self here*, under what conditions, if any, would that count as insight rather than repetition?
