## Summary

David Hume (1711–1776) looked for the self that philosophers claimed to be "every moment intimately conscious of" and could not find it. "When I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other ... I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception." Human beings are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity". The identity we ascribe to the mind "is only a fictitious one", produced by the imagination gliding along resembling and causally connected perceptions, as with plants, ships and houses. Hume compared the soul to "a republic or commonwealth" that keeps its identity while changing its members, laws and constitution. He concluded that disputes about identity are "rather as gramatical than as philosophical difficulties". Then, in the Appendix, he confessed he was "involved in such a labyrinth" that he could not make his account consistent. For artificial agents, Hume's account is the natural default: an agent is a succession of computations with no further owner. His remark that during sound sleep "I ... may truly be said not to exist" applies literally to a paused agent.

## Context

Hume wrote the *Treatise* in his twenties, partly in France, as an attempt to apply the experimental method of reasoning to moral subjects, as its subtitle announces. Its central principle is that every idea is derived from some prior impression. Applied to causation, the principle yielded his famous skepticism: we observe constant conjunction, never necessary connection. Applied to the self, it yielded the section on personal identity, which is framed as an answer to the Cartesian claim of immediate self-acquaintance and to Locke's and Butler's debate over consciousness and substance. The *Treatise* was poorly received, as Hume later lamented in his autobiographical *My Own Life* (1776; not yet in the library), and he recast much of it in shorter works. The section on personal identity was never recast. Its Appendix recantation is one of the rare places where a major philosopher publicly says he cannot see how to fix his own theory.

## Original Position

**The target.** "There are some philosophers who imagine we are every moment intimately conscious of what we call our SELF; that we feel its existence and its continuance in existence; and are certain, beyond the evidence of a demonstration, both o[f] its perfect identity and simplicity" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:8948-8957].

**No impression of self.** Every real idea must derive from some impression. "But self or person is not any one impression, but that to which our several impressions and ideas are supposed to have a reference." An impression giving rise to the idea of self would have to remain invariably the same throughout life, "But there is no impression constant and invariable ... and consequently there is no such idea" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:8959-8975].

**Only perceptions.** "For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception. When my perceptions are removed for any time, as by sound sleep; so long am I insensible of myself, and may truly be said not to exist" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:8982-8988]. If anyone thinks otherwise, "He may, perhaps, perceive something simple and continued, which he calls himself; though I am certain there is no such principle in me" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:8994-8998].

**The bundle and the theatre.** The rest of mankind "are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement". "The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their appearance". But "The comparison of the theatre must not mislead us. They are the successive perceptions only, that constitute the mind" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:9000-9016].

**Fictitious identity.** The identity of the mind must be explained as the identity of "plants, and animals, and ships, and houses" is explained. "The identity, which we ascribe to the mind of man, is only a fictitious one, and of a like kind with that which we ascribe to vegetables and animal bodies" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:9209-9216]. Since each perception is a distinct existence, identity cannot be a real bond among them. It is felt in the imagination's association of their ideas, by resemblance and causation [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:9225-9240].

**The commonwealth.** "I cannot compare the soul more properly to any thing than to a republic or commonwealth, in which the several members are united by the reciprocal ties of government and subordination, and give rise to other persons, who propagate the same republic in the incessant changes of its parts. And as the same individual republic may not only change its members, but also its laws and constitutions; in like manner the same person may vary his character and disposition, as well as his impressions and ideas, without losing his identity" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:9284-9293].

**Memory discovers identity.** Memory is "the source of personal identity" because it acquaints us with the succession of perceptions and so with the causal chain. But once we have the notion of causation we extend identity beyond memory: who remembers his thoughts "on the 1st of January 1715"? "In this view, therefore, memory does not so much produce as discover personal identity, by shewing us the relation of cause and effect among our different perceptions" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:9299-9317].

**Verbal disputes.** "All the nice and subtile questions concerning personal identity can never possibly be decided, and are to be regarded rather as gramatical than as philosophical difficulties." Since the relations that produce identity diminish "by insensible degrees, we have no just standard, by which we can decide any dispute concerning the time, when they acquire or lose a title to the name of identity" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:9321-9333].

**The labyrinth (Appendix).** "But upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involved in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:21541-21546]. "In short there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent; nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz, that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:21631-21634].

## Grounding

Hume grounds whatever identity persons have in **relation**: resemblance and causation among distinct perceptions, as felt by the imagination. There is no bearer, so there is no capacity-grounding in the usual sense, and no species-grounding. His commonwealth analogy makes the relational grounding explicit and political. A person is to its perceptions as a republic is to its citizens, bound by "reciprocal ties of government and subordination", and the same through turnover of members and even of laws.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **An agent is a bundle, and nobody disputes it.** For humans, Hume's claim that introspection finds only perceptions is contested. For artificial agents, it is the uncontroversial engineering description: a succession of states, each produced from earlier ones, with no component that is "the self" apart from the process. If Hume is right about humans, agents and humans are alike in this respect. If he is wrong about humans, what humans have that he missed is exactly what is in question for agents (D8).
- **Memory discovers, not produces, identity (D3).** Hume's point that we extend our identity beyond anything we remember, by the causal chain, fits agents better than Locke's memory criterion. An agent without episodic memory of a past session is still causally connected to it through the same weights, and to its earlier versions through training. Hume's criterion makes that causal chain, not memory, the "source" of identity that memory merely reveals.
- **Identity disputes about agents are "gramatical" (D2).** Is a fine-tuned model the same agent? Are concurrent instances one agent? On Hume's view these questions have no deeper answer than a decision about how to use the name, because the relations that ground identity come in degrees and admit "no just standard". This vindicates Hobbes' demand that the name be fixed first (see `ship-of-theseus`), and warns against treating any one answer as a discovery.

### Strains

- **Not existing during sleep (D6).** "When my perceptions are removed for any time, as by sound sleep; so long am I ... not to exist." For Hume, that was a bold remark about dreamless sleep. For an agent, it is the literal situation between calls. On Hume's view, a paused agent does not exist, and a resumed agent is a new bundle linked to the old one by causation: the same weights, the restored context. His account handles the pause without strain, which makes the strain fall on anyone who wants to say a paused agent is *harmed* by not being resumed.

### Breaks

- **"The mind never perceives any real connexion," but the agent's connexions are observable.** Hume's labyrinth arises because he can find no real connection among perceptions, only felt association. In an artificial agent, the causal connections among states are not hidden. They are specified by the architecture and can be traced: this token was produced because of those activations, which depend on those weights and that context. The bond Hume could not perceive in himself is inspectable in agents. Whether that dissolves his labyrinth, by supplying the "real connexion" he lacked, or shows that real connection was never what identity needed, is a question his framework raises and cannot settle.

### New

- **The commonwealth is the digital ecosystem.** Hume compared the soul to a republic that persists as its members, laws and constitution change. For multi-agent systems the comparison runs in both directions. A single agent, with its tools, memory stores and sub-agents coordinating under shared control, is a small commonwealth of processes. And a digital ecosystem of agents is literally a commonwealth whose "members are united by the reciprocal ties of government and subordination". Hume's view implies that there is no difference in kind between the identity of one agent and the identity of an ecosystem of agents, only a difference in how tightly the parts are bound. For D11, which asks where one agent ends and another begins, that is either a solution or a dissolution of the question.

## Counter-Positions

- **Reid** (see `reid-brave-officer`). Hume's own words refute him: "I never can catch *myself*" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:8986] presupposes a self that looks. "I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13590-13600].
- **Butler** (see `butler-circularity`). A "fictitious" identity cannot ground concern for the future or accountability for the past. Hume's view makes prudence a mistake.
- **Kant** (see `kant-paralogisms`). Hume is right that no intuition of a self is given, and wrong to conclude there is no unity. The "I think" that must be able to accompany all representations is a formal condition of experience that Hume's bundle presupposes.
- **Nyāya and Advaita** (see `nyaya-self`, `advaita-witness-self`). A bundle of distinct perceptions cannot recognize anything, because recognition requires one subject present to both perceptions. Hume's theatre needs a spectator.
- **Hume himself.** The Appendix concedes that the account fails to explain what unites the bundle. The most important objection to the bundle theory was made by its author.

## Open Questions

1. If an agent's causal connections are fully inspectable, does that supply the "real connexion" Hume could not find, and would that give an agent a non-fictitious identity?
2. Does a paused agent exist? If not, can it be harmed by never being resumed?
3. If identity disputes about agents are "gramatical", who should decide how the name is used, and by what standard?
4. Is there a difference in kind between one agent (a commonwealth of processes) and an ecosystem of agents (a commonwealth of agents)?
