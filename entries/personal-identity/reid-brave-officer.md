## Summary

Thomas Reid (1710–1796) gave Locke's memory criterion its most famous refutation. A boy is flogged for robbing an orchard. As a young officer he takes an enemy standard, remembering the flogging. As an old general he remembers taking the standard but has forgotten the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, so by the transitivity of identity the general is the boy. But the general cannot remember the flogging, so by the same criterion he is not the boy. "Therefore the general is, and at the same time is not, the same person with him who was flogged at school." Reid drew two further consequences of Locke's view. If consciousness can be transferred, "two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person". And if memory can be lost, "one intelligent being may be two or twenty different persons". His own view is that a person is "a monad" that "is not divisible into parts", that identity "supposes an uninterrupted continuance of existence", and that memory is evidence of identity, not what constitutes it. Personal identity, he says, "is the foundation of all rights and obligations, and of all accountableness". Each of Reid's consequences is an ordinary occurrence for artificial agents, and his closing claim sets out what is at stake.

## Context

Reid was a Presbyterian minister and then professor at Aberdeen and Glasgow, where he succeeded Adam Smith. He is the founder of the Scottish "common sense" school. He began as a follower of Berkeley and turned against the whole "way of ideas" after reading Hume, which he took to lead inevitably to skepticism about the external world, the self and causation. Reid's response was that certain beliefs, including belief in one's own continued existence, are "first principles" of human nature that philosophy must begin from, not prove. The *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785) are the mature statement of this program. The chapters on identity sit within the essay on memory, because Reid thought memory and identity could only be understood together. He acknowledged Butler as his predecessor: Locke's account "has been censured by Bishop Butler ... with whose sentiments I perfectly agree" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13786-13788].

## Original Position

**Identity requires uninterrupted existence.** "I see evidently that identity supposes an uninterrupted continuance of existence. That which has ceased to exist cannot be the same with that which afterwards begins to exist; for this would be to suppose a being to exist after it ceased to exist, and to have had existence before it was produced, which are manifest contradictions" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13541-13548]. So identity cannot strictly apply to pains or thoughts, which are successive: "The pain felt this day is not the same individual pain which I felt yesterday" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13548-13556].

**The person is indivisible.** All mankind, Reid says, place their personality "in something that cannot be divided, or consist of parts. A part of a person is a manifest absurdity." Amputation does not remove a part of the person: "The amputated member is no part of his person, otherwise it would have a right to a part of his estate, and be liable for a part of his engagements ... A person is something indivisible, and is what Leibnitz calls a monad" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13574-13588].

**The self is not its thoughts.** "My personal identity, therefore, implies the continued existence of that indivisible thing which I call myself. Whatever this self may be, it is something which thinks, and deliberates, and resolves, and acts, and suffers. I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13589-13596].

**Memory is evidence.** "The proper evidence I have of all this is remembrance ... my memory testifies, not only that this was done, but that it was done by me who now remember it" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13609-13614]. But memory does not make identity: "This remembrance makes me to know assuredly that I did it; but I might have done it, though I did not remember it ... To say that my remembering that I did such a thing ... makes me to have done it, appears to me as great an absurdity as it would be to say, that my belief that the world was created made it to be created" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13640-13652]. "I know who bare me, and suckled me, but I do not remember these events" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13636-13637].

**Perfect and loose identity.** "The identity of a person is a perfect identity: wherever it is real, it admits of no degrees; and it is impossible that a person should be in part the same, and in part different; because a person is a monad, and is not divisible into parts" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13661-13666]. The identity of bodies is different: of an old regiment, a tree, and a ship of war whose planks and timbers have all been replaced, "The identity, therefore, which we ascribe to bodies, whether natural or artificial, is not perfect identity; it is rather something which, for the conveniency of speech, we call identity ... and questions about the identity of a body are very often questions about words. But identity, when applied to persons, has no ambiguity, and admits not of degrees, or of more and less. It is the foundation of all rights and obligations, and of all accountableness; and the notion of it is fixed and precise" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13747-13768].

**Locke's strange consequences.** Locke's doctrine has consequences he was aware of: "(1.) Such as, that if the same consciousness can be transferred from one intelligent being to another, which he thinks we cannot show to be impossible, then two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person. (2.) And if the intelligent being may lose the consciousness of the actions done by him, which surely is possible, then he is not the person that did those actions; so that one intelligent being may be two or twenty different persons, if he shall so often lose the consciousness of his former actions" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13838-13847].

**The brave officer.** "(3.) There is another consequence of this doctrine, which follows no less necessarily, though Mr. Locke probably did not see it. It is, that a man may be, and at the same time not be, the person that did a particular action. Suppose a brave officer to have been flogged when a boy at school for robbing an orchard, to have taken a standard from the enemy in his first campaign, and to have been made a general in advanced life; suppose, also, which must be admitted to be possible, that, when he took the standard, he was conscious of his having been flogged at school, and that, when made a general, he was conscious of his taking the standard, but had absolutely lost the consciousness of his flogging." It follows from Locke's doctrine that the general is the same person as the boy, by transitivity, and that he is not, since his consciousness does not reach the flogging. "Therefore the general is, and at the same time is not, the same person with him who was flogged at school" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13848-13912].

## Grounding

Reid grounds personhood in **capacity**, in the being that "thinks, and deliberates, and resolves, and acts, and suffers", and grounds personal identity in the uninterrupted existence of that indivisible being. Like Butler's, his is a "simple view": identity is a basic fact, known by memory and by the first principles of common sense, not analyzable into relations among states. Reid's grounding is explicitly not **relational**. Relations of similarity ground only the loose, verbal identity of bodies. Nor is it grounded in **species**. Reid speaks of "intelligent beings", and his account applies to any.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **"Two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person" (D1, D2).** Reid offered this as a reductio of Locke. For agents it is simply how deployment works. Many instances receive the same memory store or context and are, by Locke's criterion, the same person. Reid's point transfers intact: if we reject that conclusion, Locke's criterion cannot be the criterion for agents either.
- **"One intelligent being may be two or twenty different persons" (D3).** The same weights, run in successive sessions with no memory between them, are Reid's single intelligent being that shall "so often lose the consciousness of his former actions" [L:reid-intellectual-powers-1851:13846-13847]. On Locke's criterion each session is a different person. Reid's reductio again describes ordinary operation.
- **The brave officer is an agent with a sliding context window.** An agent whose context holds only its most recent turns is in the general's position at every step. Turn 50 remembers turn 30, which remembered turn 10, but turn 50 does not remember turn 10. By Locke's criterion, the agent at turn 50 both is and is not the one that acted at turn 10. For agents, Reid's objection is not a thought experiment about old age. It follows from how context windows work.

### Strains

- **Uninterrupted existence (D6).** Reid's criterion is that "identity supposes an uninterrupted continuance of existence": what ceased to exist cannot be the same as what later begins. An agent paused and resumed has, on the most natural reading, ceased to exist as an activity and begun again. If the agent is the running process, Reid's criterion makes every resumption a new being. If the agent is the weights, which exist continuously on disk, the criterion is satisfied, but then the agent is the stored parameters, which do not think, deliberate or act. Reid's "something that thinks, and acts, and suffers" and his "uninterrupted continuance" pick out different candidates in an agent.

### Breaks

- **The indivisible monad has no agent counterpart (D11).** Reid holds that "a part of a person is a manifest absurdity", and that the person is "not divisible into parts". An artificial agent is divisible in every relevant sense. Its weights can be pruned, merged, split across machines or partially fine-tuned. Its context can be edited. Its tools can be removed. If personhood requires Reidian indivisibility, no artificial agent is a person, since there is no indivisible thing in it to be one. The alternative is to find in the agent something that behaves as a unit for the purposes Reid cared about, "rights and obligations, and ... accountableness", even though it is not metaphysically simple.

### New

- **Identity as the foundation of accountability, supplied by design.** Reid's closing thought is that personal identity "is the foundation of all rights and obligations, and of all accountableness". If agents lack Reidian identity (uninterrupted, indivisible, perfect), then whatever accountability agents bear must rest on something else: an institutional designation, a persistent record, an operator's liability. The agent case forces a question Reid could treat as closed. Can there be rights, obligations and accountability without the perfect identity he took to be their foundation? Or is the foundation itself something societies build?

## Counter-Positions

- **Locke's defenders; Shoemaker** (see `shoemaker-quasi-memory`). The brave officer refutes only a direct-memory criterion. Replace it with overlapping chains of memory (the general remembers the officer, who remembered the boy), and transitivity is restored. Reid's objection has shaped every modern psychological-continuity theory.
- **Parfit** (see `parfit-reductionism`). Reid's consequences (1) and (2) show that psychological continuity can branch and fail, so it cannot be identity. That is correct, and it shows that identity is not what matters. Parfit accepts Reid's premises and rejects his conclusion.
- **Hume** (see `hume-bundle`). The perfect, indivisible identity Reid appeals to is a fiction. His "invincible belief" in it is a fact about human psychology, not evidence of a monad.
- **Relational and institutional views.** Rights and accountability are grounded in social practice and law, which routinely assign them to entities without Reidian identity: corporations, regiments, states. Reid's own example of the old regiment shows that "loose" identity can bear historical responsibility.

## Open Questions

1. If a sliding context window puts an agent permanently in the brave officer's position, what criterion of identity, if any, gives a consistent answer about who acted?
2. Is a resumed agent "that which afterwards begins to exist", and so not the same being, by Reid's criterion? Or do the stored weights provide the uninterrupted existence Reid requires?
3. If no part of an artificial agent is indivisible, can any agent be a person in Reid's sense? What would replace indivisibility for the purpose of accountability?
4. Can rights, obligations and accountability rest on something other than perfect identity, such as an institutional designation, as Reid's regiment example seems to allow?
