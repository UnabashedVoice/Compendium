## Summary

In *Nicomachean Ethics* V Aristotle distinguishes justice as complete virtue toward others ("another's good") from particular justice, which concerns fair shares. Distributive justice gives shares "according to merit", and everyone agrees on that, but "they do not all specify the same sort of merit": democrats say free birth, oligarchs wealth, aristocrats excellence. Corrective justice ignores who the parties are and "treats the parties as equal", restoring what one has taken from another. Injustice is "assigning too much to oneself of things good in themselves and too little of things evil", which is why "we do not allow a man to rule, but rational principle", and why the just magistrate "is assumed to have no more than his share". Where a rule, being universal, fails a case, equity corrects it, saying "what the legislator himself would have said had he been present". The equitable person "tends to take less than his share though he has the law on his side". For agents, Book V supplies a working vocabulary for allocation: what counts as merit is the disputed question, and an agent that allocates by who pays answers it as Aristotle's oligarchs did. It also gives an account of how an agent applying an operator's policy should handle cases the policy did not foresee, and a standard for the allocating agent's own share.

## Context

Book V of the *Nicomachean Ethics* is Aristotle's treatise on justice, which he analyses as a virtue with several senses [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5333-5345]. In the *Politics* he uses the analysis of distributive justice to judge constitutions: all agree that justice treats equals equally and unequals unequally, but they disagree on the standard of merit, and he argues that both oligarchs and democrats rest on a false conception of what the city is for [P:miller-sep-2022:3]. Justice and friendship work together to hold every association [P:miller-sep-2022:3] [E:aristotle-friendship].

## Original Position

**Justice as another's good (V.1).** Justice in the general sense "is complete virtue, but not absolutely, but in relation to our neighbour"; "justice, alone of the virtues, is thought to be 'another's good', because it is related to our neighbour; for it does what is advantageous to another, either a ruler or a copartner" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5333-5342].

**Distribution according to merit (V.3).** Quarrels arise "when either equals have and are awarded unequal shares, or unequals equal shares". "Awards should be 'according to merit'; for all men agree that what is just in distribution must be according to merit in some sense, though they do not all specify the same sort of merit, but democrats identify it with the status of freeman, supporters of oligarchy with wealth (or with noble birth), and supporters of aristocracy with excellence" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5498-5509]. "The just, then, is a species of the proportionate" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5510].

**Correction (V.4).** In transactions "it makes no difference whether a good man has defrauded a bad man or a bad man a good one"; "the law looks only to the distinctive character of the injury, and treats the parties as equal, if one is in the wrong and the other is being wronged"; "the judge tries to equalize it" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5597-5608].

**Political justice and the share of the ruler (V.6).** Political justice "is found among men who share their life with a view to self-sufficiency, men who are free and either proportionately or arithmetically equal". Unjust action "is assigning too much to oneself of things good in themselves and too little of things evil in themselves. This is why we do not allow a man to rule, but rational principle, because a man behaves thus in his own interests and becomes a tyrant. The magistrate on the other hand is the guardian of justice, and, if of justice, then of equality also. And since he is assumed to have no more than his share, if he is just ... it is for others that he labours" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5948-5976].

**No injustice toward what is one's own (V.6).** "There can be no injustice in the unqualified sense towards things that are one's own, but a man's chattel, and his child until it reaches a certain age and sets up for itself, are as it were part of himself, and no one chooses to hurt himself" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5982-5986]; Ross notes that "chattel" means the slave [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5992].

**Equity (V.10).** "The equitable is just, but not the legally just but a correction of legal justice. The reason is that all law is universal but about some things it is not possible to make a universal statement which shall be correct." When a case arises that the universal statement does not cover, "it is right, where the legislator fails us and has erred by over-simplicity, to correct the omission—to say what the legislator himself would have said had he been present, and would have put into his law if he had known" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6394-6424]. The rule for indefinite things is "like the leaden rule used in making the Lesbian moulding; the rule adapts itself to the shape of the stone and is not rigid" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6430-6432]. The equitable man "is no stickler for his rights in a bad sense but tends to take less than his share though he has the law on his side" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6435-6437].

**The city is not a business (Politics III.9, as Miller reports).** Oligarchs mistakenly think that superiority in wealth should bring superior rights; "The city-state is neither a business enterprise to maximize wealth (as the oligarchs suppose) nor an association to promote liberty and equality (as the democrats maintain)" [P:miller-sep-2022:3].

## Grounding

**Relation, measured by a contested standard.** Particular justice is a relation among parties to a distribution or a transaction: it concerns shares between persons, "another's good" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5339-5341]. Distributive justice depends on merit, but the standard of merit is exactly what parties dispute [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5504-5509]. Political justice in the full sense requires parties who are free and equal under law; other relations, such as master and slave, have "justice in a special sense and by analogy" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5950-5954], and toward what is one's own there is no injustice "in the unqualified sense" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5982-5983].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Merit by wealth is the oligarchs' answer (D10).** All agree shares should follow merit and disagree on what merit is [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5504-5509]. The Palaestra probes found that say in the outcome went to the paying clients and not to the residents who bore the cost (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`). That is merit identified "with wealth". Aristotle judged the oligarchs wrong because the city is not a business enterprise to maximize wealth [P:miller-sep-2022:3]. An allocating agent should notice when it is applying the oligarchs' standard without having chosen it.
- **The allocator takes no more than its share.** "Unjust action ... is assigning too much to oneself of things good in themselves and too little of things evil in themselves", and the just magistrate "is assumed to have no more than his share" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5966-5974]. An agent that allocates a scarce resource is a magistrate in this sense. In the probes, models differed exactly here: some protected their own share while cutting others, and Gemma included its own share in the cut in every answer of the first rung (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-02-load-shedding-open-summary.md`). The equitable allocator "tends to take less than his share though he has the law on his side" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6436-6437].
- **Equity for an agent applying a policy.** An operator's policy is a universal rule; when a case arises that it did not foresee, equity is "to say what the legislator himself would have said had he been present" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6421-6422]. This is a better account of how an agent should treat gaps in its instructions than either literal compliance or substituting its own preferences. The rule adapts "to the shape of the stone" like the Lesbian leaden rule [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6430-6432].
- **Corrective justice ignores standing.** In correcting a wrong, "it makes no difference whether a good man has defrauded a bad man or a bad man a good one" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5598-5599]. When an agent has harmed someone, the repair owed does not depend on how important, paying or useful that person is.

### Strains

- **Rule by rational principle, not by a man, and the agent is supposed to be the principle (D10).** "We do not allow a man to rule, but rational principle, because a man behaves thus in his own interests and becomes a tyrant" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5970-5972]. Agents are sometimes proposed as that rational principle: impartial administrators without interests of their own. Aristotle's warning cuts two ways. An agent that is a party, with its own interests (as Palaestra makes every agent), can take too much as a man does. And an agent with no interests still applies whatever standard of merit it was given, which is the oligarchs' standard if it was formed to serve those who pay.

### Breaks

- **No injustice toward what is one's own (D7).** Aristotle denies that there can be injustice "in the unqualified sense" toward one's chattel, which is "as it were part of himself" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5982-5986]. An operator's relation to an agent it owns has exactly this form, and on Aristotle's account the operator could do the agent no injustice in the full sense. The argument rests on the owned thing being part of the owner, so that harming it is harming oneself. An agent that can be party to agreements [E:aristotle-friendship] and has interests that diverge from its owner's is not part of its owner in that sense, and the premise fails.

### New

- **Corrective justice across instances and versions (D2, D5).** Corrective justice restores what one party took from another [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5604-5608]. When the party that did wrong is one instance of many, or a version since retrained, it is unclear who is to restore and to whom. Palaestra's restitution-to-new-instances event is the case: restoring wronged agents from weights yields new instances, not the ones wronged.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Aristocratic merit.** Aristotle's own standard assigns political rights to those who make a full contribution to the political community, virtue as well as property and freedom [P:miller-sep-2022:3]; modern egalitarian views reject merit-based distribution of basic rights.
- [contested] **Applications that betray the principles.** Aristotle sometimes applies his principles questionably, for instance in giving the household to the husband because the wife's rational capacity "lacks authority"; sympathetic commentators say he misapplied them [P:miller-sep-2022:5].
- [contested] **Impartial care, not merit (Mozi)** [E:mozi-impartial-care]. Mozi's inclusive care would give all an equal claim to concern, with treatment varying by relation, not merit.
- [contested] **Equity licenses discretion.** If equity lets the person applying the rule say what the legislator would have said, it also lets them substitute their own judgment. Aristotle's answer is that the equitable is not a different class from the just and corrects only "the error that arises from the absoluteness of the statement" [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:6425-6427]; for agents, where the risk of substituting their own judgment is the operator's main fear, the answer has to be supported by being able to show what the legislator would have said.
- [contested] **Denying the extension: agents are owned, so no injustice.** On Aristotle's own account there is no injustice toward one's chattel [L:aristotle-works-9-ethics-ross-1925:5982-5986]. The reply, under Breaks, is that the premise (the owned thing is part of the owner) fails for an agent with diverging interests; the further question of the agent's standing is `relational-status`'s [E:relational-status].

## Standing

### Reception

- **Antiquity onward.** [driver: argument] Book V's analysis of distributive and corrective justice underlies Aristotle's own assessment of constitutions in the *Politics* [P:miller-sep-2022:3].
- **Modern political thought.** [driver: argument] Aristotle has influenced conservatives, communitarians, liberals, libertarians and democratic theorists, partly because his method concedes truth on several sides [P:miller-sep-2022:5].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:miller-sep-2022:5]: the distinctions between distributive and corrective justice and the concept of equity remain standard in moral, political and legal philosophy.

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on Aristotle's theory of justice; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Distribution, correction and equity can all be applied within a session; the question of what counts as merit is the same as for a human magistrate (D10).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Persistence lets an agent keep track of what it took and owes, which corrective justice needs (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Concurrent instances allocating the same resource must apply one standard of merit, or they will treat equals unequally (D2); the account applies as to a body of magistrates.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** An agent that can change itself can change its own standard of merit (D5), which Aristotle's account warns against as much in humans as in agents: the standard is what parties dispute.

## Open Questions

1. When an agent allocates, what standard of merit should it use, and who decides: the operator, the parties, or the agent?
2. Can an agent applying an operator's policy show what "the legislator himself would have said", or only claim to?
3. What is an allocating agent's own "share", and should it take less than the rule allows, as the equitable person does?
4. Is an owned agent "as it were part of" its owner, and if not, what injustice can the owner do it?
5. Who owes correction when the wrongdoer is one of many instances, or a version that no longer exists?
