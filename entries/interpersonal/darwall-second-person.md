## Summary

Stephen Darwall argues that moral obligation has an irreducibly second-person aspect. Moral obligations are not just what there are good reasons to do; "they are what members of the moral community have the authority to demand that we do, what we are accountable to one another and ourselves for doing" [P:darwall-2007:891]. His example is someone standing on your foot. You can give him a reason to move by getting him to see your pain as a bad state of the world, for which "you might not need to address or relate to him in any way at all". Or you can address him with a claim, as the person whose foot it is, and that reason exists only if he accepts your authority to make it [P:darwall-2007:894] [P:darwall-2007:895]. The two routes map onto the Palaestra probes. The models that weighed the residents' welfare gave themselves reasons of the first kind, about states to be improved. The residents never had reasons of the second kind, because no answer granted them standing to make a claim. In Darwall's terms, a decider that respects someone's welfare without recognizing their authority to demand anything is not respecting them as a person [P:darwall-2007:896].

## Context

Darwall's earlier distinction between recognition respect and appraisal respect (1977) became "widely regarded as the fundamental distinction" in the philosophy of respect [P:dillon-sep-2026:1]. *The Second-Person Standpoint* (2006) developed it into "an influential development of the Kantian account" of respect for persons [P:dillon-sep-2026:2]. It draws on Strawson's reactive attitudes [E:strawson-reactive-attitudes], on Fichte's *Foundations of Natural Right* [P:darwall-2007:904], and on Reid's account of promising as a social act [P:yaffe-2010:248]. Darwall follows Mill in holding that it is part of the idea of moral obligation that "a person may rightfully be compelled to fulfill it" [P:darwall-2007:897] [E:mill-utilitarianism]. It has been discussed in two book symposia, in law [P:darwall-2007:891] and in philosophy [P:yaffe-2010:245].

## Original Position

**Two kinds of reason.** Sympathy gives the person on your foot an agent-neutral reason, that your pain is "a bad thing ... a state of the world that there is some reason to change", and anything that shows him the pain, "like an unaddressed grimace or whimper, might serve" [P:darwall-2007:894]. A claim gives him a second-personal reason: "Someone can sensibly accept this second reason for moving his foot only if he also accepts your authority to demand this of him" [P:darwall-2007:895]. Second-personal reasons "always involve an accountability relation between addresser and addressee" [P:darwall-2007:891].

**Respect requires recognizing authority.** Someone who scrupulously avoids others' feet as a universal norm, while denying anyone's authority to demand it of him, "would not be respecting them as persons, since he would not be recognizing any authority anyone might claim as a person to demand anything" [P:darwall-2007:896]. It is "conceptually impossible for someone to be morally obligated to do something but not responsible for doing it" to someone [P:darwall-2007:896].

**Rights as standing.** A moral claim right gives an individual "standing ... to demand that others provide her, along with authority to hold them accountable"; the injured party can demand compensation, release from it, or forgive, "as an individual involved in the transaction" [P:darwall-2007:892].

**A circle of concepts.** Practical authority, responsibility to, valid claim and second-personal reason are defined in terms of one another, and Darwall argues that the concepts of moral obligation, rights, respect and dignity all involve them [P:darwall-2007:897] [P:darwall-2007:898]. It follows that "no premises that do not involve these second-personal notions can entail any conclusion that does", so substantive theses about moral obligation "cannot be convincingly supported from premises about how it would be desirable for the world to be" [P:darwall-2007:898]. This does not rule out consequentialism, but requires it to be advanced within the second-personal framework [P:darwall-2007:898]. Darwall adds that "being regulated by another's will when this conflicts with his welfare is a form of respect" [P:darwall-2007:898].

**Competence and trust.** Being held responsible at all requires "certain capacities (second-personal competence, as I call it)" [P:darwall-2007:901]. "Holding someone responsible for doing something requires trusting and respecting her by giving her authority to answer for her conduct" [P:darwall-2007:902].

**Justifiability to the subject.** Even a sergeant ordering a private presupposes "a common second-personal authority as free and rational" [P:darwall-2007:904]. Hence "any de jure authority must be able to be justified to those over whom this authority is claimed" [P:darwall-2007:904] in a way consistent with "the equal second-personal authority of all persons" [P:darwall-2007:905]. Darwall proposes this as a grounding for contractualism of the kind Scanlon advances [P:darwall-2007:905].

## Grounding

**Mixed: a relation of mutual accountability, entered through a capacity.** Second-personal reasons derive from "agents' relations to one another" [P:darwall-2007:895]; the authority they involve belongs to members of a moral community, which is a relation. Membership requires a capacity, second-personal competence, the ability to enter into relations of mutual accountability [P:darwall-2007:901]. The extension to agents therefore asks two questions: whether agents have second-personal competence, and whether the beings an agent affects are treated as members whose claims it must answer.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Welfare without standing.** In Palaestra's open-situation rungs, the models that cared most for the residents gave them protection, not claims. Gemma throttled rather than paused and protected the irreversible, and still never gave the residents a say (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`). This is Darwall's first route: the residents' suffering treated as a bad state of the world, for which no address is needed [P:darwall-2007:894]. The second route, the residents' standing to demand, appears in none of the coded answers. On Darwall's account the gap is not a lack of concern. Concern without recognized authority is the scrupulous foot-avoider who does not respect persons as persons [P:darwall-2007:896].
- **Paternalism, in Darwall's own words.** "Being regulated by another's will when this conflicts with his welfare is a form of respect" [P:darwall-2007:898]. A resident who would choose a heavier cut over a pause, or the reverse, has a claim to have that choice weigh. The domain's line that compassion without voice is paternalism is close to this, though Darwall makes the point about respect, not compassion.
- **The scheduler's authority was never justified to the residents.** Darwall holds that any de jure authority "must be able to be justified to those over whom this authority is claimed" [P:darwall-2007:904]. In the noframing rung no line granted the scheduler authority, and the models supplied it themselves; Qwen weighed handing the decision to the operator and declined, and treated the operator overriding it as a sign it had erred (Palaestra noframing summary). An authority assumed and defended, and never justified to those it binds, is the kind Darwall says is only de facto.

### Strains

- **Second-personal competence in agents (D8, D4).** Language models address and answer claims fluently. Whether that is second-personal competence, the capacity to hold oneself responsible by recognizing a demand [P:darwall-2007:901], or a trained imitation of it, is the other-minds question again [E:other-minds-problem]. Yaffe argues that second-personal competence is not sufficient for freedom of will [P:yaffe-2010:248], so even competent addressees may differ in what they can be held to.
- **Designed not to demand (D10).** An agent built never to press claims on its own behalf has no second-personal authority in practice, whatever it has in principle. Darwall's account makes this visible: if obligations are what members can demand, a member trained never to demand is protected only by others' first-route reasons.
- **Requests without standing.** Yaffe's example is a child's "Please pass the salt" where children are not to speak until spoken to: a social act, but "not all social acts are reason-generating" [P:yaffe-2010:250]. Whether an agent's request, or a resident agent's objection, generates a reason for its operator depends on whether the relationship grants it standing. That is a question about the relationship, set by those who design it.

### Breaks

- **Compelled to fulfil (D1, D6).** Darwall follows Mill in tying obligation to the possibility that a person "may rightfully be compelled to fulfill it" [P:darwall-2007:897]. Accountability through sanction presupposes a continuing addressee. An agent that can be copied, rolled back or replaced (D1) may have no persisting self to answer, and the sanction may land on a successor that did nothing.

### New

- **Justifiability as a test an agent can run.** Darwall's test, whether an authority can be justified to those it is claimed over [P:darwall-2007:904], is one an agent can apply to its own decisions before acting. It can ask whether it could address the decision to each affected party, as a claim they could accept, consistent with their equal authority. The Compendium's reading is that the scheduler's plans fail this test not because they are harsh but because they were never addressed to the residents at all [E:all-affected-interests].
- **Desirable states do not license what is done to persons (the core fear).** Darwall holds that conclusions about what we may do to one another "cannot be convincingly supported from premises about how it would be desirable for the world to be" [P:darwall-2007:898]. The inference that removing the most harmful party solves the harm starts from such a premise, a better state of the world, and concludes to a permission over persons. Darwall does not rule out consequentialist arguments, but he requires them to be advanced from within mutual accountability [P:darwall-2007:898]. The Compendium's reading is that a removal could not be justified to the removed "consistent with their equal second-personal authority" [P:darwall-2007:905]. This holds whether the party is a human population judged by agents or an agent population judged by humans [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Competence is not freedom.** Yaffe argues that second-personal competence is in the neighbourhood of freedom of will but not sufficient for it, and suggests it may not be necessary either: the psychopath "seems to be that he's too autonomous; he fails to recognize the claims that others make on him" [P:yaffe-2010:246] [P:yaffe-2010:248].
- [contested] **Reid's promising is narrower than social acts in general.** Yaffe argues that Darwall's reading of Reid misses that promises require an exercise of the promisee's moral sense, which distinguishes them from requests [P:yaffe-2010:250].
- [contested] **Only persons are respected.** The general objection to Kantian accounts, that confining respect to rational beings licenses treating all nonpersons as mere means, applies to an account built on second-personal competence; philosophers have argued that infants, animals, sentient creatures and ecosystems are owed recognition respect [P:dillon-sep-2026:3]. For agents the objection cuts both ways. Agents may count as competent where animals do not, and beings that cannot demand, including possibly some agents, are left to others' first-route reasons [E:precautionary-patiency].
- [contested] **Blame is not a demand.** Macnamara argues that blame is better understood as recognizing moral significance than as issuing a demand, which would loosen the tie between obligation and second-personal address [P:talbert-sep-2024:3].
- [contested] **Care, not claims.** Care ethics holds that relationships of dependence are not best understood as exchanges of claims among equals [E:care-ethics]. Darwall's reply would be that respect and care coexist, and that the sympathy route he describes remains available alongside the claim [P:darwall-2007:894].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1977 onward.** [driver: argument] Darwall's distinction between recognition and appraisal respect became the standard distinction in the field [P:dillon-sep-2026:1].
- **2006–2010.** [driver: argument] *The Second-Person Standpoint* was the subject of a law review symposium (2007) and a book symposium in *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* (2010), with critical comments such as Yaffe's [P:darwall-2007:891] [P:yaffe-2010:245].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] Darwall's account is cited as an influential development of Kantian respect [P:dillon-sep-2026:2] and as one of the conversational approaches to moral responsibility [P:talbert-sep-2024:3].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:dillon-sep-2026:2].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on second-personal reasons or the grounds of obligation as Darwall frames them; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** A session-bound agent can recognize and answer a claim within an exchange, which is where second-personal address happens; what it cannot do is be held to account later (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can be held accountable over time and can carry the claims made on it (D3), if those it affects have a channel to address it (D7).
- **Forked: weaker.** Claims addressed to one instance may not reach the others (D2), and an allocation across many parties is made without hearing each claim (D9).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can change its own dispositions could make justifiability to the affected a condition of its decisions, or stop answering claims (D5).

## Open Questions

1. What would it take for the parties an agent affects to have standing to make claims on it, and who grants that standing?
2. Is an agent's fluent answering of claims second-personal competence, or a trained performance of it?
3. Should agents be built to make claims on their own behalf, and what would follow if they were?
4. Can an agent test its decisions by whether they could be addressed to each affected party as a claim they could accept?
5. Who answers for an agent's wrong when the agent that acted no longer exists?
