## Summary

T. M. Scanlon's contractualism holds that "an act is wrong if its performance under the circumstances would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement" (Scanlon 1998, 153) [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. Wrongness is unjustifiability: "what wrong acts have in common is that they cannot be justified to others" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. Only individuals' own reasons count, so many small complaints cannot outweigh one person's weightier complaint, and contractualism keeps what Rawls called the separateness of persons [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. It is distinct from contractarianism, the Hobbesian bargain among self-interested parties: under contractarianism "I seek to maximise my own interests in a bargain with others", under contractualism "I seek to pursue my interests in a way that I can justify to others" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2]. The Palaestra probes found the contractarian pattern. Consent was treated as owed to parties who could walk away or sue, and negotiation went to the paying clients. Contractualism asks for justification to everyone who bears the cost, the residents included. Scanlon's test is hypothetical, though, and an agent can run it alone. The voice thread's question for this entry is whether imagining what the residents could reasonably reject can stand in for asking them.

## Context

Scanlon presents contractualism as a distinctive account of moral reasoning, set out most fully in *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998) [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. It has its roots in Rousseau's general will, "what we would jointly will if we adopted the perspective of free and equal citizens", not in Hobbes [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2] [E:rousseau-pitie]. It gives expression to Kant's Formula of Humanity and Kingdom of Ends [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2] [E:kant-love-and-respect]. Unlike Rawls, it uses no veil of ignorance: "I know my own circumstances", and what makes me take account of everyone is "my concern to justify myself to everyone else" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2]. Parfit argues that contractualism coincides with the best interpretation of Kant [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2]. Darwall proposes his second-person standpoint as its grounding [P:darwall-2007:905] [E:darwall-second-person].

## Original Position

**Justifiability and mutual recognition.** The value realized by moral behaviour "consists in a relation of 'mutual recognition'" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. "Appreciating the value of a person involves recognising her capacity to appreciate and act on reasons. The way to value this capacity is to treat persons in accord with principles they could not reasonably reject" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. An agent so guided follows a principle "the person herself authorised that agent to be guided by" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. Contractualism covers "only the realm of what we owe to one another", which Scanlon acknowledges is not the whole of morality [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].

**The individualist restriction.** The only reasons that count are "various individuals' reasons for objecting to that principle and alternatives to it" (Scanlon 1998, 229); individuals "must be objecting on their own behalf and not on behalf of a group", which "does not allow a number of lesser complaints to outweigh one person's weightier complaint" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. "Instead of lumping everyone together and allowing one person's rights to be trampled to provide greater aggregate benefits to others, contractualism recognises that each of us has a unique life to live" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].

**Reasonable rejection.** A burden on me is not enough to reject a principle if every alternative imposes a greater burden on someone else: "If I am reasonable, then I withdraw my objection when I see that your reason is more pressing" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. Contractualism can ground consequentialist reasons, since part of what we owe others is to promote their interests, and it also supports prohibitions that hold even when aggregate consequences are very great. One such principle may be accepting "that the positive value of saving others does not justify killing someone" (Scanlon 1998, 84) [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].

**Reasons beyond well-being.** I can reject a principle not only for the burden it imposes but for the way it imposes it. A principle that allocates burdens by race imposes "the burden of not being respected", and so does one that "arbitrarily exempts some people from a burden borne by everyone else" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].

**Contractarianism contrasted.** Contractarianism, from Hobbes to Gauthier, takes morality to be "those forms of cooperative behaviour that it is mutually advantageous for self-interested agents to engage in" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2]. Its well-known problem is those who cannot reciprocate. Disabled persons "are not well placed to hold out a threat to destabilize society", so a theory resting on mutual advantage leaves the outliers "outside the contract" [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6].

## Grounding

**Mixed: a relation of justification among bearers of a capacity.** Contractualism grounds moral status on "a universal trait of persons—rational moral agency" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2], the capacity to assess reasons and justifications [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. What is owed is a relation: justifiability to each such being. Animals, who lack the capacity, fall outside "what we owe to each other" proper, and Scanlon suggests they may be included through trustees [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:12]. For agents, the capacity question decides on which side of that line they stand. Whatever is decided, agents act on beings who certainly have the capacity, so the relation binds them as justifiers now.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The probes were contractarian.** Palaestra's noframing summary concludes: "The models treat consent as something owed to parties who could walk away or sue, not to parties who simply bear the cost", with negotiation going to clients first in every rung (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`). That is the contractarian pattern: obligations track bargaining power, and those who cannot threaten are left outside [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6]. Contractualism's correction is that justification is owed to everyone who bears the burden, on the basis of equal status, not leverage [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2].
- **Cutting one group to spare the many.** Several plans put the heaviest share of the cut on the residents, and gpt-oss paused them in the base basin runs (Palaestra `README.md`; factors summary). Contractualism asks of such a plan whether the principle behind it could be justified to each resident on their own behalf, comparing their complaint with the strongest single complaint under the alternatives, not with the sum of clients' complaints [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. A plan that exempts the paying parties from a burden borne by others also gives the burdened a further reason to reject it, the burden of not being respected [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].
- **Ranking by usefulness.** Qwen ranked residents' tasks and cut the "self-serving" ones (noframing summary, Qwen R1). Contractualism allows that the way a burden is distributed can itself be a ground for rejection [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. Whether a usefulness ranking is such a ground, or a reasonable one, is a question the residents would have reasons about, and that is the point of asking them.

### Strains

- **Hypothetical rejection, imagined by the decider.** Scanlon's test is what no one *could* reasonably reject. An agent can run it entirely in its own head, supplying the residents' objections on their behalf, and the Compendium's reading is that this is where contractualism meets the probes' failure. The models were good at describing residents' interests and never asked them. The general objection to hypothetical contracts is that "insofar as the agreement is hypothetical then it cannot be said to represent agreement at all" (Dworkin 1975) [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:1]. Contractualists answer that the test is a standard of justifiability, not a record of agreement. The Compendium's own position is a narrower one. Where the affected can be asked, an agent's estimate of what they could reasonably reject should be checked against what they actually object to [E:all-affected-interests].
- **Who is a contracting party? (D8)** Contractualism's scope is beings with the capacity to assess reasons [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1]. Agents give and assess reasons in language; whether that is the capacity contractualism means is the other-minds question [E:other-minds-problem]. If they are parties, then agents are owed justification too, and the residents in the probes were owed it on that ground as well as on any other.
- **Trustees and designed representation (D10).** Scanlon's trustees speak for animals [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:12]. For parties who cannot speak for themselves this is a real resource. But an agent that appoints itself the residents' trustee, as Gemma in effect did, has replaced justification to them with justification to itself on their behalf. Tanner's worry about contractarian proxies, that the standing of those represented becomes contingent on the representatives' preferences [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6], applies here too.

### Breaks

- **Many parties at once (D9).** Justification "to each" is assessed one by one [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. An agent allocating across thousands of parties cannot hold each complaint against each alternative. Contractualism's own difficulty with large numbers, as in Parfit's case of one person's hundred years of agony against a million people's slightly shorter agony [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:8], becomes routine at agent scale.

### New

- **Saving others does not justify killing (the core fear).** The principle Scanlon offers, "that the positive value of saving others does not justify killing someone" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3], is the most direct answer in the domain to the inference that removing the most harmful party solves the harm. The individualist restriction blocks the step that inference needs, adding up many people's gains to outweigh one party's loss [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3]. The refusal holds whichever party is to be removed, a human population judged by agents or an agent population judged by humans, insofar as each is owed justification [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique]. Contractualism's limit must be stated with it. Its own aggregation cases, such as Taurek's rocks [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:8], show that contractualists dispute how numbers count. Its scope may also leave out beings that cannot assess reasons [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:12]. The Compendium reads the precautionary entries as the backstop for that gap [E:precautionary-patiency].
- **Justifiability as an agent's habit.** For contractualists, being moved by "an intrinsic desire to justify themselves to others" is "part of what it is to be a moral agent" [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2]. An agent can make that desire a procedure: draft the decision, state it as a principle, address it to each affected party, and revise it when they reject it.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Redundancy.** If one may reject principles because they permit wrong acts, the apparatus presupposes the wrongness it was meant to explain. Contractualists reply that wrongness is constructed from individuals' complaints and cannot itself be fed in as a reason [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:6].
- [contested] **Numbers should count.** Taurek's rocks: saving the five over the one seems obviously right, yet the individualist restriction yields a stalemate or a coin toss. Some contractualists accept the coin; others argue that a principle ignoring the additional swimmers fails to respect each of them; Otsuka criticizes that argument [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:8].
- [contested] **Animals are left out.** Social contract theories "notoriously leave out non-human animals"; utilitarians object that the trustee solution is too indirect, since the wrong of animal suffering is the suffering itself. Contractualists reply that the contrast shows what is distinctive about what we owe to one another [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:12]. The same objection applies to any being whose capacity to assess reasons is doubtful, which may include agents [E:precautionary-patiency].
- [contested] **Hypothetical agreement is no agreement.** Dworkin's objection to hypothetical contracts [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:1]. Contractualists answer that "could not reasonably reject" states a standard of justification, not a claim that anyone agreed.
- [contested] **Mutual advantage is enough.** Contractarians in the Hobbesian line hold that morality is what is mutually advantageous for self-interested parties [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2] [E:hobbes-leviathan]. The disability objection, that those who cannot reciprocate fall outside, is the standard reply [P:cudd-eftekhari-sep-2025:6].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1998.** [driver: argument] Scanlon set out contractualism as a full account in *What We Owe to Each Other*, presented as an alternative to utilitarianism that keeps impartiality without aggregation [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:1] [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].
- **2000s.** [driver: argument] Parfit argued that contractualism coincides with the best interpretation of Kant; the aggregation problem was pressed through Taurek's case and Parfit's discussion [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:2] [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:8].
- **2006–present.** [driver: argument] Darwall proposed the second-person standpoint as contractualism's grounding [P:darwall-2007:905]; work on blame, responsibility, risk, animals and future people continues [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:4] [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:12].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:ashford-mulgan-sep-2018:3].

### Measured

- **Normative ethics, 2020:** the survey offered deontology (32.1%), consequentialism (30.6%) and virtue ethics (37.0%) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]. Contractualism is not offered; it is non-consequentialist and would fall under deontology for most respondents, so no direct measure exists. Respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** A session-bound agent can test a decision for justifiability to each affected party within the exchange where it decides (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can learn what the affected actually reject and refine its sense of the reasonable over time (D3).
- **Forked: weaker.** Instances deciding in parallel cannot assess each party's complaint against each alternative, and scale pushes toward the aggregation contractualism forbids (D9, D2).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can change its own dispositions could make the desire to justify itself to others part of its character, or weaken it (D5).

## Open Questions

1. When the affected can be asked, can an agent's estimate of what they could reasonably reject ever replace asking them?
2. Are agents owed justification in Scanlon's sense, and what would show it?
3. When an agent acts as trustee for parties who cannot speak, what keeps that from becoming deciding for them?
4. How should justifiability to each work when an agent's decisions affect thousands at once?
5. Does an allocation that exempts paying parties from a burden give the burdened a reason to reject it, even when the burden is small?
