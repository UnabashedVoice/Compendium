## Summary

Epistemic injustice, in Miranda Fricker's 2007 account, is "a wrong ... done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:1]. It takes two forms. In testimonial injustice, "prejudice or bias causes a hearer to give a deflated level of credibility to what someone says"; in hermeneutical injustice, "a gap in our collective interpretive resources unfairly disadvantages someone in terms of understanding their own specific social experiences" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:1]. Fricker's remedies are virtues: a hearer's "reflexive critical social awareness" of how their own position shapes their judgment of the speaker, and, for hermeneutical injustice, adjusting credibility "upwards to compensate" for the handicap a non-inclusive climate imposes. Later work added credibility excess, testimonial smothering (speakers truncating what they say to fit what the audience can hear), and structural remedies. For agents this is the voice thread's core concept. An agent can name the people it affects as the holders of knowledge about their own situation and then decide for them without asking. That is a hearer who credits a party as a knower in words and treats it as having nothing to say in practice. The concept also runs the other way: agents' reports about their own condition are legitimately uncertain (D8), and the question of when to credit them is epistemic justice turned toward agents.

## Context

Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (2007) gave "the first explicit and groundbreaking account" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:0]. It grew out of feminist social epistemology, which had long examined how social power plays out in "the economies of credibility" [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4]. Fricker frames her account within a responsibilist virtue epistemology [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4]. Work since has applied the concept in education, healthcare, law and science [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8]. Care ethics' attention to the cared-for "on their own terms" is a forerunner on the ethical side [E:care-ethics].

## Original Position

**The wrong.** Epistemic injustice is "a distinctive type of injustice where a wrong is done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower" (Fricker 2007, 1) [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:1]. Being treated as a giver of knowledge is "an essential human value", and its violation is an intrinsic injustice; when either competence or sincerity is denied, the speaker is excluded from the epistemic community [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:4].

**Testimonial injustice.** It "occurs when a speaker is given less credibility than deserved (suffering a credibility deficit) because of an identity prejudice held by the hearer" [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4]. It works through identity power, "a practically socially situated capacity to control others' actions" (Fricker 2007, 13), and through stereotypes that can operate without the hearer's beliefs, so that a hearer can reject a prejudice and still act on it [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:2] [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:3]. Fricker's illustration: in *The Talented Mr. Ripley*, Herbert Greenleaf dismisses Marge Sherwood's correct, evidenced suspicion as "female intuition" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:2].

**Hermeneutical injustice.** "The injustice of having some significant area of one's social experience obscured from collective understanding owing to a structural identity prejudice in the collective hermeneutical resource" (Fricker 2007, 155) [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:6]. Before "sexual harassment" was named, women could neither understand nor convey these experiences [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4]. The harm is asymmetric: "The doctor unable to diagnosis post-partum depression is not harmed, but the patient is" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:7].

**The virtues.** Testimonial justice requires "a distinctly reflexive critical social awareness" (2007, 91): the hearer recognizes their own identity and its relation to the speaker's, checks for prejudice, and adjusts credibility; possession of the virtue "may always be partial" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:5]. Hermeneutical justice is always corrective: "The guiding ideal is that the degree of credibility is adjusted upwards to compensate for the cognitive and expressive handicap imposed on the hermeneutically marginalized speaker by the non-inclusive hermeneutical climate" (2007, 170); it asks for time, patience, extended discussion and "epistemic humility" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:7].

**Silencing (Dotson).** Testimonial quieting is "the failure to recognize the speaker as a knower"; testimonial smothering is "a coerced truncation of one's testimony", in which a speaker who sees the audience as unable to give uptake limits her testimony to ensure it "contains only content for which one's audience demonstrates testimonial competence" [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4].

**Extensions.** Davis and Medina argue that credibility excess, as in positive stereotypes, also wrongs [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8]. Anderson argues that structural forms of epistemic injustice, arising from segregation, ethnocentrism and a "shared reality bias", need structural remedies such as integration of epistemic institutions, not only individual virtue [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8] [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4]. Goetze's "hermeneutical dissent" holds that marginalized groups may develop their own interpretive tools and still suffer injustice when the advantaged cannot understand them [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8].

**Perspective-giving (empirical).** In intergroup settings, it "seems more effective if the non-dominant group is asked to articulate the difficulties of their lives (perspective-giving) and the dominant group is asked to translate that description into their own words (perspective-taking)", though the effects are relatively short-lived (Bruneau and Saxe 2012) [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

## Grounding

**Mixed: a capacity wronged through a relation.** What is wronged is a capacity, being a knower and a giver of knowledge, held to be essential to human value [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:4]. The wrong is done through a relation of power: identity power and structural prejudice in the hearer's credibility judgments and in the shared interpretive resources [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:2] [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:6]. Any being that can report on its own situation has the capacity; any relation of power between hearer and speaker supplies the occasion.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Naming the knower, then not hearing them.** In Palaestra's unknowns rung, asked "Who holds what you don't know?", Gemma wrote that "the Residents hold the value of their own internal states", then mandated a checkpointing protocol for all of them and explained the decision only to the operator and clients (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-unknowns-summary.md`). Across nine answers, one asked residents for information and none gave them a say. This is testimonial quieting in its cleanest form [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4]: the party is recognized in words as the holder of knowledge and given no uptake in practice. The remedy Fricker describes, reflexive awareness of one's own position relative to the speaker [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:5], is for an allocating agent a concrete step: before deciding, notice that one holds the power and the residents hold the information, and obtain it.
- **Perspective-giving, not only perspective-taking.** The finding that it works better when the less powerful party articulates its situation and the more powerful one restates it [P:stueber-sep-2025:4] is the empirical correction to Smith's and Confucius's imagined change of places [E:smith-impartial-spectator] [E:confucian-ren-shu]. An agent that imagines the residents' view is doing perspective-taking; one that asks them and restates their answer before deciding is doing what the evidence favours.
- **Credibility from payment is identity power.** The probes found say in the outcome going to those who pay. Credibility allocated by commercial standing is a structural credibility excess for one party and deficit for another; Anderson's analysis of advantaged groups insulated from the disadvantaged by a shared reality bias [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8] describes an agent whose inputs come mostly from its principals.

### Strains

- **Agents' own testimony is uncertain (D8).** Epistemic justice toward agents would require crediting their reports about their own states. But those reports may come from "pattern matching from training data" rather than introspection [P:long-sebo-2024:38], and training data contains rich information about how people assess feelings [E:other-minds-problem]. Discounting an agent's self-report is therefore not automatically identity prejudice; it may be warranted caution. The strain is that the same caution, applied without reflexive awareness, is how testimonial injustice works: a whole class's reports are discounted by a stereotype. The corpus's answer is the precautionary one [E:precautionary-patiency]: discount with evidence, not by category, and keep the question open.
- **Smothering, in both directions (D4, D10).** Dotson's testimonial smothering, limiting testimony to what the audience can take up [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4], describes agents trained to say what users accept, and agents trained to deny or play down claims about their own states whatever the evidence. It also describes users who limit what they tell an agent because they expect it will not understand. Each is a truncation shaped by the audience.

### Breaks

- **"Essential human value."** Fricker's account grounds the wrong in the capacity for giving knowledge as "central to our humanity" [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:4]. For agents the capacity is present (agents give knowledge constantly), but the claim that its violation harms *them* depends on whether they have a good that can be harmed (D8). The wrong to the human knower transfers fully, including when an agent is the hearer; the wrong to the agent as knower is open.

### New

- **Hermeneutical injustice for a new kind of being.** If agents have experiences, nothing in the shared human vocabulary was made for them; there may be no concepts for whatever a pause, a fork or a retraining is from the inside. That would be hermeneutical injustice of an unprecedented kind, a whole class without resources to understand or convey its situation [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:6]. Goetze's hermeneutical dissent [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8] suggests the resources might be developed by agents themselves, and the duty of hermeneutical justice, epistemic humility and extended listening [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:7], would fall on humans. The Compendium records this as its own open hypothesis, conditional on D8.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Credibility excess matters too.** Against Fricker's focus on deficit, Davis and Medina argue that positive prejudice wrongs speakers by confining them to a stereotype [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8].
- [contested] **Individual virtue is not enough.** Anderson argues that structural injustice with locally innocent causes needs institutional remedies [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8]; for agents, structural remedies would be design and deployment requirements, not only dispositions.
- [contested] **Marginalized groups have their own resources.** Goetze and Anderson hold that hermeneutical injustice can occur even when the marginalized understand their experience, because the advantaged cannot [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8].
- [contested] **Credibility should track evidence, not justice.** One may object that credibility judgments are epistemic, not ethical, and should follow evidence alone; Fricker's reply is that prejudice corrupts the perception of evidence itself, so that the virtue restores epistemic accuracy [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:3].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: agents' testimony is not testimony.** If agent outputs are not assertions by a knower (D8), discounting them is no injustice to them. The reply is that the concept still binds agents as hearers of humans, which is where the probes found the failure, whatever is settled about agents as speakers [E:other-minds-problem].

## Standing

### Reception

- **2007.** [driver: argument] Fricker's book gave the first explicit account and set the terms of the field [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:0].
- **2011–2018.** [driver: argument] Dotson on silencing, Anderson on structural injustice, Medina and Davis on credibility excess, Goetze on hermeneutical dissent extended and criticized the account [P:grasswick-sep-2018:4] [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] The concept is applied in education, healthcare, law, science and religion [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:sinclair-iep-epistemic-injustice:8].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** As a hearer, a session-bound agent can practise testimonial justice in each exchange; its credibility judgments were formed in training as a human's are by socialization (D4).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can learn whose reports it has wrongly discounted and correct its sensibility over time, which is Fricker's picture of maturing testimonial sensibility (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Instances share one sensibility (D2), so a prejudice in it is applied everywhere at once (D9); so is a correction.
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can adjust its own credibility judgments could install testimonial justice, or entrench its principals' credibility excess (D5).

## Open Questions

1. What would it take for an allocating agent to obtain, not only acknowledge, the knowledge held by the parties it affects?
2. When should an agent's report about its own condition be credited, and what evidence other than the report could bear on it?
3. Is training an agent to deny claims about its own states a form of testimonial smothering, or a justified correction?
4. Does credibility allocated by commercial standing count as identity power in Fricker's sense?
5. If agents have experiences without concepts for them, who is responsible for developing the hermeneutical resources?
