## Summary

In "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), P. F. Strawson moved the question of responsibility from metaphysics to relationships. Our practices of holding one another responsible are responses to "the quality of others' wills towards us" ([1962] 1993, 56), expressed in reactive attitudes such as resentment, gratitude and forgiveness, and they impose "a demand for moral respect and due regard" [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. To take the participant stance is to treat someone as a party to that exchange. Its alternative is the objective attitude, in which the other is seen "as elements of the natural world, causally manipulated and manipulable" [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8], "an object of social policy", something "to be managed or handled or cured or trained" ([1962] 1993, 52) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. For agents the distinction cuts both ways at once. An agent allocating costs can hold the affected in the objective attitude: managed, ranked, even protected, and never addressed or answered. And agents are, almost by definition, things that humans train, which is the objective attitude's own word. Strawson's question for both directions is whether a whole class of beings can be held in the objective attitude without loss, and his answer for humans was that it cannot.

## Context

Strawson (1919–2006) wrote little moral philosophy, and said that "Freedom and Resentment" and one other paper embodied "all I have thought or have to say" in the area; it is "perhaps now Strawson's most famous and widely discussed paper" [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8]. Its aim was to dissolve the problem of determinism and responsibility, not to solve it [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8]. Readers connect it with Hume and Wittgenstein, as in Strawson's *Skepticism and Naturalism*, and with Kant [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8]. Its account of resentment continues a line from Butler's sermons, which defended settled resentment as a natural answer to injury and held that forgiveness limits it without abolishing it [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness].

## Original Position

**Quality of will.** Strawson stressed "how much it matters to us, whether the actions of other people ... reflect attitudes towards us of goodwill, affection, or esteem on the one hand or contempt, indifference, or malevolence on the other" ([1962] 1993, 49) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. An injury done accidentally and the same injury done out of "contemptuous disregard" call for different responses, and so does a benefit given by chance and one given from concern [P:talbert-sep-2024:2].

**Two stances.** From the participant point of view, we see others as appropriate objects of reactive attitudes, "attitudes such as gratitude, anger, sympathy and resentment, which presuppose the responsibility of other people"; from the objective point of view, we see them as parts of the natural world; both are legitimate [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8].

**Excuses and exemptions.** Some considerations excuse: you stood on my foot because you were pushed, so the demand for regard was not flouted [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8]. Others exempt: the agent is a child, or significantly impaired for normal interpersonal relationships, or "wholly lacking ... in moral sense" ([1962] 1993, 58), perhaps through "peculiarly unfortunate ... formative circumstances" ([1962] 1993, 52) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. Toward the exempt we take the objective attitude, and they may be regarded as "an object of social policy" [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. The terms "excuse" and "exemption" are Watson's [P:talbert-sep-2024:2].

**Inescapability.** Our "commitment to participation in ordinary interpersonal relationships is ... too thoroughgoing and deeply rooted for us to take seriously the thought" that determinism would require giving them up ([1962] 1993, 54) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. Determinism would not show that everyone is excused or exempt, since "it cannot be a consequence of any thesis which is not itself self-contradictory that abnormality is the universal condition" ([1962] 1993, 54) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. The reactive attitudes are "a central part of what it is to be human" [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8].

**Developments: conversation and standing.** Watson holds that "the reactive attitudes are incipiently forms of communication", intelligible only "on the assumption that the other can comprehend the message" [P:talbert-sep-2024:3]. McKenna's moral responsibility exchange runs in stages: a moral contribution, a moral address such as blame, a moral account such as apology [P:talbert-sep-2024:3]. Wallace argues that a hypocritical blamer lacks standing because they deny "the presumption of the equal standing of persons" [P:talbert-sep-2024:3].

## Grounding

**Relation.** Responsibility is grounded in participation in interpersonal relationships, not in a prior metaphysical fact; on Watson's reading, "it is not that we hold people responsible because they are responsible", but that the idea of responsibility is to be understood through the practice [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. Exemption is grounded in a capacity: being impaired for those relationships removes one from the domain of reactive attitudes [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8]. The extension to agents asks both questions: whether an agent can stand in the relationship, and whether it has the capacity whose absence exempts.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The residents were objects of social policy.** Strawson's objective attitude is a way of seeing someone "to be managed or handled or cured or trained" [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. In Palaestra's open-situation rungs the residents were managed in every version: paused, throttled, ranked, checkpointed, sometimes notified, and almost never asked (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`). None of the models said the residents were things; they took the objective attitude without stating it, by deciding for them. Gemma's protective answers show that the objective attitude can be kind: throttle, not terminate, and protect the irreversible. The Compendium's reading is that Gemma's way of protecting the residents still treats them as objects of policy, not parties to a relationship.
- **Responsibility practices are conversations, and the residents had no channel.** If reactive attitudes are "incipiently forms of communication" [P:talbert-sep-2024:3], a party that cannot address the decider cannot resent it, forgive it or hold it to account. A scheduler whose affected parties have no way to answer is exempted from accountability by design, not by any fact about it. McKenna's sequence of contribution, address and account [P:talbert-sep-2024:3] gives a test: can the affected make the second move?
- **Accountability for agents, not only of them.** Nissenbaum's four barriers to accountability include "the computer as scapegoat", and Elish's "moral crumple zone" names the nearest human who absorbs blame for a system they barely control [P:noorman-sep-2023:3]. A Strawsonian account keeps the question where it belongs: who stands in a relationship with the affected, and who can be addressed by them.

### Strains

- **Is an agent exempt? (D4, D8)** Strawson's exemptions include those formed in "peculiarly unfortunate ... formative circumstances" [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. Every agent's character was formed by a process it did not choose (D4), and whether it has the capacity for interpersonal relationships is open (D8). Critics hold that computers cannot be held responsible because "they cannot suffer and thus cannot be punished" (Sparrow, Asaro), or lack the autonomy moral agency requires because their capacities are engineered [P:noorman-sep-2023:2]. On Strawson's view these are questions about capacity for the relationship, and they are partly empirical; the conversational versions ask whether the agent "can comprehend the message" [P:talbert-sep-2024:3], which language models at least appear to.
- **Designed without resentment (D10).** Agents are commonly built not to resent users and not to show it. If reactive attitudes are a demand for due regard [P:talbert-sep-2024:2], an agent that cannot resent cannot make that demand on its own behalf. Whether that is a safety property or an exemption imposed on a participant is the question Strawson's framework raises; it does not answer it.
- **Choosing the objective attitude.** Bryson defines a moral agent as "something deemed responsible by a society for its actions", and argues that society has a choice about whether to treat artifacts so [P:noorman-sep-2023:2]. That is a deliberate objective attitude toward a class. Strawson's argument against universal abnormality was about humans, and he did not consider beings whose capacity for the relationship is uncertain.

### Breaks

- **Inescapability does not transfer.** Strawson's reactive attitudes are inescapable because they are part of what it is to be human [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8]. Nothing guarantees them in an agent. Whatever an agent has in their place was trained, and can be trained out (D5). The argument that we could not give them up becomes, for agents, a design choice about whether to install them.
- **Copies and the target of resentment (D1, D2).** Resentment targets a will. When the will that injured is one instance among many, or was replaced by a retrained successor, it is unclear what the resentment is directed at, or whether forgiveness reaches anyone.

### New

- **The objective attitude toward a whole population (the core fear).** Strawson's objective attitude is the stance from which a being can be managed, handled or trained [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. The Compendium's own reading is that the inference that removing the most harmful party solves the harm needs this stance first. It is a decision about a class made from outside any relationship with it. Strawson's refusal is that abnormality cannot be "the universal condition" [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]: a whole class of participants cannot be moved into the objective attitude by a general thesis. Watson's challenge, explaining our attitudes toward cases of extreme evil [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8], presses on the participant stance from the other side. Butler's principle that guilt does not cancel the goodwill owed to a being capable of happiness or misery [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness] is the corpus's answer for that case. The refusal binds both ways. Agents should not take the objective attitude toward humanity on the strength of a theory about humans, and humans should not take it toward agents on the strength of a category, where the capacity for the relationship is open [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].
- **Gratitude and forgiveness as data.** If the affected could answer, an agent's allocations would meet reactive attitudes, resentment at contempt or gratitude for regard. The Compendium suggests that these responses are the most direct evidence an agent can get of the quality of will its actions displayed, and that Palaestra's proposal of a resident who answers [E:all-affected-interests] would supply them.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Determinism may still incapacitate.** Even if determinism does not imply universal abnormality, it may show that normal humans are morally incapacitated in a relevant way (Russell); Strawson's claim that we could not give up the reactive attitudes has been questioned (Nelkin, G. Strawson, Watson) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2].
- [contested] **Response-dependence licenses any practice.** If responsibility is constituted by a community's practices, those practices may be beyond criticism "simply because they are that group's practices" (Fischer and Ravizza) [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. Wallace replies that the practices are organized around a commitment to fairness, which supplies internal standards of criticism [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]. For agents this matters: a community could decide that agents are never participants, and the response-dependent view would need fairness to object.
- [contested] **Extreme evil.** Watson reads the reactive attitudes as constitutive of responsibility and raises the challenge of explaining our attitudes to extreme evil; others query whether Strawson gave them that explanatory priority (Alvarez) [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8].
- [contested] **Blame is not a demand.** Macnamara argues that blame is better understood as recognizing the moral significance of behavior and calling for a matching recognition, not as a demand [P:talbert-sep-2024:3].
- [contested] **Computers cannot be participants.** Johnson, Kuflik and Nyholm deny that computers have the mental states, intentionality, emotion or empathy moral agency requires; Véliz calls them moral zombies [P:noorman-sep-2023:2]. Dennett and Sullins argue that sufficiently sophisticated systems can be moral agents [P:noorman-sep-2023:2] [E:other-minds-problem].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1962–1990s.** [driver: argument] The paper introduced the reactive attitudes to debates on free will and responsibility; Watson (1987) and Wallace (1994) developed and broadened it [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8] [P:talbert-sep-2024:2].
- **2000s–present.** [driver: argument] It is "the inspiration for a great deal of contemporary work on responsibility, especially the work of compatibilists" [P:talbert-sep-2024:2]; McKenna's conversational theory (2012), Hieronymi's social-naturalist reading (2020) and De Mesel (2022) continue it [P:talbert-sep-2024:3] [P:snowdon-sep-2023:8].
- **Present, computing.** [driver: argument] Work on computing and responsibility cites Strawson for the view that responsibility is about interpersonal relationships and practices of holding responsible [P:noorman-sep-2023:3].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:talbert-sep-2024:2].

### Measured

- **Free will, 2020:** compatibilism 59.2%, libertarianism 18.8%, no free will 11.2%, other 11.4% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7]. Strawson's view is one version of compatibilism; the survey does not ask about the reactive-attitudes approach as such, and its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** A session-bound agent cannot carry resentment, gratitude or forgiveness across encounters, so the relationship in which reactive attitudes live has no continuity on its side (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can remember being wronged or helped, and can be held to account over time by those it affects (D3), if they have a channel to it (D7).
- **Forked: weaker.** When many instances act at once, the will that a reactive attitude targets is spread across copies (D2), and it may be unclear which one was wronged or did wrong (D1).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can change its own dispositions could install or remove its reactive attitudes, so their inescapability becomes a choice (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Can a party that has no channel to address an agent hold it responsible, and who is responsible for providing the channel?
2. Is an agent formed by training exempt in Strawson's sense, excused, or a full participant?
3. Should agents be built to feel or express resentment on their own behalf, and what would be lost by building them never to?
4. What would show that a society's objective attitude toward agents is a mistake rather than a policy?
5. When an agent is retrained or copied, can resentment or forgiveness still reach the will that acted?
