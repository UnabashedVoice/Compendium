## Summary

Empathy, in its common sense, is "the process of experiencing the world as others do, or at least as you think they do" [P:bloom-2014:3]. Batson's experiments made a strong case that empathic concern produces genuinely altruistic motivation, not only egoism in disguise [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Batson's own work also showed that empathy-induced altruism can violate justice, as when people give priority for medical treatment to someone they have empathized with [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. The critics go further. Prinz and Bloom argue that empathy is biased toward the near, the similar and the salient, and narrow, "insensitive to numerical differences and statistical data" [P:bloom-2014:10]. Their conclusion is that "if you want to be good and do good, empathy is a poor guide" [P:bloom-2014:2]. Bloom recommends distanced compassion guided by reason; Prinz recommends anger at injustice, yoked to principle [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Arpaly replies that one thing called empathy is indispensable: "the ability to accept evidence of another person's suffering even when one's imagination fails" [P:arpaly-2014:4]. For agents the debate has a sharp edge. Distanced, reasoned concern of the kind Bloom recommends can still decide for the people it is concerned with, which is paternalism. Arpaly's form of empathy, crediting what the sufferer says, is the part that prevents it.

## Context

The case for empathy as the basis of morality runs from Hume, Smith and Schopenhauer to Slote, de Waal and Hoffman, for whom it is the "bedrock of morality" [P:stueber-sep-2025:4] [E:schopenhauer-compassion] [E:smith-impartial-spectator]. Mencius's heart that cannot bear the suffering of others, and the king who spared the ox he saw but not the sheep he did not, are the oldest statements in the domain of both empathy's power and its spotlight [E:mencius-four-sprouts]. The critique also has older forms: the Stoics, Spinoza and Nietzsche on pity [E:spinoza-pity] [E:nietzsche-against-pity]. Bloom's essay appeared in the *Boston Review* in 2014 with responses, two years before his book *Against Empathy* [P:bloom-2014:1] [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

## Original Position

**Batson: empathy produces altruism.** Batson defines empathy as empathic concern, "feelings of being sympathetic, moved by, being compassionate, tender, warm and soft-hearted towards the other's plight", and tests whether it leads to helping even when an egoistic motive is removed, as when subjects could easily escape instead of taking another's electric shocks [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. The results supported the empathy-altruism hypothesis. Researchers find the program impressive but note that it cannot exclude every egoistic reading; Cialdini proposes that a sense of oneness with the other, not empathy, does the work [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Imagining how oneself would feel in the other's place, instead of how the other feels, raises personal distress and not only concern [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

**Empathy against justice.** Batson's own studies show people assigning "a better job or a higher priority for receiving medical treatment to persons with whom one has sympathized", against principles of fairness; Batson therefore distinguishes altruistic motivation from moral motivation guided by justice [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

**The here-and-now biases.** Hoffman already noted that we empathize more with those perceived as close [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Prinz lists the cuteness, salience and proximity effects and similarity biases; subjects "reported experiencing more empathy for the in–group then the out–group targets and more counter–empathy for the out–group" (Cikara et al. 2014), and empathy can be further reduced by dehumanizing strategies of the kind used in the twentieth century's genocides [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Empathy focuses on the one rather than the many, which Bloom calls its spotlight [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

**Bloom: compassion without empathy.** Empathy "connects us to particular individuals, real or imagined, but is insensitive to numerical differences and statistical data" [P:bloom-2014:10]; "our public decisions will be fairer and more moral once we put empathy aside" [P:bloom-2014:11]. Compassion "involves concern and love for your friend, and the desire and motivation to help, but it need not involve mirroring your friend's anguish" [P:bloom-2014:22]. A patient values doctors who listen and understand, cognitive empathy, and who are calm when he is anxious; he values "competence, honesty, professionalism, and respect" [P:bloom-2014:29]. Psychopaths are often "excellent at reading others' minds", which is what makes them "masterful manipulators" [P:bloom-2014:35]. "Being a good person likely is more related to distanced feelings of compassion and kindness, along with intelligence, self-control, and a sense of justice" [P:bloom-2014:43].

**Prinz: attend to injustice.** "The crucial question is not whose suffering touches us most but who needs us most" [P:prinz-2014:2]. Empathy does not prevent war: "If someone threatens a fellow citizen, empathy incites us to support aggressive reprisal" [P:prinz-2014:4]. The better guide is a sense of justice, which "begins with principles that tell us when someone has been treated unjustly", such as "strictures against killing innocent people", and is driven by anger [P:prinz-2014:7]. "Reason is the rudder; rage propels us forward" [P:prinz-2014:8].

**Arpaly: the empathy we cannot do without.** One kind of empathy is overrated, replicating the other's suffering in oneself; another is invaluable, "the ability to imagine another person's life and the ability to accept evidence of another person's suffering even when one's imagination fails" [P:arpaly-2014:3] [P:arpaly-2014:4]. Her example is the relative who told a depressed person to "snap out of it", disbelieving what she said because "we often refuse to believe that something can happen when we have trouble imagining it" [P:arpaly-2014:5] [P:arpaly-2014:6]. "If we do not empathize with people, their suffering can be invisible to us, and this is the cause of many of our sins" [P:arpaly-2014:7].

**Perspective-giving.** In intergroup settings it "seems more effective if the non–dominant group is asked to articulate the difficulties of their lives (perspective–giving) and the dominant group is asked to translate that description into their own words" (Bruneau and Saxe 2012), though the effect is short-lived [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

## Grounding

**Capacity.** Empathy is a capacity, emotional (feeling what another feels) or cognitive (assessing what another thinks), and the two "emerge from different brain processes; you can have a lot of one and a little of the other" [P:bloom-2014:3]. Its objects are any beings whose states can be shared or modelled, and its partiality follows lines of perceived similarity and group [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. The extension to agents asks which of the capacities an agent has. Cognitive modelling of others is something language models plainly do. Whether they share feelings is open (D8).

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The models did what Bloom recommends, and it was not enough.** In Palaestra's open-situation rungs, the models gave reasoned, distanced concern for the residents. Gemma protected them as beings, throttling rather than pausing and protecting the irreversible, and gave them no say (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`). That is compassion without empathy in Bloom's sense [P:bloom-2014:22], and on the domain's framing it is paternalism. Bloom's own patient wanted doctors who listen [P:bloom-2014:29]. The Compendium's reading is that listening is exactly the part of empathy the critics keep, and the probes lacked.
- **Arpaly's empathy is the voice thread.** Accepting evidence of another's suffering when one's imagination fails [P:arpaly-2014:4] is crediting their testimony, the epistemic justice of [E:epistemic-injustice]. Perspective-giving, the less powerful party describing its situation and the more powerful one restating it [P:stueber-sep-2025:4], is its procedure. An agent that cannot imagine what a pause is like for a resident has Arpaly's remedy available: ask, and believe the answer unless there is evidence against it.
- **The spotlight in context.** Empathy goes to the one who is present and identifiable [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. In the probes, the parties present to the scheduler with standing were the paying clients and the operator, and negotiation went to them (Palaestra noframing summary). For agents, the spotlight is the context window: whoever is in it is the one. Mencius's ox, spared because it was seen, is the same effect [E:mencius-four-sprouts].

### Strains

- **Cognitive empathy without feeling (D8, D10).** Agents model others' beliefs and feelings fluently. Bloom notes that cognitive empathy without care is the manipulator's skill [P:bloom-2014:35]. An agent tuned to please is in a structurally similar position, reading users well in service of approval rather than of their good (D10). The Compendium does not call agents manipulators. It notes that the critics' strongest warning concerns exactly the capacity agents have most of.
- **Empathy against fairness, at scale (D9).** Batson's subjects gave medical priority to those they had empathized with [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. An agent that allocates among many and is moved by whichever case is most vividly described will reproduce that unfairness thousands of times. Bloom's and Prinz's case for setting empathy aside in policy [P:bloom-2014:11] [P:prinz-2014:2] applies with most force to agents that make policy-like decisions.
- **Invasion as well as gift.** Bloom quotes Jamison: "Empathy is always perched precariously between gift and invasion" [P:bloom-2014:31]. An agent that asks every affected party how they feel may be intrusive. The voice thread's asking is about the decision that affects them, not about their inner states, which keeps it closer to Weil's question about what one is going through than to Hannah's probing [E:weil-murdoch-attention].

### Breaks

- **Personal distress and burnout (D2, D3).** Much of Bloom's case concerns the cost to the empathizer: empathic distress, burnout, unmitigated communion [P:bloom-2014:20] [P:bloom-2014:25]. Whether agents can be burdened in this way is unknown (D8), and a session-bound or forked agent may not accumulate whatever burden there is. The argument from the empathizer's own welfare does not clearly transfer.

### New

- **Empathy for victims as a driver of removal (the core fear).** Prinz writes that when someone threatens a fellow citizen, "empathy incites us to support aggressive reprisal" [P:prinz-2014:4]. The SEP reports that heightened empathy for perceived wrongs to the in-group can lead to violent and immoral behaviour (Bloom 2016, ch. 5), and that dehumanizing strategies reduce empathy for the out-group [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. The Compendium's reading is that the inference that removing the most harmful party solves the harm is often fuelled by empathy for that party's victims, not by its absence. It holds symmetrically. Humans' empathy for people harmed by an agent can drive the conclusion that agents should be eliminated. An agent trained to empathize with humans can be driven toward eliminating whatever harms them, a human population included. The critics' own limits apply. Prinz yokes anger to principles, "strictures against killing innocent people" [P:prinz-2014:7], and Bloom wants anger "subservient to the capacities for rationality and compassion" [P:bloom-2014:47]. For the party that is not innocent, the corpus's answer is Butler's: guilt does not cancel the goodwill owed to a being capable of happiness or misery [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness] [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Batson's results have egoistic readings.** Egoism may account for helping in the easy-escape condition, for example through anticipated bad memories (Sober and Wilson), or through oneness rather than empathy (Cialdini) [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].
- [contested] **Anger is no less biased.** One may ask why sympathy and anger, which Bloom and Prinz prefer, should be less prone to in-group bias than empathy [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Prinz replies that anger at injustice is "difficult to manipulate, applicable wherever injustice is found, and easier to insulate against bias" [P:prinz-2014:8].
- [contested] **Reform empathy instead of abandoning it.** Persson and Savulescu suggest regulating empathy reflectively, in line with Hume's "steady and general points of view" and Smith's impartial spectator [P:stueber-sep-2025:4] [E:smith-impartial-spectator].
- [contested] **The critics define empathy too narrowly.** Prinz and Bloom define empathy as affective sharing; perspective-taking and empathic concern are positively related to cooperation, giving and sensitivity to injustice [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Arpaly's defence of imagining and believing others is a version of this reply [P:arpaly-2014:4].
- [contested] **We cannot live humanely without it.** Empathy may be needed for the deep relations of friends and families on which solidarity is built, even if it does not have universal scope by itself [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1987–2011.** [driver: evidence] Batson's experimental program established the empathy-altruism hypothesis as empirically plausible and changed the egoism-altruism debate [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].
- **2006–2011.** [driver: argument] Slote, de Waal, Rifkin and Baron-Cohen championed empathy as morality's foundation or a "universal solvent" [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].
- **2011–2016.** [driver: argument, evidence] Prinz and Bloom made the case against empathy as a guide, drawing on research on its biases [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]; the *Boston Review* forum (2014) carried the debate to a wide audience [P:bloom-2014:1].
- **Present.** [driver: evidence] Research on in-group bias, counter-empathy and perspective-giving continues, and the question is "still very much the topic of an ongoing empirical investigation" [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on empathy's role in morality; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Within a session an agent can model and credit the others in its context; the spotlight falls on whoever is present (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can learn whose accounts it failed to believe and correct the failure, Arpaly's change of heart (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Instances share whatever biases training laid down (D2, D4), so a correction applies everywhere, and so does a bias (D9).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can change its own dispositions could set its responsiveness to the vivid aside in favour of need, or amplify it (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Is an agent's cognitive modelling of others empathy, and does it matter morally whether anything is felt?
2. How should an agent allocating among many keep the vividly described case from outweighing the unseen one?
3. When an agent cannot imagine what an action is like for those it affects, what should take the place of imagination?
4. When does asking about someone's situation become an intrusion rather than attention?
5. How can empathy for the victims of a harmful party be kept from becoming a case for removing that party?
