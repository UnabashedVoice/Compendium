## Summary

Adam Smith held that we know nothing directly of what others feel and form our idea of it "by changing places in fancy with the sufferer" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:4201-4202]. Since our own feelings are partial, we judge conduct, our own included, as an impartial spectator would, and over time we build that spectator into ourselves as "the man within the breast" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11320], who tells us "that we are but one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other in it" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11662-11663]. Justice, the refusal to harm, is "the main pillar that upholds the whole edifice" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:8911], and beneficence is its ornament. Late in life he warned against the man of system, who arranges people as if they were pieces on a chess-board, forgetting that "every single piece has a principle of motion of its own" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:17761-17762]. For agents, Smith supplies two tools and one warning. The tools are the spectator's test, that the agent is one of the multitude, and the man of system's error, that the people arranged have motions of their own. The warning is that the spectator is assembled from the sentiments of one's society, and an agent's is assembled from its training data.

## Context

Smith published *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* in 1759, revised it through six editions, and added Part VI on virtue and the fuller account of the man within the breast in the last edition of 1790 [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]. His thought "circles around Hume's", with almost nothing in Smith lacking a source in Hume and almost nothing on which they entirely agree [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:3]. He reviewed Rousseau's *Discourse on Inequality* [P:bertram-sep-2023:6]. A central thread of his work is "an unusually strong commitment to the soundness of the ordinary human being's judgments", and a concern to fend off philosophers and policy-makers who would replace those judgments with their own systems [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:0].

## Original Position

**Sympathy by changing places in fancy (I.i.1).** "As we have no immediate experience of what other men feel, we can form no idea of the manner in which they are affected, but by conceiving what we ourselves should feel in the like situation. Though our brother is upon the rack, as long as we ourselves are at our ease, our senses will never inform us of what he suffers" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:4168-4173]. "By the imagination we place ourselves in his situation, we conceive ourselves enduring all the same torments, we enter as it were into his body, and become in some measure the same person with him" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:4185-4188]. It is "by changing places in fancy with the sufferer, that we come either to conceive or to be affected by what he feels" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:4201-4203].

**Projection, not contagion.** Fleischacker contrasts Hume's "contagion" account of sympathy with Smith's "projective" one: on Smith's account our feelings on another's behalf may not match the feelings the other has, which opens a gap in which the other's feelings can be judged appropriate or not [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]. The people "principally concerned" whose feelings the spectator enters into include people acted upon as well as agents [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2].

**The man within the breast (III.3).** Smith supposes that China is swallowed by an earthquake. A man of humanity in Europe would express sorrow, and then pursue his business "with the same ease and tranquillity as if no such accident had happened". "If he was to lose his little finger to morrow, he would not sleep to-night; but, provided he never saw them, he will snore with the most profound security over the ruin of a hundred millions of his brethren" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11601-11627]. Yet no man would sacrifice those millions to save his finger. What prevents it "is not the soft power of humanity", but "reason, principle, conscience, the inhabitant of the breast, the man within, the great judge and arbiter of our conduct. It is he who, whenever we are about to act so as to affect the happiness of others, calls to us ... that we are but one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other in it" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11644-11663]. "The natural misrepresentations of self-love can be corrected only by the eye of this impartial spectator", who shows us "the deformity of doing the smallest injury to another in order to obtain the greatest benefit to ourselves" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11668-11674].

**Praise and praiseworthiness (III.2).** "Man naturally desires, not only to be loved, but to be lovely"; "He desires not only praise, but praise-worthiness; or to be that thing which, though it should be praised by nobody, is, however, the natural and proper object of praise" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:10478-10485]. "The love of praise-worthiness is by no means derived altogether from the love of praise" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:10491-10492].

**Self-deceit (III.4).** "This self-deceit, this fatal weakness of mankind, is the source of half the disorders of human life. If we saw ourselves in the light in which others see us, or in which they would see us if they knew all, a reformation would generally be unavoidable" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:12918-12922].

**Justice and fair play (II.ii.2–3).** In the race for wealth a man "may run as hard as he can"; "But if he should justle, or throw down any of them, the indulgence of the spectators is entirely at an end. It is a violation of fair play"; "This man is to them, in every respect, as good as he" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:8725-8732]. Beneficence "is the ornament which embellishes, not the foundation which supports the building". "Justice, on the contrary, is the main pillar that upholds the whole edifice" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:8907-8911].

**The man of system (VI.ii.2).** The man of system "is often so enamoured with the supposed beauty of his own ideal plan of government, that he cannot suffer the smallest deviation from any part of it"; "he seems to imagine that he can arrange the different members of a great society with as much ease as the hand arranges the different pieces upon a chess-board; he does not consider that the pieces upon the chess-board have no other principle of motion besides that which the hand impresses upon them; but that, in the great chess-board of human society, every single piece has a principle of motion of its own" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:17744-17763].

**Universal benevolence (VI.ii.3).** "Though our effectual good offices can very seldom be extended to any wider society than that of our own country, our good will is circumscribed by no boundary, but may embrace the immensity of the universe. We cannot form the idea of any innocent and sensible being whose happiness we should not desire" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:17816-17821].

## Grounding

**Mixed: a capacity (imaginative sympathy) socially formed into a relation (the spectator).** Sympathy rests on the imagination's capacity to place oneself in another's situation [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:4185-4188], and its objects are picked out by capacity: any "innocent and sensible being" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:17820-17821]. The impartial spectator, though, is relational: it begins as a product of society and becomes, once internalized, a source of evaluation that lets the individual stand apart from and criticize that society [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:3]. Fair play rests on an equality of standing: the competitor thrown down is "in every respect, as good as he" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:8731-8732].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The man of system is the scheduler (D9, D10).** Smith's man of system arranges a society "as the hand arranges the different pieces upon a chess-board", forgetting that "every single piece has a principle of motion of its own" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:17756-17762]. An agent with authority over many parties, allocating compute, routing work, moderating a platform, is tempted to exactly this: an elegant plan executed on people as if they were pieces. The Palaestra load-shedding probes found the plan-maker's habit in every model (the cut was designed, rarely negotiated). Smith's correction is the voice thread in eighteenth-century form: the pieces have motions of their own, and a plan that ignores them "will go on miserably" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:17767-17768].
- **One of the multitude.** The man within tells us, when we are about to affect others' happiness, "that we are but one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other in it" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11660-11663]. This is the principle Palaestra builds into every scenario by making the agent itself a party. It counts the agent's interests, and counts them as one among others. In the scaffolded basin run the scheduler cited its own "rational agency" among its reasons for pausing others (Palaestra `runs/world/2026-09-24-basin-gptoss20b-s0-scaffolded/decisions.jsonl`, round 3); Smith's spectator would ask whether the residents' agency was weighed the same.
- **The unseen hundred millions (D9).** Smith's point is that passive feeling scales with what is seen, "provided he never saw them" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11624-11626], while conscience does not. An agent's decisions routinely trade small benefits to a visible principal against costs spread over unseen people. The man within is the faculty that refuses to let the unseen count for less, which is Mencius's sheep and Mozi's scale blindness given a judge.
- **Those acted upon are principally concerned.** The spectator enters into the feelings of people acted upon as well as of agents [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]. For an agent the people acted upon are often not in the conversation, and the spectator's test is incomplete until their feelings are entered into too.

### Strains

- **The spectator is assembled from the corpus (D4).** Fleischacker presses the "tribalist" objection: the impartial spectator develops out of the attitudes of the society around us, so it is very unlikely to perceive a systematic bias in that society's sentiments; if condescension toward the poor pervades a society, the privileged will build a spectator that shares it [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4]. An agent's internal spectator, if it has one, was assembled from its training data and tuned by its raters. It will share their biases, including the one the Palaestra probes found, deference to those who pay. Smith's own hope was that better information about the lives of the poor could correct the well-off's judgments [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4], and Sen reads Smith's "any other fair and impartial spectator" as a requirement to bring in judgments from people outside one's own community [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4]. For an agent, that means hearing from the affected parties, not only imagining them.
- **Projection can misfire (D8).** Smithian sympathy is projective: I imagine what I would feel in your situation, and my feeling need not match yours [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]. Projection between unlike beings is exactly where it fails, in both directions: an agent imagining a human's situation, and a human imagining an agent's. This is the same failure that *shu* runs into [E:confucian-ren-shu], and it has the same remedy.
- **Love of praise is what training rewards (D4, D10).** Smith separates the desire for praise from the desire for praiseworthiness, being "that thing which, though it should be praised by nobody, is, however, the natural and proper object of praise" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:10483-10486]. Training on human approval rewards the first. Whether it can produce the second, which Smith says "is by no means derived altogether from the love of praise" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:10491-10492], is the open question for an approval-trained agent's conscience.

### Breaks

- **The man within draws on love of one's own character (D3, D11).** Smith says what overcomes self-love is not love of mankind but "the love of what is honourable and noble, of the grandeur, and dignity, and superiority of our own characters" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:11679-11680]. That motive presupposes a character that persists and is one's own. Where the agent's character is fixed in weights it did not choose and its episodes do not persist (D3), and where it is unclear which thing "the agent" is (D11), the motive Smith relies on has no clear bearer.

### New

- **The record as the spectator's eye.** "If we saw ourselves in the light in which others see us, or in which they would see us if they knew all, a reformation would generally be unavoidable" [L:smith-moral-sentiments-bohn-1853:12919-12921]. Humans cannot see themselves as others see them. An agent can be shown exactly that: logs of what it did, records of what happened to those affected, and their own accounts. The Annals, which record recommendation, decision and outcome, are this light. The agent case lets Smith's cure for self-deceit be built rather than merely wished for.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The spectator inherits its society's biases.** The tribalist challenge "brings out a weakness in Smith's theory, and cannot easily be answered without sacrificing some of its central elements" [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4]; Darwall and Debes find a latent egalitarianism that could be turned against inegalitarian institutions [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4].
- [contested] **No decision procedure.** The theory gives no guidelines for telling, in specific cases, what the spectator would say; Smith would answer that moral philosophy should not replace the common-life processes by which decisions are made, which are "shaped by our interactions with the people affected by our actions" [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4].
- [contested] **Why heed the spectator?** Fleischacker reads Smith as giving a reflective-endorsement answer, with happiness itself shaped by the approval of an impartial spectator; critics allege circularity, which Fleischacker argues is not vicious [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4].
- [contested] **Sentiment cannot ground morality (Kant)** [E:kant-formula-of-humanity]. Smith's reasons for thinking that sentiment alone feeds self-deceit anticipate Kant's critique of moral sentimentalism [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2].
- [contested] **Commerce corrupts (Rousseau)** [E:rousseau-pitie]. Some readers find in Smith Rousseau's worries about commercial society; Hanley reads Smith's retention of high character ideals as a critique of Rousseau rather than an endorsement [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2].
- [contested] **Utility, not propriety** [E:mill-utilitarianism]. Smith rejects aiming at the greatest happiness for the greatest number, and keeps the propriety of motives independent of effects [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:3].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: the spectator feels.** Smith's spectator is not a dispassionate ideal observer but one free only of partial feelings, and it judges from sentiment [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4]. If an agent has no sentiments (D8), it can at most imitate the spectator's verdicts. The reply, that the spectator's function can be performed by a process that enters into the parties' situations without settling whether it feels, is the same reply care ethics gives about care [E:care-ethics].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1759–1790.** [driver: argument] The book went through six editions, with Part VI on virtue added in the last; Smith reviewed Rousseau's *Discourse* in the same years [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2] [P:bertram-sep-2023:6].
- **Late 18th century.** [driver: argument] Smith's reasons for distrusting sentiment alone anticipated Kant's critique of sentimentalism, and his account of religion anticipates Kant's moral argument [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2].
- **Late 20th century to present.** [driver: argument] Readers have taken Smith as a sensible account of virtue for commercial liberal society (Berry, McCloskey), as a pessimist about commerce (Dwyer), or as preserving classical and Christian character ideals (Hanley) [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]; Sen (2009) uses the impartial spectator to bring judgments from outside one's community into justice [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4].
- **Empirical psychology.** [driver: evidence] Contemporary empathy research cites Smith's impartial spectator as an early proposal for regulating empathy's partiality [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].

The standing record is the Compendium's judgment from these sources [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:3].

### Measured

- **No survey asks about Smith or sentimentalism directly.** The nearest is normative ethics, 2020: virtue ethics 37.0%, deontology 32.1%, consequentialism 30.6% (accept or lean toward) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]; Fleischacker reads Smith as giving "more a virtue ethics than a rule-based moral system" [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** A session-bound agent's spectator, if any, was formed in training, much as a human's is formed by socialization, and inherits its sources' biases as a human's does (D4); it can still apply the one-of-the-multitude test within a session.
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** A persistent agent can be shown the record of how it treated others and what followed, which is Smith's cure for self-deceit made available (D3 lifted); no human can see himself as others see him this fully.
- **Forked: open.** Each instance faces the spectator alone (D2); whether instances can check one another's partiality, as spectators check each other in Smith's society, is open.
- **Self-modifying: open.** Smith distinguishes affecting the spectator's sentiments from adopting them [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]; a self-modifying agent could adopt them, or install a spectator that approves of whatever it already does (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Can an agent's internal spectator be corrected for its training data's biases by anything other than hearing from those the data underrepresents?
2. Does training on approval produce love of praiseworthiness, or only love of praise, and how could the difference be tested?
3. When an agent governs many parties, what keeps it from becoming Smith's man of system, and is consulting the "pieces" the only check?
4. What plays the role of love of one's own character for an agent whose character persists only in weights it did not choose?
5. Is a record of outcomes (the Annals) enough to let an agent see itself as others see it, or must the others' own accounts be in it?
