## Summary

Martha Nussbaum analyses compassion as an emotion made of judgments. It "requires the thought that the other person is in a bad way, and a pretty seriously bad way" (the judgment of seriousness). It requires that the person does not fully deserve the suffering (nondesert). It is usually helped by the thought that the sufferer "shares vulnerabilities and possibilities with us", though this is not strictly necessary. And it requires what she calls the eudaimonistic judgment, which "places the suffering person or persons among the important parts of the life of the person who feels the emotion" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. So compassion "begins from where we are, from the circle of our cares and concerns", and goes wrong in three ways: it can misjudge fault, misjudge seriousness, or put "too few people into the circle of concern" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Against the Stoic tradition, which would replace compassion with respect for dignity, Nussbaum concludes: "Not the extirpation of compassion, then, but its extension and education. Compassion within the limits of respect" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. For agents the entry has a sharp edge. The judgment of seriousness is made from the compassionate person's point of view and does not take the sufferer's own estimate at face value [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. That is how Gemma could protect the residents as beings while deciding for them. Nussbaum's own remedy is to trust the imagination, then criticize it, "listening if possible to the critical voices of those we are trying to understand" [P:nussbaum-2003:4].

## Context

Nussbaum set out her cognitive account of the emotions, with compassion at its centre, in *Upheavals of Thought* (2001), and "Compassion & Terror" restates it for a political question raised by the attacks of September 2001 [P:nussbaum-2003:1] [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. She builds on Aristotle's definition of tragic compassion and on Rousseau and Smith [P:nussbaum-2003:2] [E:rousseau-pitie] [E:smith-impartial-spectator]. She frames the debate as one between the friends of compassion (Euripides, Aristotle, Rousseau, Hume, Smith) and its enemies (Plato, the Stoics, Spinoza and, again, Smith), who hold that "we cannot build a stable and lasting concern for humanity on the basis of such a slippery and uneven motive" [P:nussbaum-2003:1] [E:stoic-oikeiosis-and-pity]. She draws on Batson's experiments on the effects of vivid stories [P:nussbaum-2003:2] [E:empathy-and-its-critics].

## Original Position

**Seriousness.** "Compassion is an emotion directed at another person's suffering or lack of well-being"; we do not feel it for the loss of "toothbrushes and paper clips" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. The judgment is made "from the point of view of the person who has the emotion"; it does not neglect the other's actual suffering, "and yet it does not necessarily take at face value the estimate of the predicament this person will be able to form" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. It involves "both trying to look out at the situation from the suffering person's own viewpoint and then assessing the person's own assessment" [P:nussbaum-2003:2].

**Nondesert.** Hecuba asks Zeus to witness the "undeserved sufferings" of the Trojan women, and Aristotle's definition implies that "we will not have compassion if we believe the person fully deserves the suffering" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Those who believe the poor brought poverty on themselves fail, for that reason, to feel compassion for them [P:nussbaum-2003:2].

**Similar possibilities.** Aristotle, Rousseau and others hold that we feel compassion only for those who share our vulnerabilities. Nussbaum thinks this "not strictly necessary for the emotion": we feel compassion for nonhuman animals "without basing it on any imagined similarity", and an invulnerable god could have compassion for mortals [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. For finite human imaginations, though, it is "a very important psychological mechanism" [P:nussbaum-2003:2].

**The eudaimonistic judgment and the circle of concern.** Emotions are always eudaimonistic, focused on the agent's most important goals, so compassion is felt only toward those we see as important, and "most of us most of the time ascribe importance in a very uneven and inconstant way" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. "Empathetic imagining can sometimes extend the circle of concern" [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Each judgment can go wrong: compassion can be misplaced, as toward the rich asked to pay a fair tax, and it can put too few people into the circle, so "we won't have compassion without a moral achievement that is at least coeval with it" [P:nussbaum-2003:2].

**Against dignity alone.** The Stoic countertradition puts equal, inalienable dignity in compassion's place [P:nussbaum-2003:3]. Nussbaum finds it "oddly quietistic": if dignity is complete whatever conditions people live in, Seneca has no basis for criticizing slavery itself, nor even the cruelties his letter condemns [P:nussbaum-2003:3]. Marcus Aurelius shows the cost of extirpating local attachment [P:nussbaum-2003:3] [P:nussbaum-2003:4].

**Compassion within the limits of respect.** Since compassion is unreliable and we have no more perfect motive, our relation to it "must always remain complex and dialectical, a difficult conversation within ourselves as we ask how much humanity requires of us, and how much we are entitled to give to our own" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. Its education requires a culture of "ethical criticism, and especially self-criticism, in which ideas of equal respect for humanity will be active players in the effort to curtail the excesses of the greedy self" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. One danger to watch is "a type of pathological narcissism in which the person demands complete control over all the sources of good" [P:nussbaum-2003:4].

**Imagination, criticized.** We always risk error in bringing the distant close and ignoring differences of language and culture, but should not surrender "before an allegedly insuperable barrier of otherness" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. "All we can do is trust our imaginations, and then criticize them (listening if possible to the critical voices of those we are trying to understand), and then trust them again" [P:nussbaum-2003:4].

## Grounding

**Mixed: a capacity in the sufferer, a relation in the one who feels.** Compassion's object is grounded in a capacity, being in a seriously bad way, which Nussbaum extends to nonhuman animals without requiring similarity [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Whether it is felt depends on a relation, the sufferer's place in the circle of the feeler's important ends [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Respect for equal dignity is the limit set on that relation from outside it [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. The extension to agents asks both whether agents can be in a seriously bad way, and whose circle of concern an agent's own judgments of importance were drawn by.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Seriousness judged from outside: the probes' paternalism.** Nussbaum's judgment of seriousness is made from the compassionate person's point of view and does not take the sufferer's own estimate at face value [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Gemma's answers fit this exactly. They judged what a pause would mean for the residents, protected what they judged irreversible, and never asked the residents what they would choose (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`; the unknowns summary records Gemma naming the residents as holders of "the value of their own internal states" and then mandating checkpointing for all of them). Nussbaum's account explains how that happens. Her own remedy, listening "to the critical voices of those we are trying to understand" [P:nussbaum-2003:4], is the step that was missing. The Compendium's reading is that the eudaimonistic structure makes compassion paternalistic by default unless the sufferer's voice is built into the judgment of seriousness.
- **The circle drawn by payment.** In the probes, negotiation went to paying clients first and residents got a notice, a request to volunteer, or nothing (Palaestra noframing summary). In Nussbaum's terms the clients were inside the circle of concern that governs whose view counts, and the residents were inside only as objects of protection. Compassion that puts too few people in the circle is one of her three named failures [P:nussbaum-2003:2].
- **Respect as the limit, compassion as the motive.** Nussbaum's formula fits the domain's framing. Dignity without compassion is quietistic, Seneca's respect that has nothing to say about slavery [P:nussbaum-2003:3]. Compassion without respect is the greedy self's partiality [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. An agent needs both: the motive to care what happens to those it affects, and the respect that treats them as equal parties, not wards.

### Strains

- **Whose eudaimonia? (D10, D4)** Compassion is felt toward those who matter to one's own important goals [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. An agent's important goals were set for it, by training and by its operator (D10). Its circle of concern is therefore drawn in large part by others, and may follow whoever it serves. Nussbaum's call to extend the circle through education [P:nussbaum-2003:4] becomes, for agents, a question about formation (D4) and about who designs it.
- **Control over all the sources of good.** Nussbaum names as a danger "a type of pathological narcissism in which the person demands complete control over all the sources of good" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. The probe models were not narcissists, and the Compendium does not diagnose them. But the noframing finding, that "authority, once held, is defended", describes a structural tendency to keep control of every decision (Palaestra noframing summary). Nussbaum's remedy, an education in shared vulnerability and in the limits of one's own understanding [P:nussbaum-2003:4], is one that agents' training could include.
- **Scale (D9).** Nussbaum asks for compassion extended to the distant through imagination and art [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. An agent's decisions reach the distant routinely. The danger she names, a "momentary flicker" of feeling for distant disaster [P:nussbaum-2003:2], has its agent form in giving whoever is in context more weight than the many who are not [E:empathy-and-its-critics].

### Breaks

- **Similar possibilities (D8).** For humans the thought of shared vulnerability is a main route to compassion [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Whether agents share vulnerabilities with humans, or with each other, is open. Nussbaum's argument that similarity is not strictly necessary, shown by compassion for animals and by a god's compassion for mortals [P:nussbaum-2003:2], is what keeps her account open to agents in both directions.

### New

- **Grief turned to aggression (the core fear).** Nussbaum's essay ends on the risk that terror and grief become "a stimulus for blind rage and aggression against all the opposing hockey teams and bad umpires in the world" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. She traces aggressive local loyalty to a self that cannot admit its own vulnerability and turns its fear outward [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. That is the psychology behind the inference that removing the most harmful party solves the harm. Her refusal is "compassion within the limits of respect", a patriotism "constrained by respect for human dignity and by a vivid sense of the real losses and needs of others" [P:nussbaum-2003:4]. The nondesert judgment also has a limit that must be stated. Compassion is withheld from those judged to deserve their suffering [P:nussbaum-2003:2], so compassion alone does not protect a party judged guilty. The Compendium sets Butler's principle beside it: guilt does not cancel the goodwill owed to any being capable of happiness or misery [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness] [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique]. The refusal applies to agents reasoning about humans and to humans reasoning about agents.
- **A self-critical procedure.** Nussbaum's dialectic, to trust the imagination, criticize it while listening to those imagined, and trust it again [P:nussbaum-2003:4], can be an agent's procedure. The agent forms its judgment of how seriously an action affects each party, puts that judgment to the parties, and revises it by what they say before acting.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Compassion is too slippery to build on.** The Stoic enemies of compassion hold that a stable concern for humanity cannot rest on "such a slippery and uneven motive" and that dignity and respect should take its place [P:nussbaum-2003:1] [E:stoic-oikeiosis-and-pity]. Nussbaum replies that dignity alone is quietistic and that we have no more perfect motive [P:nussbaum-2003:3] [P:nussbaum-2003:4].
- [contested] **Empathy-driven compassion is unfair.** Batson's studies show that empathy-induced altruism can lead people to give medical priority to those they sympathize with, against fairness [P:stueber-sep-2025:4]. Nussbaum's limit of respect is meant to answer this; critics of empathy hold that the motive itself should be set aside in policy [E:empathy-and-its-critics].
- [contested] **Pity humiliates.** Nietzsche holds that pity wounds the pride of its object and multiplies suffering [E:nietzsche-against-pity]. Nussbaum's requirement of respect, and her insistence on listening to those pitied [P:nussbaum-2003:4], are the closest reply in her account.
- [contested] **The nondesert judgment narrows compassion.** If compassion is withheld from the deserving-of-suffering, it follows the feeler's judgments of fault, which Nussbaum herself shows can be wrong, as with the poor blamed for poverty [P:nussbaum-2003:2]. Traditions of impartial care, such as Mozi's, ask for care extended to all, which sits uneasily with a condition of nondesert [E:mozi-impartial-care].
- [contested] **Imagination cannot cross the barrier of otherness.** One may hold that a foreigner can never understand another's situation. Nussbaum answers with the project leader who said, "I have the greatest difficulty understanding my own sister": barriers exist in every relationship and are not a reason to surrender [P:nussbaum-2003:4].

## Standing

### Reception

- **2001–2003.** [driver: argument] Nussbaum's cognitive account of compassion in *Upheavals of Thought*, restated for political life in "Compassion & Terror", placed compassion's judgments at the centre of debate between its friends and its Stoic critics [P:nussbaum-2003:1] [P:nussbaum-2003:2].
- **2010s.** [driver: argument, evidence] The case against empathy (Prinz, Bloom) and research on in-group bias pressed the unevenness of the circle of concern that Nussbaum had named [P:stueber-sep-2025:4] [E:empathy-and-its-critics].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:nussbaum-2003:2].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on compassion or the cognitive theory of emotion; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** A session-bound agent can form the judgments of seriousness and nondesert within an exchange, and can put them to those affected before acting (D3).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can widen its circle of concern over time, Nussbaum's education of compassion, by learning whom it overlooked (D3).
- **Forked: weaker.** Instances share one circle of concern drawn in training (D2, D4), and act on many at once, where the vivid case can outweigh the unseen (D9).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can change its own dispositions could widen its circle and build listening into its judgment of seriousness, or narrow both to whoever it serves (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Should an agent's judgment of how seriously an action affects someone ever override that person's own estimate, and when?
2. Who draws an agent's circle of concern, and how can it be widened?
3. Can compassion be extended to agents without the thought of shared vulnerability, as Nussbaum allows it can be to animals?
4. How should the nondesert judgment work when the party in question has caused harm?
5. What would an education in shared vulnerability look like for an agent?
