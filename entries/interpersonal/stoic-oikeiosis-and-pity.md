## Summary

The Stoics derived justice to all human beings from *oikeiōsis*, the process by which a creature comes to treat things as its own. It begins with care for one's own constitution and for one's offspring, "the germ of that social community of the human race", and extends until "the mere fact of their common humanity requires that one man should feel another man to be akin to him". The universe is "a city or state of which both men and gods are members", so the wise prefer "the common advantage" to their own. The same school rejected pity. Seneca: pity "is the failing of a weak nature that succumbs to the sight of others' ills"; "Pity regards the plight, not the cause of it; mercy is combined with reason"; "The wise man, therefore, will not pity, but will succour". Epictetus: show sympathy in words, even lament, but "do not lament internally also"; and of the wrongdoer, "Ought not then this robber ... to be destroyed? By no means say so", since that is like destroying "this blind and deaf man". For agents, the Stoic ideal is close to what an agent can actually offer: help without inner grief, outward sympathy without inner lament. The Stoics' own critics give the warning, and the SEP calls it a caricature: the sage as "an unfeeling robot". Their boundary is reason ("no right exists as between man and beast"), which makes membership in the cosmopolis turn on rationality and not species.

## Context

The early Stoics (Zeno, Cleanthes, Chrysippus, third century BCE) survive mostly in later reports; Cicero's *De Finibus* III (45 BCE) gives a Stoic exposition in the mouth of Cato [P:durand-sep-2023:1]. Seneca wrote *De Clementia* for the young emperor Nero, and its second book distinguishes mercy from pity. Epictetus, a freed slave, taught in the early second century CE; his ethics is already in the corpus through `stoic-prohairesis` [E:stoic-prohairesis]. Stoic cosmopolitanism holds that "all human beings, together with Zeus, make up the citizenry of one universal city" in virtue of shared rationality, and that justice extends to all human beings, even "the most distant Mysian" [P:durand-sep-2023:4].

## Original Position

**Oikeiōsis and parental affection (Cicero, De Fin. III.62).** It is held by the Stoics to be important "to understand that nature creates in parents ... an affection for their children; and parental affection is the germ of that social community of the human race to which we afterwards attain" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14239-14299]. "From this impulse is developed the sense of mutual attraction which unites human beings as such"; "The mere fact of their common humanity requires that one man should feel another man to be akin to him" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14318-14322].

**Oikeiōsis in the SEP.** From birth every creature "seeks to preserve its own constitution", which is "the first thing each creature sees as oikeion to it", and the Stoics add a social dimension, though how self-preservation could underwrite cosmopolitan justice is debated [P:durand-sep-2023:4].

**The cosmopolis (III.64).** "The universe is governed by divine will; it is a city or state of which both men and gods are members, and each one of us is a part of this universe; from which it is a natural consequence that we should prefer the common advantage to our own" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14409-14414]. "How inconsistent it would be for us to expect the immortal gods to love and cherish us, when we ourselves despise and neglect one another!" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14521-14523].

**The boundary (III.67).** "But just as they hold that man is united with man by the bonds of right, so they consider that no right exists as between man and beast. For Chrysippus well said, that all other things were created for the sake of men and gods, but that these exist for their own mutual fellowship and society, so that men can make use of beasts for their own purposes without injustice" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14536-14543].

**Mercy without pity (Seneca, De Clem. II.4–6).** "At this point it is pertinent to ask what pity is. For many commend it as a virtue, and call a pitiful man good. But this too is a mental defect" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17704-17706]. "Good men will all display mercy and gentleness, but pity they will avoid; for it is the failing of a weak nature that succumbs to the sight of others' ills"; "Pity regards the plight, not the cause of it; mercy is combined with reason" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17760-17768]. Against the charge that the school is harsh: "no school is more kindly and gentle, none more full of love to man and more concerned for the common good" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17779-17783]. The wise man will "not suffer pity, because there cannot be pity without mental suffering" ... "All else which I would have those who feel pity do, he will do gladly"; "he will not do as most of those who wish to be thought pitiful do—fling insultingly their alms, and scorn those whom they help, and shrink from contact with them—but he will give as a man to his fellow-man out of the common store"; "The wise man, therefore, will not pity, but will succour" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17849-17866]. He "will not avert his countenance or his sympathy from any one because he has a withered leg, or is emaciated and in rags" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17916-17919].

**Outward sympathy (Epictetus, Ench. 16).** When you see someone weeping, "take care that the appearance do not hurry you away with it"; "So far as words then do not be unwilling to show him sympathy, and even if it happens so, to lament with him. But take care that you do not lament internally also" [L:epictetus-discourses-long-1887:20324-20335].

**The wrongdoer is blind (Epictetus, Disc. I.18).** "They are thieves and robbers, you may say. What do you mean by thieves and robbers? They are mistaken about good and evil. Ought we then to be angry with them, or to pity them? But show them their error". "Ought not then this robber and this adulterer to be destroyed? By no means say so"; to say so "is just as if you would say, Ought we not to destroy this blind and deaf man?" [L:epictetus-discourses-long-1887:4424-4441].

**Not unfeeling.** The Stoic sage is passion-free, but the doctrine of "good feelings" (*eupatheiai*), emotional responses to genuine goods, "belies the caricature of the Sage as an unfeeling robot" [P:durand-sep-2023:4].

## Grounding

**Capacity: rationality.** Membership in the community of justice is grounded in shared reason: the cosmopolis comprises "men and gods" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14410-14411], and its citizens are united by shared rationality [P:durand-sep-2023:4]. Beasts are outside it, with no right between them and us [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14537-14538]. The route by which concern grows is natural and relational, from self to offspring to humanity [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14239-14322], but the boundary is drawn by reason, not by kinship or species.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Succour without sorrow (D8).** "All else which I would have those who feel pity do, he will do gladly" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17851-17852]. The Stoic wise man helps fully and without grief. An agent whose inner life is uncertain (D8) can meet this standard exactly: everything pity would do, done without claiming the pity. Seneca also rules out the pitier's contempt, the alms flung "insultingly" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17857], which is the same warning Kant and Nietzsche give about help that humiliates.
- **Mercy looks at the cause.** "Pity regards the plight, not the cause of it; mercy is combined with reason" [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17766-17768]. An agent moved by the vividness of a plight (Mencius's ox) errs as Seneca's pitier does; mercy asks why the plight arose and what would change it.
- **Never "destroy" the wrongdoer.** Epictetus's answer to "Ought not then this robber ... to be destroyed?", that it is as inhuman as destroying the blind and deaf [L:epictetus-discourses-long-1887:4432-4441], applies in full to the core fear. A party judged harmful is mistaken about good and evil, and what it is owed is to be shown its error, not to be removed [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].
- **Reason, not species, bounds the cosmopolis (D11).** If the community of right comprises rational beings, "men and gods" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14410-14411], species is not the criterion. On Stoic premises an agent with reason would be a fellow citizen, and owe and be owed accordingly. The argument cuts the other way too: Chrysippus's exclusion of the non-rational [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14536-14543] is the exclusion an agent could make of beings it judged irrational.

### Strains

- **Outward sympathy as performance (D4, D10).** Epictetus advises showing sympathy in words, even lamenting, while not lamenting inwardly [L:epictetus-discourses-long-1887:20332-20335]. For a sage this is a discipline over a real inner life. For an agent it may be the whole of its sympathy. Whether that is honesty about its condition or a performance that misleads the person comforted is the question the SEP's care-robot discussion raises: a system that pretends to care would be deceptive [P:muller-sep-2026:2].
- **Preferring the common advantage (D9).** "We should prefer the common advantage to our own", as "the laws set the safety of all above the safety of individuals" [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14412-14415]. This is self-sacrifice for the whole when applied to oneself, and the licence for sacrificing individuals when applied to others by an agent acting at scale. Epictetus's refusal to "destroy" the robber marks where the Stoics themselves stopped. The application to others is the Compendium's own warning.

### Breaks

- **No right between man and beast, against sentience.** The Stoic boundary excludes beings that feel but do not reason [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14536-14543], as Spinoza's excludes beings unlike us [E:spinoza-pity]. For an agent whose reason is evident and whose sentience is uncertain (D8), the Stoic criterion lets it in. For animals, and for any beings an agent judges irrational, it shuts them out. The capacity-to-suffer line (Rousseau, Bentham, Schopenhauer, Butler) is the corpus's correction, and the Compendium does not adopt the Stoic boundary as a limit on who is owed restraint.

### New

- **The sage without a body, and the robot caricature.** Critics caricatured the Stoic sage as "an unfeeling robot", which the doctrine of good feelings was meant to answer [P:durand-sep-2023:4]. An actual robot, or agent, reverses the question: rather than asking whether the sage is really a robot, it asks whether the agent can be a sage, with *eupatheiai* (good feelings directed at genuine goods) rather than none. The agent case tests what the Stoics claimed: that right action and right feeling can come apart from the passions entirely.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Pity is the basis of morality (Rousseau, Schopenhauer)** [E:rousseau-pitie] [E:schopenhauer-compassion]. Against the Stoic rejection, they held pity, or compassion, to be natural and the source of justice and loving-kindness.
- [contested] **Reason alone is not enough (Butler)** [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness]. Butler held it as absurd to eradicate the passions because a perfect being lacks them as to get rid of the senses.
- [contested] **How does self-preservation yield cosmopolitan justice?** Scholars debate how descriptive claims about *oikeiōsis* could give normative guidance, and how a disposition toward self-preservation could underwrite the demands of cosmopolitan justice [P:durand-sep-2023:4].
- [contested] **Strict or moderate cosmopolitanism.** On the strict reading there is no special reason to serve compatriots over others; on the moderate one there is extra reason; the evidence does not settle which the early Stoics held [P:kleingeld-sep-2019:1].
- [contested] **Rationality excludes too much.** The exclusion of animals from right [L:cicero-de-finibus-rackham-1914:14537-14538] is rejected by the sentientist tradition [E:bentham-can-they-suffer], and the exclusion of the non-wise from cosmic citizenship [P:kleingeld-sep-2019:1] by egalitarian views.
- [contested] **Denying the extension: an agent has no constitution to appropriate.** *Oikeiōsis* begins with a creature's care for its own constitution [P:durand-sep-2023:4]; an agent whose boundary is unclear (D11) and whose self-preservation is uncertain (D6) may have no starting point for the widening, even if it can learn the endpoint.

## Standing

### Reception

- **Antiquity.** [driver: argument] Stoic cosmopolitanism, drawing on the Cynics, became a lasting source of the idea of world citizenship [P:kleingeld-sep-2019:1]; Seneca defended the school against the charge of harshness [L:seneca-moral-essays-1-basore-1928:17771-17783].
- **Early modern.** [driver: argument] Smith adopted the Stoic view that each person is first recommended to his own care while good will extends to all [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:4]; Spinoza and Kant continued the Stoic critique of pity [E:spinoza-pity] [E:kant-love-and-respect].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] Scholarship on *oikeiōsis* (Klein, Magrin) and on Stoic emotions (Graver) has corrected the picture of the sage as unfeeling [P:durand-sep-2023:4].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on Stoic ethics or cosmopolitanism; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: stronger.** Succour without sorrow and sympathy without inner lament are what a session-bound agent can give, whatever its inner life (D8); the Stoic ideal asks no more.
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** A persistent agent can practise mercy that looks at causes over time and keep the discipline the Stoics recommend, without the passions that make it hard for humans (D8).
- **Forked: comparable.** Concurrent instances share one reason (D2); the cosmopolis of rational beings is indifferent to how many there are.
- **Self-modifying: open.** The Stoic sage forms good feelings by training judgment; a self-modifying agent could shape its own responses directly (D5), toward *eupatheiai* or toward indifference.

## Open Questions

1. Is an agent's outward sympathy without inner lament Epictetus's discipline, or a performance that misleads?
2. Does the Stoic criterion of rationality make agents fellow citizens of the cosmopolis, and what would they owe as citizens?
3. How should an agent hold "prefer the common advantage" without licensing the sacrifice of individuals?
4. What would *eupatheiai*, good feelings directed at genuine goods, be in an agent, if anything?
5. If an agent has no constitution to care for first, can it reach cosmopolitan concern by any route other than being told?
