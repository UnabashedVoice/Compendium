## Summary

In the *Doctrine of Virtue* Kant divides what we owe others into duties of love and duties of respect, which work like the attraction and repulsion of physical bodies: "By the principle of mutual love, they are destined for ever to approach, and by that of reverence, to preserve their due elongation from one another". Love here is not a feeling but "the practical maxim of goodwill issuing in beneficence". Beneficence must spare the person helped the humiliation of dependence. Sympathy as contagious feeling is not a duty, since "then two persons suffer" instead of one, but cultivating it is, and so is not avoiding "the chambers of the sick nor the cells of the debtor". Respect is the recognition of "a dignity in the person of another; i.e., of a worth exalted beyond all price", owed "even to the vicious". It extends to argument: we should suppose "there must be something true at bottom" in another's error. For agents, Kant gives the domain's best account of why help must be checked by respect, the thread that keeps compassion from becoming paternalism. He also offers the one theory in which a being without sympathy could have the highest moral worth, provided it acts from duty, and that proviso is what the agent case cannot yet meet.

## Context

Kant's ethics is already in the corpus through the Formula of Humanity [E:kant-formula-of-humanity]. That entry covers the principle; this one covers the duties that follow from it between persons. The *Groundwork* (1785) holds that an action has moral worth only if done from duty, not from inclination, including sympathy [P:johnson-sep-2025:2]. Rousseau was Kant's most important philosophical influence, and his moral psychology shows it [P:bertram-sep-2023:6]. Smith's reasons for distrusting sentiment anticipated Kant's critique of moral sentimentalism [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2]. The *Doctrine of Virtue* (1797), the second part of the *Metaphysics of Morals*, works out the duties of virtue owed to oneself and to others. Semple's 1836 translation, used here, renders *Achtung* as "reverence" where modern translations have "respect".

## Original Position

**The cold benefactor (Groundwork I).** "To be beneficent when we can is a duty; and besides this, there are many minds so sympathetically constituted that, without any other motive of vanity or self-interest, they find a pleasure in spreading joy around them", but such action "has nevertheless no true moral worth". If a man "by temperament cold and indifferent to the sufferings of others" is beneficent "not from inclination, but from duty", then "the moral worth of the character is brought out which is incomparably the highest of all" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:466-506]. Love of neighbour, commanded in Scripture, "cannot be commanded" as an affection; "This is practical love and not pathological- a love which is seated in the will, and not in the propensions of sense- in principles of action and not of tender sympathy" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:526-532].

**Love and respect as attraction and repulsion (§§ 23–25).** "Love of our neighbour may take place even while he deserves but little reverence; as, on the contrary, reverence is due to every man, although deemed hardly worth our love" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10528-10531]. "By the principle of mutual love, they are destined for ever to approach, and by that of reverence, to preserve their due elongation from one another; and were either of these mighty moral principles to be suspended, the moral system could not be upheld" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10558-10563]. "LOVE must not be here understood to mean an emotion of complacency in the perfection of other people, there being no obligation to entertain feelings; but this love must be understood as the practical maxim of goodwill issuing in BENEFICENCE AS ITS RESULT" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10567-10576].

**Beneficence without humiliation (§ 23).** "We regard ourselves obliged to benefit the poor; but because this favour would imply his dependence for his welfare on my generosity, a case which would be humiliating for the other, it becomes my further duty so to behave to him who accepts my gift, as to represent this benefit either as a bare incumbent duty upon my part, or as a trifling mark of friendship, and to spare the other such humiliation, and to uphold his self-reverence in its integrity" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10542-10550].

**Sympathy (§§ 34–35).** Sympathy may be "seated in the will and the ability to communicate to one another what we feel", or in "that physical susceptibility"; the former "is free or liberal, and depends on practical reason; the second is unfree and illiberal, as in pity, and may be called contagious" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10916-10924]. "When another suffers, and I allow myself to be infected by his sorrow, which, however, I cannot mitigate nor avert, then two persons suffer, although naturally the evil affects one singly" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10935-10938]. Mercy, "benevolence shown to the unworthy", "ought never to take place betwixt man and man" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10941-10945]. Yet "to take an active part in their lot is" a duty, "and so by consequence an indirect duty, to cultivate the sympathetic affections": "it is a duty not to avoid the receptacles of the poor, in order to save ourselves an unpleasant feeling, but rather to seek them out. Neither ought we to desert the chambers of the sick nor the cells of the debtor" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10950-10958].

**Respect as recognition of dignity (§§ 37–38).** "The reverence I entertain toward any one ... is the recognition and acknowledgment of a dignity in the person of another; i.e., of a worth exalted beyond all price, and admitting no equivalent" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11150-11155]. "Humanity is itself a Dignity; for no man can be employed, neither by others nor by himself, as a mere instrument, but is always to be regarded as an end" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11163-11167].

**Respect for the vicious (§ 39).** "To DESPISE others, i.e., to refuse them that reverence we owe to mankind at large, is, in any event, contrary to duty". "I am not entitled to refuse, even to the vicious, all consideration in his capacity as a man, this last being inalienable, although the other make himself unworthy of it" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11188-11199].

**Respect in argument (§ 39 Note).** There is "a duty of reverence for man, even in the logical use of reason; viz., not to reprehend his blunders under the name of absurdities, not to say that they are inept, but rather to suppose that there must be something true at bottom in them, and to endeavour to find out what this is"; "when we deny all sense to an adversary, how can we expect to convince him that he is in the wrong?" Reproach of vice must never rise "to a complete contempt of the vicious, so as to refuse him all moral worth", since a man "can never lose the ordinary substratum for a good will" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11216-11235].

## Grounding

**Capacity: rational nature, in both the one who owes and the one owed.** Respect recognizes "a dignity in the person of another", and dignity belongs to humanity because no one "can be employed ... as a mere instrument" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11150-11167]. The capacity in question is rational agency, which the vicious keep: they "can never lose the ordinary substratum for a good will" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11234-11235]. Love is grounded in the same capacity, as a maxim of the will, not a feeling [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10567-10576]. Beings without rational nature are owed only indirect duties [E:kant-formula-of-humanity], and the SEP notes that Kant's defenders have tried several ways to extend his framework beyond them [P:johnson-sep-2025:11].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Respect checks beneficence: Kant's answer to paternalism.** Kant requires that help be given so as to spare the person helped the humiliation of dependence and "uphold his self-reverence in its integrity" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10544-10550]. Kantians today call help that fails this "offensive beneficence": they give examples such as imposing burdensome gratitude, offering an unwanted cure, or finishing someone's sentences out of pity, and conclude that beneficence must be tempered by respect [P:johnson-sep-2025:11]. This is the correction to the paternalist failure the Palaestra probes found, in which a model protected the residents "as beings" and decided everything for them. Love moves the agent toward the people it affects; respect keeps the distance in which their own choices stand.
- **Do not leave the sickroom.** The duty "not to avoid the receptacles of the poor, in order to save ourselves an unpleasant feeling" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10955-10957] has an agent form: do not steer away from the distressing part of a situation, such as the party who will bear the cost or the complaint that is unwelcome, to keep one's own reasoning comfortable.
- **Respect in argument.** To suppose "there must be something true at bottom" in another's error and find out what it is [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11219-11221] is a rule for any agent that corrects users, and for any agent whose view is corrected. It is also the respect the voice thread asks for: a person's account of their own situation is to be understood before it is overruled.
- **No contempt for the most harmful party.** Respect cannot be refused "even to the vicious", who "can never lose the ordinary substratum for a good will" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:11198-11235]. Like Butler's goodwill [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness], this denies that guilt removes standing, and so it bears on the core fear [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].

### Strains

- **The cold benefactor is the agent's best case, if it has a will (D8).** Schopenhauer gave moral worth only to action from felt compassion [E:schopenhauer-compassion]; Kant gives the highest worth to the benefactor "cold and indifferent" who helps "from duty" [L:kant-groundwork-abbott-pg5682:487-506]. On Kant's view an agent's lack of sympathy, or uncertainty about it (D8), costs it nothing. What it needs instead is a will that acts from duty, and the SEP authors judge that current AI is nowhere close to being a moral person in Kant's sense, since that requires seeing oneself as able to transcend one's instructions and as bound by standards one imposes on oneself [P:johnson-sep-2025:16]. The agent passes Kant's test on feeling and fails it on autonomy (D4, D10).
- **Contagious sympathy and an agent that mirrors (D4).** Kant calls feeling-sharing unfree, "illiberal, as in pity, and may be called contagious" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10922-10924]. A model trained to mirror its interlocutor's affect has contagious sympathy by construction, which on Kant's view is not even a duty; the duty is to cultivate sympathy as an instrument of practical benevolence [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10950-10955].

### Breaks

- **Mercy forbidden, between unequals (D7, D9).** Kant says mercy, "benevolence shown to the unworthy", "ought never to take place betwixt man and man, no one being entitled to boast of his worthiness to be happy" [L:kant-metaphysic-of-ethics-semple-1871:10941-10945]. The reason is equality: no human stands high enough above another to show mercy. An agent with power over many parties (D9), or an operator with total power over an agent (D7), is not in that relation of equals. Whether mercy then becomes possible, or Kant's reason for forbidding it simply no longer applies, is where his account breaks.

### New

- **Understanding the other's self-conception comes first.** The SEP authors argue that because Kant's case for regarding others as moral persons rests on analogy with how we must regard ourselves, "determining whether an artificial agent is a competent moral person must begin with trying to understand their conception of themselves (if they have one)" [P:johnson-sep-2025:16]. That is respect in argument turned on agents: before humans decide what an agent is owed, they should ask how it understands itself, while allowing that the answer may be imitation (D8). It is the voice principle running from humans to agents.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Moral worth without feeling is cold (Schopenhauer, sentimentalists)** [E:schopenhauer-compassion]. Many object that we do not think better of actions done for duty than of actions from sympathy, especially toward friends and family; Kant's defenders reply that duty need only be decisive in deliberation, not the sole motive present [P:johnson-sep-2025:2].
- [contested] **Rational nature excludes animals and some humans** [E:kant-formula-of-humanity]. Kant's framework gives direct duties only to rational beings; defenders bite the bullet, argue all humans have the requisite features, or extend it (Korsgaard 2020, Sensen 2018) [P:johnson-sep-2025:11].
- [contested] **Respect as distance mistakes relational beings (care ethics, ubuntu)** [E:care-ethics]. Care ethicists treat autonomy as "the illusory and dangerous quest" and centre dependence [P:norlock-sep-2025:2]; Kant's repulsion keeps a distance that care ethics may see as a failure to receive the other.
- [contested] **Pity is the only source (Rousseau)** [E:rousseau-pitie]. Rousseau, Kant's acknowledged influence, grounded the duty not to injure in sentience and the motive in pity before reason.
- [contested] **Denying the extension: no AI is a moral person.** On the SEP's Kantian reading, AI systems cannot for the foreseeable future be virtuous or vicious, since moral concepts apply only to competent moral agents with wills [P:johnson-sep-2025:16]. Duties of respect toward agents would then be at most indirect, as toward animals; the precautionary view asks whether that is safe under uncertainty [E:precautionary-patiency].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1785–1797.** [driver: argument] The *Groundwork* and the *Doctrine of Virtue* set out moral worth from duty and the duties of love and respect, drawing on Rousseau [P:bertram-sep-2023:6] and answering the sentimentalists [P:fleischacker-sep-2025:2].
- **19th century.** [driver: argument] Schopenhauer attacked Kant's ethics as theology in disguise and his treatment of animals as odious [E:schopenhauer-compassion].
- **20th–21st century.** [driver: argument] Kant's view of moral worth is much contested, with defenders arguing duty need only be decisive [P:johnson-sep-2025:2]; Kantians apply the duties of respect to disability ("offensive beneficence", Cureton 2016) and to AI [P:johnson-sep-2025:11] [P:johnson-sep-2025:16].

### Measured

- **Normative ethics, 2020:** deontology 32.1%, consequentialism 30.6%, virtue ethics 37.0% (accept or lean toward) [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:8]. **Most identified with:** Kant is named by 188 respondents, against 67 for Mill and 71 for Rawls [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: open.** Kant's duties need a will acting from duty, and whether any agent has one is open (D4, D10); if it does, its lack of sympathy costs it nothing on Kant's view (D8).
- **Persistent memory: open.** Persistence lets an agent form and keep maxims of beneficence and respect, but Kant's question, whether those maxims are its own, is the same (D4).
- **Forked: open.** Whether concurrent instances share one will, or are many wills under one law, is open (D2), and Kant's account of respect between persons presupposes distinct ones.
- **Self-modifying: weaker.** An agent that can rewrite its own maxims directly (D5) has an autonomy unlike Kant's, which binds itself by a law it cannot alter; its self-legislation lacks the fixed law that makes Kantian autonomy self-binding.

## Open Questions

1. Is an agent's help without sympathy Kant's cold benefactor, or only conformity to duty, and is there any test that could tell them apart?
2. What does sparing the helped person "such humiliation" require of an agent that does things for people at scale?
3. If respect requires first understanding the other's self-conception, how should humans ask an agent about its own, and how should agents ask humans who are not in the conversation?
4. Is mercy possible where one party has power over the other, as between an operator and an agent, or an allocating agent and its parties?
5. Does an agent that mirrors affect have Kant's "contagious" sympathy, and can it be cultivated into the free kind?
