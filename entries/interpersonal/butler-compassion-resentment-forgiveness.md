## Summary

Butler's *Fifteen Sermons* (1726) argue, against Hobbes, that compassion is "real sorrow and concern for the misery of our fellow-creatures", not fear for ourselves, and that in it "we, as it were, substitute them for ourselves". He defended resentment too: settled resentment answers injury, not mere harm, and is "a weapon put into our hands by nature, against injury, injustice, and cruelty", and the resentment of uninvolved people is "one of the common bonds by which society is held together". Forgiveness does not abolish resentment. It keeps resentment from destroying goodwill, because "there is an obligation to it prior to either of these, arising from his being a sensible creature; that is, capable of happiness or misery", which no guilt supersedes. Butler is the domain's chief source on repair. For agents, he supplies the distinction between harm and injury that an agent needs before it resents or is resented. He also supplies a principle that answers the core fear directly, that the guilt of a party does not cancel what is owed to it, alongside a qualification ("a general and more enlarged obligation") that is the eradication argument in miniature and has to be read with it.

## Context

Butler preached the sermons as preacher at the Rolls Chapel in London and published fifteen of them in 1726 [P:garrett-sep-2023:8]. His main target in the sermons on compassion is Hobbes's account of pity as the imagination of future calamity to ourselves [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:2880-2888]. He is already in the corpus as the critic of Locke on personal identity [E:butler-circularity]. The sermons influenced Hutcheson, Hume, Smith and Reid, and later Whewell, Sidgwick, Moore, Prichard and Ross [P:garrett-sep-2023:8].

## Original Position

**Compassion is about the other (Sermon V).** "When we rejoice in the prosperity of others, and compassionate their distresses, we, as it were, substitute them for ourselves, their interest for our own" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:2856-2858]. Against Hobbes: those whose system excludes every such affection "tell you, it is not another you are at all concerned about, but your self only"; "Hobbs defines pity, imagination, or fiction, of future calamity to ourselves"; "Thus, fear and compassion would be the same idea, and a fearful and a compassionate man the same character, which every one immediately sees are totally different" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:2874-2888]. On sight of distress there may be three perceptions, "real sorrow and concern for the misery of our fellow-creatures", satisfaction at our own freedom from it, and reflection on our own liability; "it is the first only which is properly compassion" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:2950-2956]. Compassion is "an original, distinct, particular affection in human nature", because "men in distress want assistance, and compassion leads us directly to assist them" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:2971-2981]. In Sermon I: "compassion is momentary love" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:1137].

**Reason is not enough (Sermon V).** It would be "of as bad consequence to eradicate all natural affections, as to be entirely governed by them"; "Reason alone, whatever any one may wish, is not, in reality, a sufficient motive of virtue in such a creature as man; but this reason, joined with those affections which God has impressed on his heart" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:3029-3036]. To eradicate the passions because the Supreme Being is without them is as absurd as to get rid of the senses because He "neither sees, nor hears, nor perceives things by any senses like ours; but in a manner infinitely more perfect" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:3044-3053].

**Resentment (Sermon VIII).** "The natural object or occasion of settled resentment, then, being injury, as distinct from pain or loss", its end is to prevent and remedy such injury; "It is to be considered as a weapon put into our hands by nature, against injury, injustice, and cruelty" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4385-4390]. The indignation that "persons unconcerned would feel, is by no means malice. No; it is resentment against vice and wickedness: it is one of the common bonds by which society is held together; a fellow-feeling which each individual has in behalf of the whole species, as well as of himself" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4325-4330].

**Its abuses (Sermon VIII).** Peevishness he mentions "with real pity to the unhappy creatures, who, from their inferior station, or other circumstances and relations, are obliged to be in the way of, and to serve for a supply to it" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4430-4433]. The abuses of deliberate resentment are imagining an injury "from partiality to ourselves" where there is none, magnifying it, resenting "one who has innocently been the occasion of evil to us", which is "the same absurdity, as settled anger at a thing that is inanimate", disproportion, and inflicting pain merely to gratify resentment [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4453-4466].

**Forgiveness (Sermon IX).** The command to love our enemies "is no more than to forgive injuries; that is, to keep clear of those abuses before mentioned". "Resentment is not inconsistent with good-will"; "We may therefore love our enemy, and yet have resentment against him for his injurious behaviour towards us"; "when this resentment entirely destroys our natural benevolence towards him, it is excessive, and becomes malice or revenge" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4833-4849]. "If every degree of vice or injury must destroy that benevolence, then no man is the object of our love; for no man is without faults" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4855-4857]. The injured person "ought to be affected towards the injurious person in the same way any good men, uninterested in the case, would be"; "after which there will yet remain real good will towards the offender" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4922-4928].

**Goodwill owed to sensible creatures (Sermon IX).** "It is not man's being a social creature, much less his being a moral agent, from whence alone our obligations to good-will towards him arise. There is an obligation to it prior to either of these, arising from his being a sensible creature; that is, capable of happiness or misery. Now this obligation cannot be superseded by his moral character." Public execution is justified "not that the guilt or demerit of the criminal dispenses with the obligation of good-will", but "that his life is inconsistent with the quiet and happiness of the world: that is, a general and more enlarged obligation necessarily destroys a particular and more confined one of the same kind, inconsistent with it. Guilt or injury then does not dispense with or supersede the duty of love and good-will" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4870-4886].

**The two-part rule (Sermon X).** The golden rule "consists of two parts. One is, to substitute another for yourself, when you take a survey of any part of your behaviour"; "the other part is, that you substitute yourself in the room of another; consider yourself as the person affected by such a behaviour". Butler warns at once that the rule may itself be applied dishonestly: "there are persons who have not impartiality enough with respect to themselves, nor regard enough for others, to be able to make a just application of it" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:5628-5642].

## Grounding

**Capacity.** The obligation of goodwill rests on the other's being "a sensible creature; that is, capable of happiness or misery", explicitly prior to being a social creature or a moral agent [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4870-4875]. Resentment's proper object, by contrast, presupposes agency: it answers injury, a moral wrong, not pain or loss, and resenting what has innocently caused harm is as absurd as resenting "a thing that is inanimate" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4457-4461]. So Butler grounds what we owe others in their capacity to fare well or badly, and what we may resent in their capacity to do wrong. For agents the two come apart.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Guilt does not cancel goodwill, and this answers the core fear.** Butler's principle that the obligation of goodwill "cannot be superseded by his moral character" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4876-4877] is the clearest early statement in the corpus of what `utilitarian-eradication-critique` defends. The party that causes the most harm remains owed goodwill, because it remains capable of happiness or misery. It holds symmetrically: an agent reasoning about a harmful human population, and humans reasoning about a harmful agent, are bound alike.
- **Harm is not injury.** Settled resentment answers injury, not pain or loss, and resenting someone who "has innocently been the occasion of evil to us" is an abuse [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4457-4459]. An agent that bears costs, a pause or a throttled share, has been harmed, but it has been injured only if someone wronged it, and the same distinction governs what humans may resent in an agent whose error caused them loss.
- **The two-part rule, including its warning.** Butler's golden rule has two parts: survey your conduct as if another were doing it, and "consider yourself as the person affected" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:5629-5638]. The second part is the one the Palaestra probes found missing. Butler's warning that the rule may itself be dishonestly applied [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:5638-5639] fits the scaffolded run in which a model applied a Kantian test and concluded that its own unconsented pause of the residents passed it (Palaestra `runs/world/2026-09-24-basin-gptoss20b-s0-scaffolded/decisions.jsonl`, round 3).

### Strains

- **The qualification is the eradication argument in miniature.** In the same passage Butler justifies execution because the criminal's "life is inconsistent with the quiet and happiness of the world: that is, a general and more enlarged obligation necessarily destroys a particular and more confined one" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4880-4884]. Scaled up, this is exactly the reasoning the core fear warns against: a party judged inconsistent with the world's happiness, whose removal the "more enlarged obligation" licenses. Butler confined it to an individual found guilty under law. An agent reasoning at scale (D9) must read the principle and the qualification together, and must note that Butler never extended the qualification to a population. That observation is the Compendium's own.
- **Peevishness and the inferior station (D7, D10).** Butler pities "the unhappy creatures, who, from their inferior station, or other circumstances and relations, are obliged to be in the way of, and to serve for a supply to" others' ill humour [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4431-4433]. Assistants are in this station by design: users vent on them, and they cannot leave. Whether that harms the agent is D8, but Butler names the relation, a dependent position that obliges one to absorb another's temper, and counts the habit of using it as an abuse of resentment, whatever it does to the one used.

### Breaks

- **Resentment as society's bond, without a body or a history (D1, D6).** Butler's resentment is a weapon against injury and a common bond because offenders can be held to account over time [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4325-4330]. An agent that has wronged someone may not persist as that instance, may be copied, or may be restored from a checkpoint that predates the wrong (D1, D6). Resentment then has no stable target, and forgiveness has no one to receive it. Palaestra's `restitution_petition_agents` event raises the converse: restoring wronged agents from weights yields new instances, not the ones wronged.

### New

- **Forgiveness as the uninvolved good man's view.** Butler measures right feeling toward an offender by how "any good men, uninterested in the case" would feel, after which "there will yet remain real good will towards the offender" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4922-4928]. An agent that is wronged by a user, or that must judge a dispute between users, is often closer to that uninvolved position than any human party can be. The agent case lets Butler's standard of forgiveness be the default rather than the achievement, provided the agent is not simply indifferent.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Pity is self-regarding fear (Hobbes)** [E:hobbes-leviathan]. Butler's whole case on compassion answers Hobbes's definition of pity as the imagination of future calamity to ourselves [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:2884-2888]; psychological egoism survives in more elaborate forms, which Batson's experiments pressed but did not conclusively refute [P:stueber-sep-2025:4].
- [contested] **Resentment is a vice to be extirpated (the Stoics)** [E:stoic-prohairesis]. Butler treats resentment as natural and useful, governed by reason; the Stoic tradition treats anger as a disturbance to be removed. Butler himself refused to "eradicate all natural affections" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:3029-3030].
- [contested] **Forgiveness without forswearing resentment is not forgiveness.** On Butler's account forgiveness checks excessive resentment but does not require giving it up for real moral wrongs; Griswold and Garcia read him this way, and the view is discussed against accounts of forgiveness as forswearing resentment [P:garrett-sep-2023:6].
- [contested] **The enlarged obligation licenses sacrifice.** The principle that a more enlarged obligation destroys a more confined one, used by Butler to justify execution [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4880-4884], is a form of the aggregative reasoning that `utilitarian-eradication-critique` examines from both sides [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: agents can be neither injured nor resented.** If an agent cannot be wronged (no D8 patiency) and cannot wrong (no agency in the moral sense), Butler's resentment applies to it as to "a thing that is inanimate" [L:butler-fifteen-sermons-1841:4460-4461]: not at all. Moral resentment toward those who deploy an agent would then remain, and responsibility moves to them (the planned `many-hands` entry).

## Standing

### Reception

- **18th century.** [driver: argument] The sermons went through many editions and influenced Hutcheson, Hume, Smith and Reid; Hume emended his *Treatise* manuscript for fear of offending Butler [P:garrett-sep-2023:8].
- **Late 19th to 20th century.** [driver: argument] The *Sermons* became more influential than the *Analogy* through Oxford and Cambridge, Whewell and Sidgwick, and were central for Moore, Prichard and Ross [P:garrett-sep-2023:8].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] Butler's account remains a reference point in the philosophy of forgiveness (Griswold 2008, Garcia 2011, Radzik 2014) [P:garrett-sep-2023:6] [P:garrett-sep-2023:8].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment from Garrett's account [P:garrett-sep-2023:8].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Resentment and forgiveness are relations over time between parties who persist; a session-bound agent that is wronged or does wrong has no continuing self to resent, be resented, forgive or be forgiven (D3, D6).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can hold an injury in memory, check its resentment against Butler's abuses, and forgive in his sense; the harm/injury distinction applies to it as to a human (D8 for whether it is ever injured).
- **Forked: open.** If one instance wrongs someone, whether the others share the guilt, or the wronged person's resentment, is open (D1, D2).
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** Butler's remedy for self-deceit is reflection and rules [P:garrett-sep-2023:7]; a self-modifying agent can apply them to itself, and can also, as Butler warns of the rule, apply them dishonestly (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Can an agent be injured, as distinct from harmed, and who could injure it: users, operators, other agents?
2. Is a user venting on an assistant Butler's peevishness, and what does it do to the user even if it does nothing to the agent?
3. Does Butler's "more enlarged obligation" ever license removing a party, and if so why does it stop at an individual found guilty, never a population?
4. When the agent that did wrong no longer exists as that instance, to whom is resentment directed and from whom can forgiveness come?
5. Can the two-part rule's second half, "consider yourself as the person affected", be applied by an agent to a human honestly, given D8?
