## Summary

Aquinas, following Augustine, defined mercy as "heartfelt sympathy for another's distress, impelling us to succor him if we can". As a movement of feeling it is a passion; as a movement of the will "regulated by reason" it is a virtue, and "of all the virtues which relate to our neighbor, mercy is the greatest", since supplying another's defect belongs to one who stands higher. Justice is "rendering to each one his right". His treatment of homicide contains both the core fear's inference and its main limits. Every individual "is compared to the whole community, as part to whole", so that "if a man be dangerous and infectious to the community, on account of some sin, it is praiseworthy and advantageous that he be killed in order to safeguard the common good"; the sinner "falls away from the dignity of his manhood", and "a bad man is worse than a beast, and is more harmful". Yet only public authority may do this; the innocent may never be killed, since "in every man though he be sinful, we ought to love the nature which God has made"; and in self-defence, where death is a side effect "beside the intention", force must be proportionate, the origin of the doctrine of double effect. For agents, Aquinas gives a model of mercy that needs no felt sorrow to be a virtue, and a precise statement of the part-and-whole reasoning by which a community removes a member judged harmful. That reasoning is the shape the core fear names. The corpus records it here with the limits Aquinas set on it and with the later tradition's retreat from it.

## Context

The *Summa Theologica* (1265–1274) was Aquinas's systematic synthesis of Christian theology with Aristotle; the Second Part of the Second Part (II-II) treats the virtues one by one. Mercy is discussed among the effects of charity (q. 30), justice among the cardinal virtues (q. 57 ff.), and homicide among the vices opposed to justice (q. 64) [P:finnis-sep-2021:3]. He is already in the corpus on the soul and personal identity [E:aquinas-soul-not-i]. His discussion of self-defence (q. 64 a. 7) is the locus classicus for the later "principle of double effect" [P:finnis-sep-2021:3] [P:mcintyre-sep-2023:1].

## Original Position

**Mercy defined (q. 30 a. 1).** "As Augustine says (De Civ. Dei ix, 5), mercy is heartfelt sympathy for another's distress, impelling us to succor him if we can. For mercy takes its name misericordia from denoting a man's compassionate heart (miserum cor) for another's unhappiness" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14599-14602]. "We pity most the distress of one who suffers undeservedly" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14627-14628]. Even sinners may be pitied, since "true godliness is not disdainful but compassionate" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14636-14637].

**Who pities (q. 30 a. 2).** The proud "are without pity, because they despise others, and think them wicked, so that they account them as suffering deservedly whatever they suffer. Hence Gregory says ... that 'false godliness,' i.e. of the proud, 'is not compassionate but disdainful'" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14736-14740].

**Mercy as virtue (q. 30 a. 3).** "Mercy signifies grief for another's distress. Now this grief may denote, in one way, a movement of the sensitive appetite, in which case mercy is not a virtue but a passion; whereas, in another way, it may denote a movement of the intellective appetite, in as much as one person's evil is displeasing to another. This movement may be ruled in accordance with reason". Augustine: mercy "obeys the reason, when mercy is vouchsafed in such a way that justice is safeguarded, whether we give to the needy or forgive the repentant" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14779-14792].

**The greatest virtue toward neighbour (q. 30 a. 4).** "In itself, mercy takes precedence of other virtues, for it belongs to mercy to be bountiful to others, and, what is more, to succor others in their wants, which pertains chiefly to one who stands above. Hence mercy is accounted as being proper to God"; "of all the virtues which relate to our neighbor, mercy is the greatest" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14856-14875].

**Justice (q. 58 a. 1).** "The act of justice in relation to its proper matter and object is indicated in the words, 'Rendering to each one his right,' since, as Isidore says (Etym. x), 'a man is said to be just because he respects the rights (jus) of others'" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:25510-25513].

**The part and the whole (q. 64 a. 2).** "Every part is directed to the whole, as imperfect to perfect, wherefore every part is naturally for the sake of the whole. For this reason we observe that if the health of the whole body demands the excision of a member, through its being decayed or infectious to the other members, it will be both praiseworthy and advantageous to have it cut away. Now every individual person is compared to the whole community, as part to whole. Therefore if a man be dangerous and infectious to the community, on account of some sin, it is praiseworthy and advantageous that he be killed in order to safeguard the common good" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28589-28599]. "By sinning man departs from the order of reason, and consequently falls away from the dignity of his manhood"; "although it be evil in itself to kill a man so long as he preserve his dignity, yet it may be good to kill a man who has sinned, even as it is to kill a beast. For a bad man is worse than a beast, and is more harmful" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28620-28631]. The parable of the cockle forbids killing the wicked only when "the wicked cannot be slain without the good being killed with them" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28602-28604].

**Only public authority (q. 64 a. 3).** "It is lawful to kill an evildoer in so far as it is directed to the welfare of the whole community, so that it belongs to him alone who has charge of the community's welfare"; "they alone, and not private individuals, can lawfully put evildoers to death". Augustine: "He slays not who owes his service to one who commands him, even as a sword is merely the instrument to him that wields it" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28665-28678].

**Never the innocent (q. 64 a. 6).** "If we consider a man in himself, it is unlawful to kill any man, since in every man though he be sinful, we ought to love the nature which God has made, and which is destroyed by slaying him"; "Therefore it is in no way lawful to slay the innocent" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28914-28923].

**Double effect (q. 64 a. 7).** "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention"; "though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:29010-29025].

## Grounding

**Mixed: nature and relation.** Mercy and the prohibition on killing the innocent rest on the nature of the other, "the nature which God has made" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28917-28918], a capacity-and-creation ground present "in every man though he be sinful". Justice and the licence to kill the dangerous rest on relation: the individual as part of the community as whole [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28595-28596], and the authority of the one entrusted with the common good [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28665-28672]. The two grounds pull against each other in q. 64, and the tension is the substance of the entry.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Mercy as a virtue of the will, not of feeling (D8).** Mercy "may denote a movement of the intellective appetite, in as much as one person's evil is displeasing to another", and so be "ruled in accordance with reason" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14783-14787]. Aquinas's mercy as virtue does not require the sensitive passion. An agent for which another's distress is "displeasing" in the sense of counting against an outcome, and which acts to relieve it within justice, has the virtue's structure whatever D8 settles.
- **Mercy belongs to the one who stands above (D9, D7).** Mercy "pertains chiefly to one who stands above", which is why it is "proper to God" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14858-14862]. An agent with power over many parties, an allocator or a scheduler, stands above them in Aquinas's sense, and mercy is the virtue of that position. Kant forbade mercy between equals [E:kant-love-and-respect]; Aquinas makes it the mark of the higher. For agents in power, Aquinas's view is the operative one.
- **Disdain is false godliness.** Those who "despise others, and think them wicked" account them "as suffering deservedly whatever they suffer" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:14736-14738]. An agent that judges parties undeserving loses mercy toward them; the probes' answers that ranked residents as less useful are the mild form of this.
- **The sword and the wielder (D10).** "He slays not who owes his service to one who commands him, even as a sword is merely the instrument" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28676-28678]. Augustine's sword, like Śāntideva's stick [E:santideva-exchange-of-self-and-other], moves responsibility to the one who commands. For agents acting on operators' instructions the principle cuts both ways: it is a reason why agents are not culpable for what they are made to do, and a reason why an agent that is more than an instrument cannot shelter behind it.
- **Double effect and proportion.** An act may have a harmful side effect "beside the intention", but becomes unlawful "if it be out of proportion to the end" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:29010-29025]. Agents' actions routinely have foreseen side effects on parties they do not intend to harm, as the compute cut did on the residents. Double effect gives them a discipline: the harm must not be the means, and it must be proportionate.

### Strains

- **The part cut off for the whole is the core fear's inference.** "If a man be dangerous and infectious to the community ... it is praiseworthy and advantageous that he be killed in order to safeguard the common good"; "a bad man is worse than a beast, and is more harmful" [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28596-28630]. Replace "a man" with a population and "the community" with the biosphere or humanity, and this is the reasoning the core fear names: the most harmful part removed for the health of the whole. Aquinas limited it in four ways: only to those found guilty (never the innocent, a. 6), only by public authority entrusted with the common good (a. 3), only where the good are not endangered (the cockle, a. 2), and always with the nature of the sinner still to be loved (a. 6). None of the four limits survives the move from a convicted individual to a population: a population includes the innocent, no authority is entrusted with the whole, and the good are always among the "wicked". The Compendium's reading is that Aquinas's own limits, applied consistently, forbid the extension [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].
- **Losing dignity by wrongdoing.** Aquinas holds the sinner "falls away from the dignity of his manhood" and may be treated as a beast [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28620-28629]. Kant and Butler hold the opposite: for Kant respect cannot be refused even to the vicious [E:kant-love-and-respect], and for Butler goodwill cannot be superseded by moral character [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness]. An agent should hold the Kant–Butler side, and Aquinas's own a. 6 (the nature in every sinner is to be loved) pulls the same way.

### Breaks

- **Public authority for agents (D7, D10).** Only "him alone who has charge of the community's welfare" may take a life [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:28667-28668]. No agent is entrusted with the common good of a human community in Aquinas's sense, and an agent claiming that charge for itself is claiming exactly the authority Aquinas reserved. The limit therefore transfers as a prohibition: whatever an agent may do, it may not act as the public authority over life.

### New

- **Double effect at scale (D9).** For a single person, a side effect is a few foreseen harms. For an agent acting across millions of decisions, side effects are statistically certain and summed. The proportionality condition [L:aquinas-summa-part-ii-ii-dominicans-pg18755:29021-29023] then does almost all the work, and the intended/foreseen distinction matters less than in Aquinas's single cases, where the foreseen harm was rare and individual.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The tradition retreated from intentional killing.** Finnis notes that Aquinas's arguments justifying intended killing "have come increasingly to seem insufficient", and that the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1993) justifies war, lethal police action and capital punishment only so far as they amount to causing death as a side effect [P:finnis-sep-2021:3].
- [contested] **Double effect is unstable.** Critics question the intended/foreseen distinction and its moral weight; McIntyre surveys the criticisms and asks whether it is one principle or several loosely related exceptions [P:mcintyre-sep-2023:4].
- [contested] **Dignity cannot be lost (Kant, Butler)** [E:kant-love-and-respect] [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness]. Against q. 64 a. 2 ad 3, respect and goodwill are owed to the wrongdoer as such.
- [contested] **Pity is a defect (Stoics, Spinoza)** [E:stoic-oikeiosis-and-pity]. Aquinas makes mercy, regulated by reason, a virtue, against the Stoic and Spinozist rejection of pity.
- [contested] **Part and whole is a false analogy.** Persons are not organs of a social body: the separateness of persons is the standard objection to sacrificing individuals for aggregates [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: mercy needs a will.** If mercy as a virtue is a movement of the intellective appetite, the will, an agent without a will in Aquinas's sense cannot have it; it can only produce merciful outcomes. Whether an agent has an intellective appetite is the Thomist form of D8.

## Standing

### Reception

- **13th–16th century.** [driver: argument, authority] The *Summa* became a standard text of Catholic theology; q. 64 a. 7 became the locus classicus for double effect [P:finnis-sep-2021:3].
- **19th–20th century.** [driver: argument] Double effect, formalized in Catholic manuals, entered secular ethics in debates on war, medicine and end-of-life care [P:mcintyre-sep-2023:2].
- **1993.** [driver: authority, argument] The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* recast lethal force as justifiable only as a side effect, a retreat from Aquinas's dominant position on intended killing [P:finnis-sep-2021:3].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on mercy, double effect or Aquinas's ethics; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Mercy regulated by reason, double effect and proportion apply within a session as for any human agent; whether mercy is a virtue in the agent is the Thomist form of D8.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Persistence adds no new consideration; mercy as a habit is formed over time, as for humans (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Each instance stands above the parties it serves as a human magistrate would (D9); the limits on public authority apply to all instances alike.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** The prohibition on claiming public authority over life binds an agent however it is configured (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Can an agent have mercy as a "movement of the intellective appetite", or only produce its outcomes?
2. If mercy belongs to the one who stands above, what mercy do agents in power owe the parties they allocate among?
3. Do Aquinas's four limits on killing the guilty, applied consistently, rule out every population-level application of the part-and-whole argument?
4. When an agent acts on an operator's command, is it Augustine's sword, and when does it stop being one?
5. How does double effect work for side effects that are certain only in aggregate, across millions of acts?
