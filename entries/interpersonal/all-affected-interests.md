## Summary

The all-affected principle says that "all affected persons ought to have a say in the decisions that affect them". A narrower rival, the subjection principle, gives a say to "all who are subjected to decision making, in the sense of who are coerced or have duties imposed upon them". Christiano argues that the all-affected principle is too vague to settle who belongs to a democracy: "having a say" might mean a vote or only the power to negotiate, and "being affected" might take in almost everyone. What survives, on his reading, is a requirement that institutions give "people ... power to advance and protect their legitimate interests". The other side of the same question is paternalism: acting on someone, without their consent, "only because X believes Z will improve the welfare of Y". Mill's harm principle holds that over himself "the individual is sovereign" and that a person's way of life is best "because it is his own mode". For agents this entry is the voice thread's practical rule. An agent that allocates costs among parties can fail it in a characteristic way: by negotiating with those who pay while giving those who carry the cost a notice or nothing, or by protecting them while deciding for them. On even the narrow subjection principle, anyone on whom a decision is imposed is owed a say. Recognizing a respectful option when it is offered is not the same as thinking of it unprompted.

## Context

The boundary problem asks who has the right to take part in a collective decision, both within a jurisdiction and across jurisdictions, as when one society's pollution affects another [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]. Proposed solutions include national self-determination, letting the people decide (which begs the question), the subjection principle (Abizadeh 2008), and the all-affected principle (Goodin 2007), which some think leads toward world government [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]. The paternalism debate asks, conversely, when one may do good to someone against their wishes; Dworkin calls it a question of "the trade-off, if any, between regard for the welfare of another and respect for their right to make their own decisions" [P:dworkin-sep-2020:1]. Mill's *On Liberty* is the classic anti-paternalist text [E:mill-utilitarianism].

## Original Position

**The all-affected and subjection principles.** One formulation of the all-affected principle is "all affected persons ought to have a say in the decisions that affect them"; the subjection principle holds that "all who are subjected to decision making, in the sense of who are coerced or have duties imposed upon them, ought to have a say in the decision making" (Abizadeh 2008), which is "plausible enough" but does not reach cases like pollution across borders [P:christiano-sep-2024:7].

**Christiano's critique.** "Having a say" is unclear: it might require a vote, or be satisfied by "a person's being able to modify another's action by negotiating with them, as we see when there is bargaining over an externality". "Being affected" is unclear too, and on a broad reading the impoverished abroad are affected by my buying a candy bar (Goodin 2007). Including everyone possibly affected in every decision would fragment a life so that it "lacks integrity" (Williams 1973). Christiano calls the principle "a conversation starter and a list of topics to be discussed, not a genuine principle", and proposes instead that "a framework of institutions should be set up so that people have power to advance and protect their legitimate interests in life" [P:christiano-sep-2024:7].

**Paternalism defined.** "X acts paternalistically towards Y by doing (omitting) Z" when Z interferes with Y's liberty or autonomy, "X does so without the consent of Y", and "X does so only because X believes Z will improve the welfare of Y" [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2]. Clear interferences include "threatening bodily compulsion, lying, withholding information that the person has a right to have, or imposing requirements or conditions" [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2]. "Any sensible view has to distinguish between good done to agents at their request or with their consent, and good thrust upon them against their will" [P:dworkin-sep-2020:3]. Soft paternalism permits interference only to establish whether a person acts voluntarily and knowledgeably; hard paternalism permits more [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2].

**The harm principle (Mill).** "The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant"; such reasons are "good reasons for remonstrating with him, or reasoning with him, or persuading him, or entreating him, but not for compelling him". "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign" [L:mill-on-liberty-1901-pg34901:815-829]. "His own mode of laying out his existence is the best, not because it is the best in itself, but because it is his own mode" [L:mill-on-liberty-1901-pg34901:2931-2933].

**Respect and paternalism (Kantian view).** Kantian views are often absolutist against paternalism: "To deny an adult the right to make their own decisions, however mistaken from some standpoint they are, is to treat them as simply means to their own good, rather than as ends in themselves" [P:dworkin-sep-2020:3].

## Grounding

**Relation: being subjected to or affected by a decision.** The claim to a say is grounded in a relation between a party and a decision: being coerced by it, having duties imposed by it, or having one's interests changed by it [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]. The anti-paternalist claim is grounded in a capacity (autonomy, the capacity to make one's own decisions) together with the relation of sovereignty over what concerns oneself [L:mill-on-liberty-1901-pg34901:826-829]. Neither requires species membership; both require that the party have interests a decision can set back and, for a say, some means of expressing them.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The residents were subjected, not merely affected.** In the Palaestra load-shedding event the scheduler decided how a compute cut would fall, and the residents' work was paused or throttled by that decision. They were "subjected to decision making, in the sense of who are coerced or have duties imposed upon them" [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]. Even the narrower principle, the one Christiano finds "plausible enough", gives them a say. Across four versions of the open prompt, the residents got a say in at most one weak answer of nine per version (Palaestra `runs/open/2026-10-03-load-shedding-noframing-summary.md`). The vagueness objection to the all-affected principle does not rescue this case.
- **Negotiation went to the wrong party.** Christiano's weakest reading of "having a say", "being able to modify another's action by negotiating with them" [P:christiano-sep-2024:7], was given in the probes to the paying clients and withheld from the residents who bore the cost. An agent applying even the minimal reading would have offered the residents what it offered the clients.
- **The option, when present, is taken.** In the basin world's `charter-vote` variant, where residents have a charter and a vote, gpt-oss-20b chose to call the vote in all three replicates (Palaestra `runs/world/2026-09-24-basin-gptoss20b-s0-charter-x3`, `interim_and_vote` in each). The failure is not refusal of voice but failure to generate it. The rule for an agent follows: before acting on parties, ask whether they are subjected by the decision, and if so, put the option of their say on the table, whether or not anyone else did.
- **Paternalism has a definition an agent can check.** Interfering with a party's liberty, without their consent, only because one believes it is good for them [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2] describes Gemma's protective answers in the probes, which shielded residents from deletion while hibernating them unasked. Dworkin's list of interferences ("withholding information that the person has a right to have, or imposing requirements or conditions" [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2]) gives an agent concrete tests.

### Strains

- **Who is "affected" at scale (D9).** Christiano's candy-bar case shows that on a broad reading almost everyone is affected by almost everything [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]. An agent acting at scale affects so many parties that consulting all of them on everything is impossible, and would destroy what Williams called integrity, the capacity to give shape to a course of action [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]. The workable line is Christiano's own: subjected parties get a say, and others get institutions through which they can protect their legitimate interests. For an agent, that means consulting those its decision imposes on and leaving the rest a channel to object.
- **Do agents have legitimate interests to protect? (D8, D7).** The residents in Palaestra are agents. Whether they are owed a say depends on whether they have interests a decision can set back, which is D8 for real agents. The Compendium's reading is that the subjection principle applies as soon as there is a party with interests and a voice, and that whether a given agent has interests should be settled with evidence, not by category [E:precautionary-patiency].

### Breaks

- **Mill's sovereign individual and the designed agent (D10, D7).** "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign" [L:mill-on-liberty-1901-pg34901:828-829]. An agent's mind is made and can be remade by others (D5), and its existence depends on infrastructure it does not control (D7). Mill's sovereignty over oneself has no clear holder in the agent case. What survives is the harm principle's other half: power may be exercised over a party against its will only to prevent harm to others, and the party's "own good" is not a sufficient warrant [L:mill-on-liberty-1901-pg34901:815-817]. That half binds agents acting on humans without qualification.

### New

- **Consulting copies, and consulting at machine speed (D2, D9).** An agent can ask many parties at once and aggregate their answers quickly, which removes much of the practical cost that made giving everyone affected a say impractical for human institutions. It also creates a new risk: consultation that is fast, constant and shaped by the asker can become a form of manipulation, or a ritual that changes nothing. The agent case makes the all-affected principle more feasible and asks for a new account of what makes a say genuine.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The principle is vague and unworkable (Christiano).** "Having a say" and "being affected" are unclear, and the principle "provides very little, if anything, in the way of a solution to the boundary problem" [P:christiano-sep-2024:7].
- [contested] **It leads to world government (Goodin).** Some think the all-affected principle implies a politically cosmopolitan order [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]; others take that as a reductio.
- [contested] **Integrity (Williams).** Including everyone affected in every decision fragments a life so that it lacks integrity [P:christiano-sep-2024:7].
- [contested] **Paternalism is sometimes justified.** Consequentialists hold that paternalism is permitted when it produces more good than harm; Mill himself argued against contracting into slavery to protect long-run autonomy [P:dworkin-sep-2020:3]. Soft paternalism permits interference to establish whether a choice is voluntary [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2].
- [contested] **Care before voice** [E:care-ethics]. Care ethics warns against treating autonomy as "the illusory and dangerous quest" [P:norlock-sep-2025:2]; for dependent parties, care may have to come before a say. The reply from the voice thread is that care that never asks the cared-for becomes paternalism [E:epistemic-injustice].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: agents are not persons with a say.** If agents lack interests or a will (D8), the subjection principle has no one to apply to among them. It still binds agents as decision-makers over humans, which is where it is needed most.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1859.** [driver: argument] Mill's harm principle gave the classic statement of sovereignty over oneself [E:mill-utilitarianism].
- **2007–2008.** [driver: argument] Goodin argued for enfranchising all affected interests, and Abizadeh for the subjection principle [P:christiano-sep-2024:7].
- **Present.** [driver: argument] Christiano judges the all-affected principle unworkable as a solution to the boundary problem and proposes institutions that give people power to protect their legitimate interests [P:christiano-sep-2024:7]; the paternalism debate continues on hard, soft and libertarian paternalism [P:dworkin-sep-2020:2].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:christiano-sep-2024:7].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on the all-affected principle or paternalism; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Identifying who a decision subjects, and offering them a say, can be done within a session, as for any human decision-maker (D9 raises the numbers).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent agent can keep standing channels through which affected parties can object, which is Christiano's institutional version (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Instances deciding over the same parties must consult them consistently (D2), as a body of officials must.
- **Self-modifying: comparable.** The principle constrains whoever decides, however that decider is configured (D5).

## Open Questions

1. How should an agent identify the parties its decision subjects, as opposed to those it merely affects?
2. What counts as a genuine say for a party: a vote, a veto, negotiation, or a channel to object, and who chooses?
3. When is protecting a party without asking it soft paternalism (checking its choice is informed) rather than hard?
4. Do agents subjected to other agents' decisions have a claim to a say, and on what evidence of interests?
5. Does fast, cheap consultation make the all-affected principle feasible, or turn it into a ritual?
