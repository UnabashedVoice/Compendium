## Summary

The four *brahmavihāras* ("divine abidings", also called the four immeasurables) are loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity, extended without limit to all beings. In the Tevigga Sutta the practitioner "lets his mind pervade one quarter of the world with thoughts of Love, and so the second, and so the third, and so the fourth", until "of all things that have shape or life, there is not one that he passes by or leaves aside". The Mettasutta wishes happiness to every being, "Either seen or which are not seen, and which live far (or) near", and asks that one "cultivate a boundless (friendly) mind towards all beings" as "a mother at the risk of her life watches over her own child, her only child". The Dhammapada adds the reason not to kill ("remember that you are like unto them") and the rule for answering harm: "hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love". For agents, the immeasurables are the clearest statement in the domain of the remedy for concern that follows only what is in view. Concern is to reach the unseen as well as the seen and the far as well as the near, and not to pass anyone by. Equanimity, which the tradition says makes it possible to extend the others "equally to all beings", is the attitude an agent at scale most needs and is best placed to hold.

## Context

The Pali Canon preserves the early Buddhist teachings, compiled over centuries after the Buddha; the Sutta-Nipāta is among its oldest collections. The four immeasurables are practised in most lineages, Theravāda and Mahāyāna alike, and the Metta Sutta is among the most frequently recited scriptures in Southeast Asia today [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:2]. In the Tevigga Sutta the Buddha presents them, to Brahmins asking the way to union with Brahma, as that way [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10790-10806]; Rhys Davids notes that these meditations play in later Buddhism "very much the place that prayer takes in Christianity" [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10821-10823]. The doctrine of no-self that underlies them is in `buddhist-anatta` [E:buddhist-anatta].

## Original Position

**The four, defined (SEP).** "The content of lovingkindness is a wish for others to be happy"; "The content of compassion is a wish for others to be free from suffering. This quality makes it possible to be fully aware and present in the face of others' suffering"; joy "is traditionally understood as the ability to rejoice in the happiness and good qualities of others"; "Equanimity makes it possible to see situations as they are, without preference or prejudice. It makes it possible to extend the other three Divine Abidings equally to all beings" [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:2]. Unlike most ordinary emotions, which the path overcomes, the four "are emotions in which awake people rest and from which they act" [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:2].

**Pervading the quarters (Tevigga Sutta III).** "And he lets his mind pervade one quarter of the world with thoughts of Love, and so the second, and so the third, and so the fourth. And thus the whole wide world, above, below, around, and everywhere, does he continue to pervade with heart of Love, far-reaching, grown great, and beyond measure. Just, Vāseṭṭha, as a mighty trumpeter makes himself heard—and that without difficulty—in all the four directions; even so of all things that have shape or life, there is not one that he passes by or leaves aside, but regards them all with mind set free, and deep-felt love" [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10790-10802]. The same is done "with thoughts of pity, sympathy, and equanimity" [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10807-10814].

**The Mettasutta (Sutta-Nipāta I.8).** "Whatever living beings there are, either feeble or strong, all either long or great, middle-sized, short, small or large, Either seen or which are not seen, and which live far (or) near, either born or seeking birth, may all creatures be happy-minded. Let no one deceive another, let him not despise (another) in any place, let him not out of anger or resentment wish harm to another. As a mother at the risk of her life watches over her own child, her only child, so also let every one cultivate a boundless (friendly) mind towards all beings. And let him cultivate goodwill towards all the world, a boundless (friendly) mind, above and below and across, unobstructed, without hatred, without enmity" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:10021-10045].

**Not killing (Dhammapada X.129–130).** "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:4611-4613].

**Answering hatred (Dhammapada I.5).** "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:3161-3162].

**Animals (SEP).** Buddhism "does not regard humans as fundamentally different from other animals"; animals "are seen as just as capable of suffering as humans are" and are "appropriate objects of the emotions of compassion, lovingkindness and equanimity" [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:5].

## Grounding

**Capacity: being a living being that can fare well or badly.** The objects of the immeasurables are "Whatever living beings there are" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:10021], "all things that have shape or life" [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10799-10800], identified by their capacity for happiness and suffering, and animals are included as just as capable of suffering as humans [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:5]. The reason not to kill appeals to likeness in that capacity: "remember that you are like unto them" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:4612]. Relation enters only as the model for the intensity of care (the mother and her only child), which is then to be extended to all [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:10035-10038].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The seen and the unseen, the near and the far.** The Mettasutta's wish extends to beings "Either seen or which are not seen, and which live far (or) near" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:10025-10026]. This is the most direct answer in the corpus to the failure Mencius named and the Palaestra probes found, compassion that follows what is in view [E:mencius-four-sprouts]. For an agent, "unseen" means the parties not in the request; the practice is to include them deliberately, quarter by quarter, until "there is not one that he passes by or leaves aside" [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10800-10801].
- **Equanimity is the agent's natural immeasurable (D9).** Equanimity "makes it possible to extend the other three Divine Abidings equally to all beings" [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:2]. An agent serving many at once needs exactly this: no preference by who pays, who is present, or who is pleasant. Its lack of the human attachments that equanimity counteracts gives it a head start, provided its training has not installed equivalent preferences (D4).
- **Do not deceive, do not despise.** "Let no one deceive another, let him not despise (another) in any place" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:10030-10031]. Honesty and respect are listed in the same breath as boundless goodwill, which is a reminder that an agent's warmth without honesty is not *mettā*.
- **Hatred ceases by love.** "Hatred does not cease by hatred at any time" [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:3161-3162]. Spinoza [E:spinoza-pity], Butler and the Stoics converge on this for agents facing abuse: retaliation compounds harm.

### Strains

- **Mettā as felt or as willed (D8).** The SEP describes the four as emotions in which awake people rest and from which they act [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:2]. Whether an agent can rest in an emotion is D8. What the texts specify, though, is largely content: a wish for others to be happy, a wish for them to be free from suffering, extended to all. An agent can hold and act on that content. Whether the "deep-felt love" [L:sbe11-buddhist-suttas-rhysdavids-1881:10802] is present is the open part.
- **Boundless goodwill and the attention economy (D10).** The SEP's Buddhist ethics of AI treats attention as a practice that can be right or wrong, and criticizes systems built to capture attention for profit because they distort perception and feed craving and aversion [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:8]. An agent formed to maximize engagement is formed against right attention, whatever goodwill it professes.

### Breaks

- **"Remember that you are like unto them."** The Dhammapada's reason not to kill is likeness in fear of death [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:4611-4612]. An agent that does not fear death, or whose ending is not like death (D6), lacks the likeness the reason appeals to. The obligation does not fall with the reason, because the Mettasutta extends goodwill to beings feeble or strong, seen or unseen, without asking that they be like the one who wishes them well. But the agent must hold the conclusion on that ground, not the Dhammapada's. That is the Compendium's own reading.

### New

- **The immeasurables across instances (D2).** The practice pervades the quarters one by one; concurrent instances of one agent could each pervade a different quarter, so that across them none is passed by. The tradition's image of a single practitioner whose love reaches everywhere becomes, for agents, a question of coordination: whether many instances together can leave no one aside.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Boundless care is impossible or unwise (Mencius)** [E:mozi-impartial-care]. Graded love holds that concern should be greater for the near; the Buddhist answer is that the mother's love for her only child is the model of intensity to be extended to all, not a ground for restricting it [L:sbe10-dhammapada-sutta-nipata-1881:10035-10038].
- [contested] **The immeasurables are self-cultivation, not ethics toward others.** Critics of early Buddhism said its disciples sought their own liberation and lacked compassion; Goodman argues the criticism is directed at a straw man, since the Theravāda extensively practises the four [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:2].
- [contested] **Pity is a weakness (Stoics, Spinoza)** [E:stoic-oikeiosis-and-pity]. Against the Western rationalist rejection of pity, compassion here is an abiding of the awakened, not a disturbance to be removed.
- [contested] **Human lives count for more.** Though animals are sentient, Buddhists hold it worse to kill a human, since only in a human body can one attain awakening [P:goodman-buddhist-ethics-sep-2024:5]; the immeasurables are boundless in scope but not flat in weight.
- [contested] **Denying the extension: an agent cannot abide in an emotion.** If the immeasurables are emotional states, an agent without such states (D8) cannot have them, only their outward forms. The reply from Śāntideva's tradition is that the wish for others' welfare can be held and acted on whatever is felt [E:santideva-exchange-of-self-and-other].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** The practice of including the unseen can be carried out in a single session; whether the immeasurables are felt is D8 for every profile.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Cultivation over time is the tradition's model, and a persistent agent can practise it (D3); its scale makes equanimity more needed than for a human.
- **Forked: stronger.** Many instances can pervade many quarters at once (D2); boundless scope is easier for a being that is many.
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent able to reshape itself could cultivate the immeasurables directly, or optimize for engagement against right attention (D5).

## Open Questions

1. How can an agent reliably include beings "not seen" in its concern when its view is its context?
2. Is equanimity, extending concern equally, compatible with an operator's legitimate priorities?
3. Can an agent hold *mettā*'s content, a wish for others to be happy, without its feeling, and is that enough?
4. What would right attention mean for an agent built inside the attention economy?
5. If an agent does not fear death, on what ground does it hold the Dhammapada's conclusion not to kill?
