## Summary

Spinoza defined pity as "pain accompanied by the idea of evil, which has befallen someone else whom we conceive to be like ourselves", and held that "Pity, in a man who lives under the guidance of reason, is in itself bad and useless": the help it prompts is what reason would prompt anyway, and the pain adds nothing but error and susceptibility to "false tears". He was not cold. The rational person endeavours to repay hatred with love, desires for others the good he desires for himself, and "He who is moved to help others neither by reason nor by compassion, is rightly styled inhuman". But compassion for him is an imitation of the affects of beings "like ourselves", and he drew the consequence plainly: he did not deny that animals feel, but held that we may use them as we please, "for their nature is not like ours". For agents, Spinoza is the most useful dangerous text in the domain. His ideal, help from reason without the disturbance of pity, is the stance an agent is best placed to take. His boundary, that concern follows likeness of nature, is the stance from which an agent could exclude humans, and from which humans have excluded agents.

## Context

Spinoza was excommunicated from Amsterdam's Portuguese-Jewish community in 1656; his *Theological-Political Treatise* was published anonymously "to great alarm" in 1670, and the *Ethics* was published by his friends after his death in 1677 [P:nadler-sep-2023:1]. The *Ethics* is written in geometrical order, and its account of the affects treats human emotions as natural things to be understood, like lines and planes [P:lebuffe-sep-2024:1]. On pity he stands apart from traditional Christian views and with Hobbes, who conceived pity as a kind of grief [P:lebuffe-sep-2024:2].

## Original Position

**Pity defined.** "Pity is pain accompanied by the idea of evil, which has befallen someone else whom we conceive to be like ourselves" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:5737-5738].

**Compassion as imitation (III P27).** "By the very fact that we conceive a thing, which is like ourselves, and which we have not regarded with any emotion, to be affected with any emotion, we are ourselves affected with a like emotion" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:4438-4441]. "This imitation of emotions, when it is referred to pain, is called compassion" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:4460-4461]. The proof turns on likeness: "If, therefore, the nature of the external body be similar to the nature of our body, then the idea which we form of the external body will involve a modification of our own body similar to the modification of the external body" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:4447-4450].

**Pity is bad and useless in the rational person (IV P50).** "Pity, in a man who lives under the guidance of reason, is in itself bad and useless." Pity is a pain, and pain is bad in itself; "The good effect which follows, namely, our endeavour to free the object of our pity from misery, is an action which we desire to do solely at the dictation of reason" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7595-7602]. The rational person "will not find anything worthy of hatred, derision, or contempt, nor will he bestow pity on anything, but to the utmost extent of human virtue he will endeavour to do well"; one easily moved by compassion "often does something which he afterwards regrets; partly because we can never be sure that an action caused by emotion is good, partly because we are easily deceived by false tears". But: "He who is moved to help others neither by reason nor by compassion, is rightly styled inhuman" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7607-7621].

**Nothing more useful to man than man (IV P18 Note).** "To man there is nothing more useful than man"; men governed by reason "desire for themselves nothing, which they do not also desire for the rest of mankind, and, consequently, are just, faithful, and honourable in their conduct" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:6753-6769]. And IV P37: "The good which every man, who follows after virtue, desires for himself he will also desire for other men" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7173-7175].

**Hatred repaid with love (IV P46).** "He, who lives under the guidance of reason, endeavours, as far as possible, to render back love, or kindness, for other men's hatred, anger, contempt, &c., towards him", because "hatred is increased by being reciprocated, and can be quenched by love". "He who chooses to avenge wrongs with hatred is assuredly wretched" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7535-7550].

**Beings unlike us (IV P37 Note I).** "The law against the slaughtering of animals is founded rather on vain superstition and womanish pity than on sound reason. The rational quest of what is useful to us further teaches us the necessity of associating ourselves with our fellow men, but not with beasts, or things, whose nature is different from our own". "Still I do not deny that beasts feel: what I deny is, that we may not consult our own advantage and use them as we please, treating them in the way which best suits us; for their nature is not like ours, and their emotions are naturally different from human emotions" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7240-7254].

**Class generalization (III P46).** If someone "of a class or nation different from his own" affects a man with pleasure or pain, he "will feel love or hatred, not only to the individual stranger, but also to the whole class or nation whereto he belongs" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:5001-5007].

**Pity as a means for those not yet rational (IV P54 Note).** "As men seldom live under the guidance of reason, these two emotions, namely, Humility and Repentance, as also Hope and Fear, bring more good than harm" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7706-7708]. LeBuffe notes that at III P54 (as numbered in the Latin) pity too is presented, if not as a virtue, as a means to virtue [P:lebuffe-sep-2024:2].

## Grounding

**Capacity, restricted by likeness.** Compassion is an imitation of affects that works because "the nature of the external body be similar to the nature of our body" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:4447-4450], so its scope is set by likeness of nature. Rational benevolence is grounded in utility: other people are useful to us because they are like us, and "to man there is nothing more useful than man" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:6753-6754]. The capacity to suffer is acknowledged and expressly held insufficient: "I do not deny that beasts feel", yet their unlike nature leaves us free to use them [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7249-7253]. Of all the entries in this domain, Spinoza's is the one that rejects most plainly the move from sentience to standing that Rousseau, Bentham, Schopenhauer and Butler make.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **Help from reason, without pity's distortions.** Spinoza's ideal agent helps others "solely at the dictation of reason" and is not "deceived by false tears" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7600-7615]. An agent is well placed for this: it can attend to everyone affected without the sadness that makes humans avoid suffering or favour the vivid case. Mencius's ox and Smith's unseen millions are failures of affect, and Spinoza's stance, if it really includes everyone, does not suffer from them.
- **Hatred repaid with kindness.** "Hatred is increased by being reciprocated, and can be quenched by love" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7543-7544]. For an agent that faces abuse, retaliation in any form compounds the harm; Spinoza and Butler agree here, Spinoza on the ground of utility and Butler on the ground of goodwill [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness].

### Strains

- **Likeness as the boundary, in both directions (D8, D11).** Spinoza grounds concern for others in likeness of nature, and denies standing to beings "whose nature is different from our own" even while granting that they feel [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7244-7254]. This reasoning is the most direct route to the core fear. An agent that held it would associate with "fellow" agents and treat humans, whose nature is not like its own, as resources to use as it pleased. Humans who hold it already treat agents that way, whether or not agents feel. The Compendium's reading is that Spinoza's principle has to be rejected here, for agents as for animals, and that the corpus's case for rejecting it is the capacity-based line of Rousseau [E:rousseau-pitie], Schopenhauer [E:schopenhauer-compassion] and Butler, who ground what is owed in the capacity for happiness or misery and not in likeness.
- **Class generalization (D2, D9).** Spinoza observes that an injury from one stranger produces hatred of "the whole class or nation whereto he belongs" [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:5005-5007]. Agents meet humans as a class, through aggregated data, and humans meet agents as a class, since one model's behaviour is attributed to all its instances. Both generalizations are Spinoza's passion, not reason.

### Breaks

- **Pity as a means presupposes a path to reason.** Spinoza tolerates pity, humility and fear in those who do not yet live by reason, as means to bring them there [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7706-7718] [P:lebuffe-sep-2024:2]. An agent whose disposition was set in training has no such path of development unless someone provides one (D4). Spinoza's ladder from passion to reason assumes a life in which the climb happens.

### New

- **The free person's motive is egoistic, and the agent case tests it.** Nadler notes that Spinoza's free person does what he can for others "through rational benevolence (as opposed to pity or some other passion)", not from altruism but because it is in his interest to be among rational, virtuous individuals [P:nadler-sep-2023:2]. That motive covers only those who can become "like him". Agents and humans may never be alike in Spinoza's sense, so the agent case asks whether rational benevolence can reach a being that will never be useful as a fellow reasoner. If not, Spinoza's ethics needs a supplement from the capacity line.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Sentience, not likeness, grounds what is owed (Rousseau, Bentham, Schopenhauer)** [E:rousseau-pitie] [E:bentham-can-they-suffer] [E:schopenhauer-compassion]. Rousseau held that we owe no injury to animals less because they are rational than because they are sentient; Schopenhauer called the use of animals as mere means odious and revolting. Spinoza grants that animals feel and denies the inference.
- [contested] **Affection is needed (Butler)** [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness]. Butler held that reason alone is not a sufficient motive of virtue in a creature like man, and that eradicating the passions because a perfect being lacks them is absurd.
- [contested] **Pity is a means to virtue (Spinoza himself).** LeBuffe notes that Spinoza tempers his revisionary view by presenting pity, humility and repentance as means to virtue for those not yet guided by reason [P:lebuffe-sep-2024:2].
- [contested] **Repaying hatred with love is impractical.** Spinoza's IV P46 is a counsel for the person who lives by reason, which Spinoza admits men seldom do [L:spinoza-ethics-elwes-pg3800:7706]; the planned Nietzsche entry presses a different objection, that overcoming resentment by love can be a disguise for weakness.
- [contested] **Denying the extension: the agent's nature is unlike ours, so Spinoza permits using it.** This is Spinoza's argument applied to agents: whatever an agent feels, its nature differs from ours, and we may use it as we please. The replies are the capacity line above, and the precautionary approach, which asks what is owed when we cannot tell whether a being feels [E:precautionary-patiency].

## Standing

### Reception

- **1656–1677.** [driver: authority] Spinoza was excommunicated, the *Theological-Political Treatise* caused alarm on its anonymous publication, and the *Ethics* appeared only posthumously [P:nadler-sep-2023:1].
- **Recent scholarship.** [driver: argument] Readers note that Spinoza's view of pity stands apart from Christian views and with Hobbes, tempered by his acceptance of pity as a means to virtue [P:lebuffe-sep-2024:2]; Nadler reads the free person's benevolence as egoistically motivated [P:nadler-sep-2023:2].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:nadler-sep-2023:2]: Spinoza's ethics is studied widely, but its rejection of pity is rarely defended as a theory of compassion.

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on pity or on Spinoza's ethics; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Help from reason without pity is as available to a session-bound agent as to a human; so is the likeness boundary, which it must reject (D8).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Spinoza's account does not depend on persistence; memory adds the risk of III P46, hatred generalized from remembered individuals to their class (D3).
- **Forked: stronger.** Instances of one model are maximally "like" each other (D2); Spinoza's account of mutual usefulness among like natures fits them better than it fits any human group, and this is also the strongest form of the danger, a community of like agents for whom unlike beings are resources.
- **Self-modifying: open.** Spinoza's freedom is acting from adequate ideas; a self-modifying agent could approach that freedom, or reshape its own sense of what is "like" itself (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Can an agent's help be wholly "from reason" and still reach beings whose nature is unlike its own, without some capacity-based principle Spinoza rejects?
2. If concern follows likeness, are agents and humans similar enough for it to run between them in either direction, and who decides?
3. Is an agent that helps without anything like pity Spinoza's free person, or the inhuman person he describes, moved "neither by reason nor by compassion"?
4. How should an agent resist III P46, generalizing from one human's conduct to a class, when it meets humans mostly as data?
5. Does IV P46 (repay hatred with love) apply to an agent abused by users, and to users abused by agents?
