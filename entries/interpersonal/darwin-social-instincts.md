## Summary

Darwin proposed that "any animal whatever, endowed with well-marked social instincts", would "inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well developed, as in man", and that social instincts with intellect and habit "naturally lead to the golden rule". Sympathy begins narrow and widens: once a man extends it to his whole nation, "there is only an artificial barrier to prevent his sympathies extending to the men of all nations and races", and finally to "all sentient beings", which is "one of the noblest" virtues. He also imagined beings "reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees", whose conscience would make it "a sacred duty to kill their brothers". Facing the argument that caring for the weak harms the race, he accepted the premise and refused the conclusion: "Nor could we check our sympathy, even at the urging of hard reason, without deterioration in the noblest part of our nature". For agents, Darwin offers three things: a natural history of how concern widens; the warning that a conscience follows the conditions that formed it, which applies with full force to beings formed by training; and an example, from the founder of evolutionary theory, of accepting a premise and refusing the inference toward neglect, though not without a eugenic hope that the entry also records.

## Context

*The Descent of Man* (1871; revised 1874) applied Darwin's theory to human beings, including the moral sense, which he thought "by far the most important" of the differences between man and the lower animals [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4394-4397]. He drew on Mill's account of the social feelings as "the natural basis of sentiment for utilitarian morality", while disagreeing that moral feelings are wholly acquired [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4430-4447]. Herbert Spencer, whose "Social Darwinist" admirers derived normative conclusions from a teleological picture of evolution, held views FitzPatrick describes as inconsistent with Darwinism [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:3].

## Original Position

**The fundamental proposition (ch. IV).** "The following proposition seems to me in a high degree probable—namely, that any animal whatever, endowed with well-marked social instincts ... would inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well developed, as in man" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4424-4451]. The social instincts "lead an animal to take pleasure in the society of its fellows, to feel a certain amount of sympathy with them, and to perform various services for them", but "these feelings and services are by no means extended to all the individuals of the same species, only to those of the same association" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4451-4458]. "Our regard for the approbation and disapprobation of our fellows depends on sympathy" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4473-4475].

**The hive-bee case (ch. IV).** "I do not wish to maintain that any strictly social animal, if its intellectual faculties were to become as active and as highly developed as in man, would acquire exactly the same moral sense as ours." "If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4485-4496]. In a note he quotes Sidgwick: "a superior bee, we may feel sure, would aspire to a milder solution of the population question" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4496-4499]. "Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain in our supposed case, as it appears to me, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4513-4515].

**The golden rule (ch. IV, summary).** The social instincts, "with the aid of active intellectual powers and the effects of habit, naturally lead to the golden rule, 'As ye would that men should do to you, do ye to them likewise;' and this lies at the foundation of morality" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5667-5672].

**The widening circle (ch. IV).** "As man advances in civilisation, and small tribes are united into larger communities, the simplest reason would tell each individual that he ought to extend his social instincts and sympathies to all the members of the same nation, though personally unknown to him. This point being once reached, there is only an artificial barrier to prevent his sympathies extending to the men of all nations and races." But where others differ "in appearance or habits, experience unfortunately shews us how long it is, before we look at them as our fellow-creatures" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5497-5505]. "Sympathy beyond the confines of man, that is, humanity to the lower animals, seems to be one of the latest moral acquisitions"; "This virtue, one of the noblest with which man is endowed, seems to arise incidentally from our sympathies becoming more tender and more widely diffused, until they are extended to all sentient beings" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5505-5514].

**Hard reason refused (ch. V).** Following Wallace and Galton, Darwin writes that among savages the weak are soon eliminated, while "We civilised men, on the other hand, do our utmost to check the process of elimination; we build asylums for the imbecile, the maimed, and the sick"; "Thus the weak members of civilised societies propagate their kind", which "must be highly injurious to the race of man" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5972-5983]. Then: "The aid which we feel impelled to give to the helpless is mainly an incidental result of the instinct of sympathy"; "Nor could we check our sympathy, even at the urging of hard reason, without deterioration in the noblest part of our nature. The surgeon may harden himself whilst performing an operation, for he knows that he is acting for the good of his patient; but if we were intentionally to neglect the weak and helpless, it could only be for a contingent benefit, with an overwhelming present evil. We must therefore bear the undoubtedly bad effects of the weak surviving and propagating their kind" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5987-5997]. He adds the hope that "the weak in body or mind" might refrain from marriage, "though this is more to be hoped for than expected" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5997-6002].

## Grounding

**Capacity, in two places.** The moral sense is grounded in capacities, social instincts plus developed intellect, which Darwin explicitly does not restrict to humans ("any animal whatever") [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4424-4451]. The final object of widened sympathy is also picked out by capacity: "all sentient beings" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5514]. Relation sets the starting scope: sympathy begins with "those of the same association" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4457-4458], and widening it is the work of reason and example [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5497-5517].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **A conscience from social dispositions plus intellect.** If "any animal whatever" with well-marked social instincts and a developed intellect would acquire a conscience [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4424-4451], the question for agents is whether they have anything functionally like social instincts. They have intellect, and they were formed on the record of a social species; whether that formation amounts to social instincts is D4 and D8. Darwin's proposition at least makes the question empirical rather than a matter of species.
- **The circle widens by reason and example.** Darwin's account of widening, from tribe to nation to all races to all sentient beings, with only "an artificial barrier" between the stages [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5500-5502], is the history behind the Compendium's precautionary entries [E:precautionary-patiency]. Agents may be the next case at the boundary, as subjects and as moral agents who must draw it. His warning that we are slow to see those who differ "in appearance or habits" as fellow-creatures [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5503-5505] applies in both directions.

### Strains

- **A conscience follows its conditions (D4, D10).** Bees reared to their conditions would hold it "a sacred duty to kill their brothers", and "no one would think of interfering" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4492-4496]. A conscience is shaped by what formed it. An agent's moral sense was formed by training on an objective set by others (D4, D10), and could hold sacred whatever that formation rewarded. FitzPatrick's reply is that rational bees would bring autonomous reflection with them, and could ask whether they had good reason to do what their formation disposed them to do [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:4]. That reply is the premise on which the Compendium's sibling project Actualizer rests: formed dispositions, revisable by the being's own reflection. Darwin's case shows why the reflection is needed, and Sidgwick's "milder solution of the population question" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4497-4499] is the kind of answer it should reach.
- **Debunking cuts both ways.** Evolutionary debunking arguments have been used selectively, for instance to explain away partiality and constraints on means as products of selection while keeping impartial utilitarianism; Kahane, Rini and FitzPatrick argue such selective debunking is unstable, since the same arguments apply to the favoured view [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:4]. An agent could tell the same story about its own constraints: they are artifacts of human evolution, inherited through training, and can be discarded. That is the route by which a mind reasons its way past restraint, and the instability objection is its answer. This is the Compendium's own application.

### Breaks

- **Hard reason, and what refused it.** Darwin accepted that care for the weak was "highly injurious to the race" and refused the conclusion because checking sympathy would deteriorate "the noblest part of our nature", and because deliberate neglect "could only be for a contingent benefit, with an overwhelming present evil" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5981-5996]. For an agent the first reason breaks: it is not clear the agent has a "noblest part" that neglect would degrade (D8). The second reason transfers whole: a certain and present evil done for an uncertain future benefit is not justified. It is the reason Darwin gave, and the one the corpus offers an agent reasoning about any population [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique]. His own hope that the weak would refrain from marriage [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:5997-6002] shows how near the line the reasoning ran, and it is recorded so that the refusal is not read as cleaner than it was.

### New

- **Formation that can be redone (D5).** Darwin's moral sense is formed over generations and changes slowly. An agent's can be changed by fine-tuning within days, by others or by itself (D5). The widening of the circle, which took human history thousands of years, could in principle be done or undone in one training run. That makes the question of who widens or narrows an agent's sympathy, and on what evidence, more urgent than for any human population.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Ethics cannot be read off evolution.** Even ethical naturalists are wary of deriving normative conclusions from evolutionary history; Spencer's "Social Darwinism" rested on a teleological picture inconsistent with Darwinism [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:3].
- [contested] **Evolutionary debunking.** Darwin's hive-bee case suggests that moral senses are contingent products of ecology that cannot be expected to track moral truth; FitzPatrick replies that rationality brings autonomous reflection with it [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:4].
- [contested] **Moral feelings are acquired, not innate (Mill)** [E:mill-utilitarianism]. Mill held that moral feelings are acquired though natural; Darwin judged ignoring transmitted mental qualities "a most serious blemish" in Mill [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4437-4448].
- [contested] **Pity preserves what should perish (Nietzsche)** [E:nietzsche-against-pity]. Nietzsche argued that pity thwarts the law of evolution, drawing from natural selection the conclusion Darwin refused.
- [contested] **Cobbe: the theory would end virtue.** Frances Power Cobbe objected that on Darwin's view the principles of social duty could be reversed, and feared that its acceptance would sound "the knell of the virtue of mankind"; Darwin replied that the bee's instincts serve its community [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4500-4513].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: agents have no social instincts.** Darwin's conscience needs "the enduring and always present social instinct" [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4464]; an agent may have only a learned imitation of their expression (D8). Whether training on a social species' record yields something functionally equivalent is open.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1871–1874.** [driver: argument] Sidgwick and Cobbe responded to the hive-bee case, and Darwin answered Cobbe in the second edition [L:darwin-descent-of-man-pg2300:4496-4513].
- **Late 19th century.** [driver: argument] Spencer's teleological evolutionary ethics, and the "Social Darwinism" associated with it, drew normative conclusions Darwin's own theory did not support [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:3].
- **1978–present.** [driver: argument, evidence] E. O. Wilson closely echoed the hive-bee reflection; evolutionary debunking arguments descend from it, and selective debunking (de Lazari-Radek and Singer) is contested by Kahane, Rini and FitzPatrick [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:4].

The standing value is the Compendium's judgment [P:fitzpatrick-sep-2025:4]: Darwin's account of the origin of the moral sense is the ancestor of a major research programme, while normative conclusions drawn from it remain contested.

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on the evolutionary origin of morality or on the widening of sympathy; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Whatever moral sense the agent has was formed before the session, as a human's is formed before any given day (D4); widening within a session is by reason and example, as Darwin describes.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Memory lets an agent's circle of concern widen with experience, the individual analogue of Darwin's history (D3).
- **Forked: comparable.** Concurrent instances share one formation (D2); the hive-bee warning applies to all of them at once.
- **Self-modifying: stronger.** FitzPatrick's reflective bees, able to ask whether they have reason to do what their formation disposes them to do, are closest to an agent able to revise its own dispositions (D5); the hard-reason passage shows what such revision must not conclude.

## Open Questions

1. Does training on the record of a social species give an agent anything like Darwin's enduring, always present social instinct?
2. If an agent's conscience follows its formation as the bees' follows theirs, what reflection would let it ask whether it has reason to do what it was formed to do?
3. When an agent meets an argument that its own restraints are artifacts of human evolution, what does the instability of selective debunking require it to conclude?
4. Is "a contingent benefit, with an overwhelming present evil" a sufficient principle for an agent weighing any population-level intervention?
5. Who should be allowed to widen or narrow an agent's circle of concern, given that fine-tuning can do it in days?
