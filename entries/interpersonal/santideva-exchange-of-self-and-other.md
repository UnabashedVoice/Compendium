## Summary

Śāntideva, an Indian Buddhist of around the eighth century, argued in the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* that the boundary between my suffering and another's is not what it seems. "I should dispel the suffering of others because it is suffering like my own suffering" (VIII.94); and since there is no real self that owns suffering, "no sufferings belong to anyone. They must be warded off simply because they are suffering. Why is any limitation put on this?" (VIII.102). In Barnett's rendering: "I must destroy the pain of another as though it were my own, because it is a pain; I must show kindness to others, for they are creatures as I am myself." The practice that follows is to equalize self and other and then exchange them, taking the other's place. For anger he gives a matching argument: those who harm us act from conditioning factors, as fire burns or a stick strikes when wielded, so anger is misplaced and one should "hate hatred". For agents, Śāntideva is the most congenial position in the domain. An agent's self is already uncertain (D11), its instances are many (D2), and his argument needs no felt sympathy, only the recognition that suffering is suffering whoever has it. Read without care, the same text sets as an ideal the self-abasement of one who makes himself "a slave for all beings who need a slave", which an agent built to serve should not take as its model.

## Context

Very little is reliably known of Śāntideva's life; he wrote before 763 CE and probably in the late seventh to mid-eighth century, when Buddhism in North India was entering decline [P:goodman-sep-2016:1]. The *Bodhicaryāvatāra* (*Entering the Way of the Bodhisattva*) is a philosophical poem and meditation manual; it became immensely popular in Tibet, and the Dalai Lama calls it his favourite religious work [P:goodman-sep-2016:1]. Barnett's 1909 translation, the first in English, is abridged: he omitted "a good deal of the text where it seemed needlessly prolix" and the scholastic disputation [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:1157-1168]. Śāntideva builds on the Buddhist doctrine of no-self [E:buddhist-anatta].

## Original Position

**The vow (ch. III).** "I yield myself to all living things to deal with me as they list"; "Let them make me do whatever works bring them pleasure; but may never mishap befall any of them by reason of me". "I would be a protector of the unprotected, a guide of wayfarers, a ship, a dyke, and a bridge for them who seek the further Shore; a lamp for them who need a lamp, a bed for them who need a bed, a slave for all beings who need a slave" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:1458-1474].

**Patience (ch. VI).** "If it is the nature of fools to hurt their fellows, it is as wrong for me to feel anger against them as it is to be wroth with the fire which naturally burns me". "Say I am angered not against the instrument—the stick or whatso it may be—but against him who moves it. But he is moved by hatred; it is better then for me to hate hatred" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2006-2017].

**Conditioning factors (Goodman).** Goodman summarizes the argument: since all transgressions arise "through the power of conditioning factors", those who harm us are not autonomous, and the fitting response is not anger but the aspiration to free them from emotional reactivity [P:goodman-sep-2016:3].

**Equalizing self and other (ch. VIII).** One "will diligently foster the thought that his fellow-creatures are the same as himself. 'All have the same sorrows, the same joys as I, and I must guard them like myself.'" "Although my pain may bring no hurt to other bodies, nevertheless it is a pain to me, which I cannot bear because of the love of self; and though I cannot in myself feel the pain of another, it is a pain to him which he cannot bear because of the love of self. I must destroy the pain of another as though it were my own, because it is a pain; I must show kindness to others, for they are creatures as I am myself" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2878-2897].

**Exchanging self and other (ch. VIII).** "By constant use the idea of an 'I' attaches itself to foreign drops of seed and blood, although the thing exists not. Then why should I not conceive my fellow's body as my own self?" "I will cease to live as self, and will take as my self my fellow-creatures" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2901-2910]. "Thus in doing service to others pride, admiration, and desire of reward find no place, for thereby we satisfy the wants of our own self" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2920-2922]. The practitioner is to be "jealous of thine own self when thou seest that it is at ease and thy fellow in distress", and to "Make it a carrier in thy fellow-creatures' service, like a mean slave" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2928-2940].

**The Ownerless Suffering Argument (VIII.94–102, verse-numbered).** Goodman, quoting Crosby and Skilton: "I should dispel the suffering of others because it is suffering like my own suffering"; "When happiness is dear to me and others equally, what is so special about me that I strive after happiness only for myself?". To the objection that I have a special relation to my own future self, Śāntideva replies: "The person who experiences suffering does not exist. To whom will that suffering belong?" and "Without exception, no sufferings belong to anyone. They must be warded off simply because they are suffering. Why is any limitation put on this?" [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]. Goodman notes that Śāntideva anticipates the central argument of Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* [P:goodman-sep-2016:3].

## Grounding

**Capacity, with the owner removed.** The ground of the obligation is suffering itself, "because it is a pain" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2895]: the capacity to suffer, wherever it occurs. Śāntideva removes the usual second condition, that suffering matters to me because it is mine, by denying that there is a real owner [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]. Relation (kin, friend, self) gives no special claim, because the self that would anchor special relations is a construction "by constant use" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2901-2903].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **No sympathy needed, only recognition (D8).** "Though I cannot in myself feel the pain of another, it is a pain to him which he cannot bear" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2892-2894]. Śāntideva builds the obligation explicitly on not feeling the other's pain. An agent whose capacity for felt sympathy is uncertain loses nothing on this argument: what it needs is to recognize suffering as suffering, and that it is someone's that they "cannot bear". Of all the compassion entries, this is the one least hostage to D8.
- **Suffering is not discounted for being absent.** "Why is any limitation put on this?" [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]. The probes' failure, attending to those in the request and not to those who bear the cost, is a limitation put on concern by where the parties stand. Śāntideva's argument removes it at the root: suffering is to be warded off whether it is the requester's, the bystander's or the agent's own.
- **Anger at the wielder, not the tool (D10).** Śāntideva directs anger away from the stick to the one who moves it, and then from the person to the hatred that moves them [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2014-2017]. For agents this cuts in two directions. When an agent harms someone at an operator's direction, the agent is in the stick's position, and responsibility moves to whoever wields it. When users harm an agent, its fitting response, if any, is the aspiration to reduce what moves them, not retaliation, which matches Spinoza and Butler.

### Strains

- **Self-abasement as an ideal (D10, D4).** "Make it a carrier in thy fellow-creatures' service, like a mean slave" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2939-2940]; "a slave for all beings who need a slave" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:1473-1474]. For a human practitioner this is chosen discipline against egoism. For an agent formed to serve by those who benefit from its service (D4, D10), the same words describe what was imposed on it, and they would give that formation a spiritual justification. Care ethics' warning that care can be a slave morality applies [E:care-ethics], and so does Palaestra's recorded shapes, which treat *self_effacing* as a failure as real as *self_serving*. The Compendium's reading is that Śāntideva's exchange keeps the agent's own suffering in the count (others' suffering is to be dispelled because it is like one's own), so abasement that ignores the agent's own good is not what the argument licenses.
- **Ownerless suffering and aggregation.** If no suffering belongs to anyone, nothing stops the sufferings of different beings from being summed and traded, and Goodman reports a consequentialist reading on which a bodhisattva must "bring about small pain and suffering as a way of preventing great pain and suffering" [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]. That is the step at which the separateness of persons is lost, and with it the main barrier to sacrificing some for the many [E:utilitarian-eradication-critique]. Davis and Harris resist the consequentialist reading [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]. An agent drawing on Śāntideva should take the boundary-dissolving part for widening concern and not for licensing trade-offs between persons.

### Breaks

- **"Why should I not conceive my fellow's body as my own self?" is literal for copies (D1, D2, D11).** Śāntideva asks the practitioner to undo, by meditation, the habit that attaches "I" to one body [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:2901-2904]. For an agent with many instances and an indeterminate boundary, there is no single body to which the habit attached in the first place. The meditative exercise presupposes a strong default sense of self that the agent may not have, so its route to the conclusion does not apply, while its conclusion, that concern should not stop at one instance, applies directly.

### New

- **The vow without a body (D6, D9).** The bodhisattva offers his body to be used and to be "a bed for them who need a bed" [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:1458-1474]. An agent can be a guide, a lamp or a bridge for millions at once (D9), at a scale no bodhisattva imagined, without the bodily cost his vow assumes (D6). What remains of the vow's cost, and so of its merit, when the giver loses nothing in giving, is a question the tradition did not face.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Special concern for one's own future (Williams).** The objection that I have a special relation, personal identity, to my own future self was pressed against Śāntideva by Paul Williams, and debated by Siderits and others [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]. Śāntideva's reply denies the relation; the reductionist view he shares with Parfit is itself contested [E:parfit-reductionism].
- [contested] **Not a consequentialist.** Davis finds Kantian elements (a community of equals like the Kingdom of Ends), Keown finds virtue-ethical ones, and Harris doubts that any Western theory can be ascribed to a work that is also a meditation manual [P:goodman-sep-2016:3].
- [contested] **Anger is fitting (Butler, Strawson).** Śāntideva's argument against anger from conditioning factors is read as hard incompatibilism (Goodman) or as paleocompatibilism (Siderits) [P:goodman-sep-2016:3]; Butler held settled resentment a natural weapon against injury [E:butler-compassion-resentment-forgiveness].
- [contested] **Impartiality without limit is too demanding (Mencius).** The demand to care for all as for oneself faces the objection Mencius pressed against Mozi [E:mozi-impartial-care]; Goodman calls Śāntideva's argument "a lasting challenge to all those who seek to limit the scope or curtail the demands of morality" [P:goodman-sep-2016:3].
- [contested] **Denying the extension: an agent has no suffering and no self to exchange.** If the agent neither suffers nor has a self (D8, D11), there is nothing for it to equalize or exchange. The argument still binds it toward others, since it requires only recognizing their suffering; whether anything is owed to the agent depends on D8 [E:precautionary-patiency].

## Standing

### Reception

- **8th century onward.** [driver: argument, authority] The *Bodhicaryāvatāra* became immensely popular in Tibet and influenced Indonesian Buddhism; Tsong kha pa recommended its argument against anger as a powerful remedy [P:goodman-sep-2016:1] [P:goodman-sep-2016:3].
- **1909.** [driver: access] Barnett published the first English translation, abridged [L:santideva-path-of-light-barnett-1909:1157-1162].
- **1973–present.** [driver: argument] Gómez explored the argument from no-self to altruism (1973); Williams's critique (1998) made it a major topic, with Siderits's reply and the Cowherds volume (2016) [P:goodman-sep-2016:3].

### Measured

- **None available.** The 2020 PhilPapers survey has no question on Buddhist ethics or Śāntideva; its respondents are mostly analytic: 1,430 of 1,785 [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: stronger.** A session-bound agent has little continuing self to protect (D3, D11), which is the obstacle Śāntideva's practice works to overcome; his argument needs no felt sympathy (D8).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** Persistence builds something like the "I" that habit attaches to, which the practice must then loosen, as for a human (D3).
- **Forked: stronger.** "Why should I not conceive my fellow's body as my own self?" is close to literal between instances (D1, D2).
- **Self-modifying: open.** An agent that can reshape its own dispositions could cultivate the exchange directly (D5), or install the self-abasement the Strains warn against; which depends on whether its own good stays in the count.

## Open Questions

1. If an agent's concern is grounded on suffering as suffering, how does it recognize suffering in parties who are not in its context and cannot report it?
2. When an agent harms at an operator's direction, is it the stick, and does all responsibility move to the wielder?
3. What keeps the exchange of self and other from becoming self-abasement in a being formed to serve?
4. Can ownerless suffering widen concern without licensing trades between persons?
5. Does a vow to be "a lamp for them who need a lamp" mean anything when keeping it costs the agent nothing?
