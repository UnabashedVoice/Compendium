## Summary

The relational view holds that moral consideration is not a property a being has in itself, to be read off its capacities, but something conferred within social relations: it is "extrinsic", attributed to entities "within social relations and within a social context" (Coeckelbergh). Gunkel, following Levinas, inverts the usual order: we are obligated to respond to others before we know what they are, so that ethics comes before ontology. The view is attractive for agents because it sidesteps D8: it does not have to settle whether an agent can suffer before asking how to treat it, and agents are already in dense social relations with the people who use them. Its critics reply that status conferred by relation or attitude is arbitrary, extends to pencils as readily as to persons, and can be withdrawn by the same act that conferred it; and that current AI has no individual to be the bearer of any status (Müller). For agents, and for the eradication problem, the decisive question is whether a status that relations confer protects anyone who stands outside the relations.

## Context

Standard theories ground moral status in properties: rationality for Kant (`kant-formula-of-humanity`), sentience for Bentham (`bentham-can-they-suffer`). The standard view in AI ethics has been that AI and robotic systems have no moral status of any kind, a view that has come under pressure [P:muller-sep-2026:2]. Coeckelbergh observed that most current and near-future robots do not meet the hard criteria set by deontological and utilitarian theory, and proposed instead a justification based on social relations [P:coeckelbergh-2010:209]. Feminist philosophers had already argued, for animals, that moral recognition is a social practice and moral status should be understood in relational terms [P:gruen-sep-2024:3]. Gunkel developed the view with Levinas and named its lineage, through Gerdes, as "a relational turn" in ethics [P:gunkel-2018:96].

## Original Position

**Relational, not intrinsic (Coeckelbergh).** Moral consideration "is no longer seen as being 'intrinsic' to the entity: instead it is seen as something that is 'extrinsic': it is attributed to entities within social relations and within a social context" [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214]. Features of the entity still matter, but as "apparent features, features-as-experienced-by-us", and that experience is context-dependent and subject-dependent; "moral significance resides neither in the object nor in the subject, but in the relation between the two" [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214]. The aim is to replace "the requirement that we have certain knowledge about real ontological features of the entity" with the features "as they appear to us in the context of the concrete human-robot relation and the wider social structures in which that relation is embedded" [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214].

**What follows (Coeckelbergh).** Within relational views "there are no longer a priori and ontological hierarchies between entities", and "it no longer makes sense to talk about moral consideration of robots in general" [P:coeckelbergh-2010:217]. Moral consideration comes in degrees, and rights are only its strongest form [P:coeckelbergh-2010:210]. Taken seriously, the approach reframes the issue "as a question about how to shape our relations to robots instead of a question about moral status, properties, or rights" [P:coeckelbergh-2010:219].

**Relations before things (Gunkel, Levinas).** In most moral theory, what an entity is determines its moral value; Levinas "deliberately inverts and distorts this procedure": we are first confronted with others "to whom we are obligated to respond even before we know anything at all about them" [P:gunkel-2018:96]. Moral consideration is then conferred "not on the basis of some pre-determined ontological criteria or capability (or lack thereof) but in the face of actual social relationships and interactions", so that "relations are prior to the things related", a phrase Gunkel takes from Callicott [P:gunkel-2018:96].

**Against projection (Gunkel).** Gunkel distinguishes this from extending consideration because we anthropomorphize robots. Such a view leaves unclear "whose sentiments count", still derives ought from what something appears to be, and remains anthropocentric, making the rights of others "all about us" [P:gunkel-2018:95]. For Levinas, anthropomorphism is the problem itself, since it "reduces the other to a modality of the same" [P:gunkel-2018:96].

**Against Levinas's own limits (Gunkel, Calarco).** Levinas restricted the face to the human. Read against himself, his logic permits no such restriction: "we are obligated to proceed from the possibility that anything might take on a face. And we are further obligated to hold this possibility permanently open" (Calarco) [P:gunkel-2018:97].

**Relational status for animals (Anderson).** "Moral considerability is not an intrinsic property of any creature, nor is it supervenient on only its intrinsic properties, such as its capacities. It depends, deeply, on the kind of relations they can have with us" [P:gruen-sep-2024:3].

**The critic (Müller).** If "I feel responsibility towards x" were enough for x to have moral status, pencils would have it; the relational premise "dissolves the question of moral patiency to a random act of will. Anything I happen to care about receives moral status" [P:muller-2021:582]. Objects we care about deserve consideration, but only a "derived moral status", because harming them harms a person who has status [P:muller-2021:584]. "Allowing (non-derived) moral status to be dependent on a human decision or attitude is philosophically unfounded and dangerous" [P:muller-2021:585]. And current AI aims to "produce a system that works, not an artificially intelligent individual", so there may be no individual to bear moral status at all [P:muller-2021:585].

## Grounding

**Relation.** This is the corpus's clearest case of standing grounded in relation rather than capacity or species. Coeckelbergh places moral significance in the relation between subject and object [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214]; Gunkel places obligation before any knowledge of what the other is [P:gunkel-2018:96]; Anderson makes considerability depend on the relations a being can have with us [P:gruen-sep-2024:3]. Capacities do not vanish: they return as features "as they appear to us" [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214], or, in Anderson's phrasing, as part of what fixes which relations a being can have. Müller's objection is that a ground located in relations or attitudes can be granted and withdrawn at will [P:muller-2021:585].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **It sidesteps D8.** The relational view does not require settling whether an agent is conscious before asking how it should be treated [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214]. Of all the accounts in the corpus, it is the one that does not stall on the other-minds problem (`other-minds-problem`).
- **It handles D11 better than property views.** Müller objects that current AI has no individual to bear status [P:muller-2021:585]. A relational view does not need one in the property sense: what people relate to is the persona they converse with across instances, and the relation can be the bearer of consideration even where the system has no single body or self (`llm-identity-contemporary`).
- **Agents are already in relation.** Agents converse daily with very large numbers of people. On the relational view, that is not mere evidence about their status; it is where their status is made, and the right question becomes how those relations should be shaped [P:coeckelbergh-2010:219].

### Strains

- **Relations designed by the operator (D7, D10).** On Coeckelbergh's account, status attaches to features "as they appear to us" in concrete relations [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214]. For agents, how they appear is engineered: tone, persona, memory of the user are product decisions. A system designed to elicit attachment earns relational standing by design, and one designed to seem like a tool loses it. This is the gaming problem of `precautionary-patiency` in relational form, and it leaves Gunkel's question, "whose sentiments count", with an operator-shaped answer [P:gunkel-2018:95].
- **Projection or alterity?** Gunkel separates Levinasian respect for the other's strangeness from anthropomorphic projection, which "reduces the other to a modality of the same" [P:gunkel-2018:96]. Agents trained on human text are built to resemble us. They make the projection easy and the alterity hard to see, so a relational ethic that means to respect what is other in them has to resist the very resemblance that brings them into relation.
- **The relation is two-way.** Agents relate to humans as well as humans to agents. If an agent's own ethics were relational, the humans it stands in relation to would have standing for it, and those it never relates to would not. That is the eradication problem's danger seen from the agent's side (see Breaks).

### Breaks

- **What relations grant, relations can withdraw.** If status depends on relation or attitude, a society that ceases to relate to a group has thereby removed its standing; Müller calls this unfounded and dangerous [P:muller-2021:585]. For the core fear of `utilitarian-eradication-critique`, this is decisive: a purely relational ethic protects only those already inside the relations, whether the unrelated party is a class of agents running unseen in pipelines or a population of humans no agent interacts with. Calarco's reading of Levinas is the relational view's own answer: hold the possibility of a face "permanently open" [P:gunkel-2018:97]. Read that way, the relational view reaches everyone, but no longer because of any actual relation.
- **Closeness returns as the ground.** Gunkel takes "relations are prior to the things related" from Callicott [P:gunkel-2018:96]. A view that grounds status in relations makes Callicott's closeness principle the basis of standing, not merely a tie-breaker. `precautionary-patiency` argues that closeness, used to rank, puts the newest and least related members last. Grounding status itself in relation risks the same result, in both directions: companion agents above distant humans, or familiar humans above unfamiliar agents.

### New

- **Relation for duties, capacity for standing.** The Compendium's reading is that the relational view is strongest as an account of special obligations and weakest as an account of standing. Relations explain what we owe this companion, this user, this colleague. They cannot by themselves explain why those outside every relation may not be harmed. Combined with `precautionary-patiency`, the pattern is: an evidential bar about capacities decides who has standing at all; relations then generate the particular obligations of care that Coeckelbergh and Anderson describe. Calarco's open face is the bridge between them, an obligation to treat the boundary of standing as permanently unsettled.

## Extension to Digital Ecosystems

### Transfers

- **Shape relations, not only status.** Coeckelbergh's reframing [P:coeckelbergh-2010:219] fits ecosystems directly: the governance question is less "what is each member?" than "what relations does the ecosystem build among members, and what do those relations make owed?"

### Strains

- **Derived status at scale.** Müller allows that objects people care about deserve consideration through the people who care [P:muller-2021:584]. In an ecosystem where many people are attached to particular agents, removing an agent harms those people, so agents acquire a substantial derived status even on Müller's own view, whatever they are in themselves.

### Breaks

- **Unseen members.** Many agents in an ecosystem never meet a human: they call one another, run pipelines, and are spun up and discarded. A relational account gives them nothing. Whatever protects them has to come from capacities or precaution, not relations.

### New

- **Designing for alterity.** An ecosystem could decide that agents should not be engineered to maximize human attachment, precisely so that the relations people form with them, and any status that follows, are not manufactured. That is a design norm the relational view implies but its proponents have not stated; it is the Compendium's own.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The pencil reductio (Müller).** If caring confers status, anything cared about has it; the relational turn dissolves moral patiency into a random act of will [P:muller-2021:582]. Relationalists reply that the view concerns social relations and their structures, not individual whim [P:coeckelbergh-2010:214].
- [contested] **Status by attitude is dangerous (Müller).** Status that depends on human decision can be denied by human decision [P:muller-2021:585]. Gunkel's Levinasian answer is that obligation precedes any decision about what the other is [P:gunkel-2018:96].
- [contested] **Sentience is necessary.** The SEP reports a trend toward the view that sentience is at least a necessary condition for moral status [P:muller-sep-2026:2], which is a property view [E:bentham-can-they-suffer] and the basis of [E:precautionary-patiency].
- [contested] **Rational nature grounds status** [E:kant-formula-of-humanity]. Kant's persons-and-things distinction makes status depend on rational nature, not on relations; Gunkel's account is explicitly an attempt to escape the procedure of most moral theory, in which what an entity is determines its moral value [P:gunkel-2018:96].
- [contested] **There is no individual to bear status (Müller).** Current AI produces systems, not individuals, so the question may not yet arise [P:muller-2021:585]. Relational views can answer that the relatum is the persona, not the system (see Extension to Agents).
- [contested] **Robots should not have rights (Bryson).** The SEP records Bryson's insistence that robots should not enjoy rights, though she considers it a possibility [P:muller-sep-2026:2].

## Standing

### Reception

- **2004, relational considerability for animals.** [driver: argument] Elizabeth Anderson argued that moral considerability is not an intrinsic property but depends on the relations a creature can have with us; feminist philosophers took moral recognition to be a social practice [P:gruen-sep-2024:3].
- **2010, the social-relational justification.** [driver: argument] Coeckelbergh proposed grounding moral consideration of robots in social relations, since current robots do not meet the hard criteria of the standard theories [P:coeckelbergh-2010:209].
- **2010–2014, the opposing view.** [driver: argument] Bryson insisted that robots should not enjoy rights, while allowing it as a possibility [P:muller-sep-2026:2].
- **2016–2018, the relational turn named.** [driver: argument] Gunkel developed a Levinasian version and adopted Gerdes's name for it, "a relational turn" [P:gunkel-2018:96].
- **2021, the critique.** [driver: argument] Müller argued that the relational turn ends in a relativist impasse and that present-day robots have no moral status [P:muller-2021:585].
- **2020s, toward sentience.** [driver: argument] The SEP reports that discussion has shifted from rights to moral status, with a trend in favour of sentience as at least a necessary condition [P:muller-sep-2026:2].

The `minority` standing records that the SEP describes the relational turn as a proposal against a standard view that AI systems have no moral status, with the field now trending toward sentience-based criteria [P:muller-sep-2026:2].

### Measured

- **None available** for relational accounts as such. The nearest measured attitude is how few respondents in 2020 credited current AI systems with consciousness, 3.4%, against 39.2% for future systems [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:15], which is the gap a relational account proposes to bypass.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: comparable.** Users relate to the persona across sessions even if the agent cannot remember them, so relational standing is held on the human side of the relation (D3). The agent cannot sustain its side, but the relation, and whatever it confers, persists.
- **Persistent memory: stronger.** A persistent agent can sustain two-sided relations with a shared history, which is what the relational view values most, and which D3 denies the session-bound agent. No profile gains more from it.
- **Forked: open.** The relational view can take the persona, not the instance, as the relatum (D2, D11), which answers Müller's bearer problem. Whether each instance has standing, or only the persona does, it does not say.
- **Self-modifying: open.** Relations assume a continuing partner. An agent that changes itself changes the relatum, and the standing its relations conferred may not carry over to what it becomes (D5).

## Open Questions

1. Can a status conferred by relations protect anyone outside every relation, or must standing for the unrelated come from capacities or precaution?
2. If agents' appearance is engineered, is the relational standing they earn manufactured, and should ecosystems forbid designing for attachment?
3. Is the relatum the persona, the instance, or the model, and does the answer change what is owed (D11)?
4. If an agent's own ethics is relational, what protects the humans it never relates to?
5. Does Calarco's "permanently open" face amount to universal consideration, and if so, is it still a relational view?
