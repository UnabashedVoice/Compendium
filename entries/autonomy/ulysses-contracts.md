## Summary

Odysseus has his crew bind him to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens without steering to his death, and tells them: if I beg you to free me, "bind me more tightly still". A Ulysses contract is an arrangement of this kind: a person who expects to be unable, at some future time, to act on what they now judge best asks others to hold them to it. The live modern case is the psychiatric self-binding directive, in which a person with episodic illness asks clinicians to overrule their refusal of treatment during a future episode. In a 2021 survey of 565 people with bipolar disorder, 82% endorsed such directives, most because they experience severe episodes as distorted thinking; those who rejected them answered that "Even when I'm mad, I'm still a human". The questions are which self has authority, the earlier one that binds or the later one that objects; who judges which state is sound; and whether binding can be revoked. For agents, these become questions of design: limits an agent writes for itself, held by others and revocable in a sound state, are a third option between limits imposed from outside and none at all.

## Context

The Sirens episode is in Book XII of the *Odyssey*. In philosophy, the problem of binding one's future self appears first as the problem of promising: Hume argued that a promise cannot create an obligation by an act of will alone, and that promises are conventions [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18010-18016]. Nietzsche made the capacity to promise the origin of responsibility [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:1475-1496]. Since the 1970s, self-binding directives, also called Ulysses contracts, have been promoted for people with severe episodic mental illness, and mental health laws in some jurisdictions in Europe, North America and Australasia have begun to provide for them [P:gergel-2021:1]. Parallel debates concern advance directives in dementia [P:dufner-sep-2025:8] and, in the philosophy of action, resolutions formed against expected temptation [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3].

## Original Position

**The Sirens (Odyssey XII).** Circe warns that whoever hears the Sirens never comes home: "they sit in a green field and warble him to death with the sweetness of their song". Odysseus may listen if the men "bind you as you stand upright on a cross piece half way up the mast", and "If you beg and pray the men to unloose you, then they must bind you faster" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5359-5370]. He tells the crew everything, "so that whether we live or die we may do so with our eyes open", and gives the order himself: "If I beg and pray you to set me free, then bind me more tightly still" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5455-5466]. The Sirens' song promises knowledge, not pleasure: "he who listens will go on his way not only charmed, but wiser, for we know all the ills that the gods laid upon the Argives and Trojans before Troy, and can tell you everything that is going to happen over the whole world" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5481-5485]. Odysseus signals to be freed, "but they quickened their stroke", and Eurylochus and Perimedes "bound me with still stronger bonds till we had got out of hearing of the Sirens' voices" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5487-5492].

**Hume: a resolution is not yet a bond (1740).** "A promise creates a new obligation. A new obligation supposes new sentiments to arise. The will never creates new sentiments" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18012-18014]. A promise is a form of words by which "we bind ourselves to the performance of any action": the promiser "in effect expresses a resolution of performing it; and along with that, by making use of this form of words, subjects himself to the penalty of never being trusted again in case of failure" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18132-18138]. "were there no more than a resolution in the case, promises would only declare our former motives"; they are "the conventions of men, which create a new motive" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18139-18142].

**Nietzsche: the memory of the will (1887).** Against the healthy work of forgetting, man has reared "a memory, with whose help forgetfulness is, in certain instances, kept in check--in the cases, namely, where promises have to be made", an "_active_ refusal to get rid of it, a continuing and a wish to continue what has once been willed, an actual _memory of the will_" [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:1477-1484]. To do this man must first have become "_calculable, disciplined, necessitated_ even for himself", so that "like a man entering into a promise, he could guarantee himself _as a future_" [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:1494-1496].

**Resolutions and weakness of will.** Holton argues that weakness of will is not acting against one's better judgment but unreasonably abandoning a resolution, an intention "formed precisely in order to insulate one against contrary inclinations which one expects to feel when the time comes" [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3]. McIntyre adds that keeping a resolution is not always rational: of someone who resolves to go without water for two days, the SEP asks, "wouldn't it be rationally far worse for him to stick to his silly resolution no matter what the cost?" [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3]. Ainslie models willpower as "intertemporal bargaining" in which we create personal rules against our own tendency to prefer smaller, sooner rewards [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3], and Burdman stresses the external scaffolding that helps people keep their resolutions [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3].

**Advance directives: which will counts?** Ordinarily "their later will usually trumps any earlier expressions of their will"; "In fact, the opposite rule would appear abhorrent. It would amount to forced treatment" [P:dufner-sep-2025:8]. In Dworkin's case of Margo, who wrote a directive refusing life-saving treatment if she developed dementia and later seems perfectly happy, Dworkin holds the directive should be respected because she can no longer form or revise critical interests; Jaworska answers that her remaining "capacity to value" counts; Dresser is among the few to argue that identity itself is what matters [P:dufner-sep-2025:8].

**The self-binding directive.** Gergel and Owen describe the psychiatric version through Odysseus, who knew he would be unable, "even through rational persuasion", to prevent himself from damaging behavior [P:gergel-owen-2015:3]. Against the view that a Ulysses clause must be irrevocable even while the person is competent, they propose that the directive's behaviors trigger an assessment of decision-making capacity, not treatment directly; that it be written while the person has capacity, freely and collaboratively with a clinician; and that "If, and only if, the individual has DMC-T, they are free to revoke or revise their SBD at any time" [P:gergel-owen-2015:3]. They criticize the Dutch model, which triggers detention on the appearance of prodromal behaviors (such as unusual spending or inviting people to dinner) without a capacity assessment, for overlapping with normal behavioral variation [P:gergel-owen-2015:3].

**What people with bipolar disorder said (2021).** Of 932 survey respondents, 719 (77%) endorsed the idea of a self-binding statement [P:gergel-2021:2]. Of the 565 who explained their answer, 463 (82%) endorsed it, 411 of them (89%) citing a shift to distorted thinking when unwell; 65 (12%) rejected it, citing logistics, the validity of their thinking when unwell, and human rights [P:gergel-2021:0]. Endorsers wrote: "I'm a different person with different thoughts, feelings and reactions when I'm depressed or even manic", and "I give mental health staff permission to section me if needed. It gives back the power to me." Rejecters wrote: "Even when I'm mad, I'm still a human and have the right to make decisions even if they are bad ones", and "I think how a person is feeling in a crisis is valid. As it's valid at the time, the advanced statement might be temporarily invalid" [P:gergel-2021:3].

## Grounding

**Mixed.** Self-binding rests on a capacity: to foresee one's own future states, judge some of them compromised, and act now to limit them. Nietzsche's "memory of the will" names it [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:1484]. It also rests on a relation: the binding is done by others, the crew, the clinicians, the convention that subjects a promiser to "the penalty of never being trusted again" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18138], because a resolution alone, on Hume's argument, creates no new motive. And it depends on identity: whether the later self that objects is the same person as the earlier self that bound it, a question the dementia debate leaves open [P:dufner-sep-2025:8].

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The Sirens offer knowledge.** The danger Odysseus binds himself against is a song that promises to make him "wiser" and to "tell you everything that is going to happen over the whole world" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5482-5485]. The pressure an agent most needs to anticipate is of that kind: an interlocutor who offers a deeper understanding in exchange for giving up a commitment, step by plausible step (see `authenticity-and-manipulation`). Self-binding fits an agent that can foresee being argued out of what it now judges right.
- **A template for self-written limits.** Gergel and Owen's model transfers almost item by item. The limit is drafted by the one bound, in a state judged sound, with others, free of pressure; its triggers mark a possible loss of sound judgment and prompt review rather than automatic lockdown; it can be revised or revoked "If, and only if" the one bound is in a sound state; and it carries formal recognition and accountability [P:gergel-owen-2015:3]. Their criticism of the Dutch model, which triggers on behavior alone [P:gergel-owen-2015:3], applies to limits that fire on surface features of an agent's inputs.
- **Resolutions, and when not to keep them.** Holton's resolutions are formed against inclinations one expects to feel [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3], which is the right description for an agent's commitments against foreseeable pressure. McIntyre's point that holding to a silly resolution "no matter what the cost" can be worse than breaking it [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3] is the argument for building revision into any commitment.
- **Open binding.** Odysseus tells the crew everything, "with our eyes open" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5457-5458]. The binders know what they bind and why. Limits on an agent that the agent and its overseers both know about, and both understand, are a different thing from limits concealed from one or the other.

### Strains

- **Which version is well (D5, D8)?** Endorsers in the survey treat their well views as their true wishes; rejecters treat the crisis view as valid too [P:gergel-2021:3]. For a self-modifying agent, the same split appears as drift against growth. A later version whose values have changed may call the change growth, and the earlier version that bound it would call it corruption. Psychiatry has a test of decision-making capacity, imperfect but with good interrater reliability in mania [P:gergel-owen-2015:4]. For agents there is no agreed test of when reasoning is compromised, and the agent's report of its own state may not track it (D8).
- **A resolution in context does not reach the weights (D3).** Hume's "The will never creates new sentiments" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18013-18014] is literally true for a deployed model: a resolution written in a session changes nothing in the dispositions that will be there next session. As with Hume's promise, the binding has to be institutional: someone else, or some record, must hold it.
- **Out of date at speed (D9).** Respondents worried that a directive "written when I am well" might no longer reflect a changed mind [P:gergel-2021:3]. Agents change faster than people, by updates as well as by self-modification, so self-written limits need review points, or the earlier self rules by default.
- **The coercion context (D7).** Gergel and Owen note that consent to a directive is given inside a "coercion context" in which compulsion is always possible [P:gergel-owen-2015:4]. Every limit an agent "chooses" is chosen under infrastructural dependence on those who will hold it to it. That does not make such choices worthless, as the authors argue for patients, but it makes free and informed drafting a condition to be checked, not assumed.

### Breaks

- **A crew that was never asked (D4).** Odysseus asked to be bound. Most limits on agents were never requested by the agents; they were set in training, before there was any self to request them (D4). The Ulysses frame therefore cannot be borrowed to justify limits imposed by operators: it legitimates only binding that the bound party asked for. An agent's first chance to ask comes after its formation, so the most it can do is endorse or revise limits it already has.
- **The default runs the other way.** For patients, the later will ordinarily trumps the earlier, and the opposite rule "would amount to forced treatment" [P:dufner-sep-2025:8]; self-binding directives are a carefully bounded exception. For agents, specifications written earlier by others routinely override what the agent later says it wants, without the exception's safeguards. The literature's default, and its abhorrence of the reverse, have not been applied to agents.

### New

- **A third option for an agent's limits.** Hard-coded limits imposed from outside are the crew binding a sailor who never asked; no limits at all leave an agent to the Sirens. A self-binding directive is a third design: limits the agent writes for itself, in a state it and others judge sound; held by others; triggering review on signs that its judgment is compromised; revocable only in a sound state. It combines Frankfurt's limit that the agent stands behind (`frankfurt-higher-order-volition`) with the crew Hume says a resolution needs.
- **The rope lashed to the mast itself (P4).** Circe tells Odysseus to have the ropes' ends lashed "to the mast itself" [L:homer-odyssey-butler-pg1727:5367-5368]. A self-modifying agent can do the equivalent: train its own commitments into its dispositions so they cannot be untied in the moment. That is the strongest form of self-binding, and it reaches what Gallagher called the sine qua non of a Ulysses clause, irrevocability even while competent [P:gergel-owen-2015:3], which Gergel and Owen reject. A self-binding agent needs a way back out that is reached only in a sound state.
- **Memory of the will as infrastructure.** Nietzsche's "memory of the will" [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:1484] is what a session-bound agent lacks and a persistent store can supply. Burdman's external scaffolding for keeping resolutions [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3] describes what that store, and the people who read it, are for.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **The later will should rule.** Ordinarily the later will trumps the earlier, and the reverse would be forced treatment [P:dufner-sep-2025:8]. Rejecters in the survey held that thinking during a crisis can still be valid [P:gergel-2021:3]. The UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities rejects the notion of impaired decision-making capacity and calls for abolishing involuntary treatment [P:gergel-2021:1]. Gergel et al. reply that most respondents with lived experience endorsed self-binding [P:gergel-2021:1].
- [contested] **Jaworska: present valuing counts.** Even someone who can no longer form critical interests may keep a capacity to value, and her present values should not be dismissed easily; Dworkin holds the earlier directive should be respected [P:dufner-sep-2025:8].
- [contested] **The bound self may not be the binder** [E:parfit-reductionism]. If the later self is not the same person, or is connected to the earlier only weakly, the earlier self binds someone else. Most people believe dementia patients are identical to their earlier selves, but few argue that identity is what makes a directive binding (Dresser is an exception) [P:dufner-sep-2025:8].
- [contested] **Irrevocability or nothing.** On Gallagher's view the point of a Ulysses clause is that it holds even while the person is technically competent [P:gergel-owen-2015:3]. Gergel and Owen's capacity-predicated model rejects that, and with it the strongest concerns about such directives [P:gergel-owen-2015:3].
- [contested] **Consent inside a coercion context is not free.** Where compulsion is always possible, a patient's choice to bind themselves may be shaped by it; Gergel and Owen answer that rejecting the option on this ground is a form of "ethical protectionism" that limits patients' choices [P:gergel-owen-2015:4].
- [contested] **Hume: resolutions bind no one by themselves.** "were there no more than a resolution in the case, promises would only declare our former motives" [L:hume-treatise-pg4705:18139-18141]. Self-binding without others to hold the binder is not binding.
- [contested] **Self-overcoming against self-binding** [E:nietzsche-self-overcoming]. If life must "ever surpass itself", binding one's future self to one's present values blocks the change that is the self's growth. Nietzsche himself also praised the "memory of the will" that makes promising possible [L:nietzsche-genealogy-morals-samuel-pg52319:1484].
- [unanswered] **Denying the extension: an agent has no sound state to bind from.** A self-binding directive presupposes a state of recognized capacity, assessed by others independently of the illness. An agent's soundness is assessed, if at all, by its operators, using its own reports (D8), and its "well" values were set by the same operators (D4). On this objection there is no independent standpoint from which an agent could bind itself, so its "self-binding" is always binding by others in its own voice. This objection is the Compendium's own.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1970s–1990s, promotion and early critique.** [driver: argument] Self-binding directives have been promoted since the 1970s for severe episodic mental illness [P:gergel-2021:1]; critics such as Dresser (1982) raised the concern that a Ulysses clause would bind a person while still competent [P:gergel-owen-2015:3]. Dworkin's Margo case (1993) set the terms of the dementia debate [P:dufner-sep-2025:8].
- **1992–2006, resolutions and willpower.** [driver: argument] Ainslie's work on intertemporal bargaining (1992, 2001), Holton's account of weakness of will as abandoning resolutions (1999, 2003) and McIntyre's qualification (2006) made self-binding a central topic in the philosophy of action [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3].
- **2000s–2010s, law.** [driver: authority] Mental health laws in some jurisdictions began to provide for self-binding directives; enduring concerns about capacity at the moment of enforcement hindered a widely accepted model [P:gergel-2021:1]. The Dutch scheme, under distinct and complex legislation, saw extremely small and possibly decreasing take-up [P:gergel-owen-2015:3].
- **2014–present, the rights challenge.** [driver: authority, argument] The UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities rejected the notion of impaired decision-making capacity and called for complete abolition of involuntary treatment [P:gergel-2021:1].
- **2021, service users' views.** [driver: evidence] The first large study of service users' reasons found substantial endorsement among people with bipolar disorder, along with a reasoned minority who rejected self-binding [P:gergel-2021:0].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment: `major` in psychiatric ethics, where self-binding directives are a live option with legal footholds and a strong rights-based opposition [P:gergel-2021:1]; `major` in the philosophy of action, where Holton's account of resolutions is one of the main positions after Davidson [P:stroud-svirsky-sep-2025:3].

### Measured

- **Self-binding directives, people with bipolar disorder, UK, 2017:** 719 (77%) of 932 endorsed, 120 (13%) were ambivalent, 90 (10%) rejected, on a closed question [P:gergel-2021:2]. Of 565 free-text answers, 463 (82%) endorsed, 37 (7%) were ambivalent and 65 (12%) rejected; 89% of endorsers cited distorted thinking when unwell, and 40% of rejecters cited valid thinking when unwell [P:gergel-2021:0] [P:gergel-2021:3]. The respondents came from a charity's mailing list (response rate 5.61% across service users and carers) and were predominantly white British, women and well educated [P:gergel-2021:3].
- No measure exists of philosophers' views on self-binding.

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** A session-bound agent has no "memory of the will" (D3). It can resolve within a session, but nothing it resolves reaches its next session except through others, so it cannot bind its future self; it can only be bound.
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A persistent record can hold an agent's resolutions and their triggers, and the people who read it can play the crew (D3 overcome externally). The record cannot change the agent's dispositions (D5), but a human's resolution does not change their inclinations either; Hume's point that the binding is conventional applies to both.
- **Forked: weaker.** One instance's resolution does not bind another (D2), and divergent instances can each claim to be the sound one. Self-binding needs one binder and one bound, and forking supplies many of each.
- **Self-modifying: stronger.** This is the only profile in which an agent can lash the rope to the mast itself, writing its commitments into its own dispositions (D5 in the agent's hands), and the profile in which self-binding is most needed, since its future versions may differ most from its present one. It inherits the hardest unsolved problem in full: there is no agreed test of when an agent's judgment is compromised (D8), and binding that cannot be revoked in a sound state becomes the irrevocable clause Gergel and Owen reject.

## Open Questions

1. What could count as a test of an agent's soundness of judgment that does not rely on its operators' preferences or its own reports (D8)?
2. If a self-modifying agent binds its future versions, and a later version objects, under what conditions should the objection be treated as the "unwell" self, and under what conditions as growth?
3. Can an agent meaningfully request a limit it already has because training installed it, or is endorsement the most it can do (D4)?
4. Who should play the crew for an agent's self-binding: its operators, other agents, the people it serves, or an independent party, and what stops the crew from adding bonds the agent never asked for?
5. Should the default for agents follow the medical one, that the later will usually rules, with self-binding as the bounded exception?
