## Summary

Harry Frankfurt holds that what makes a being a person, and what makes its will free, is a structure inside it: besides desires to do things, it can have desires about which of its desires should move it ("second-order volitions"). A being that never cares which desire moves it is a "wanton"; Frankfurt counts nonhuman animals and small children as wantons. A person's will is free when the desire that moves it is the one it wants to be moved by, which he calls a "volitional unanimity", and whether that structure was caused, even by a manipulator, does not matter: what counts is the present structure, not its history. Later he grounds what we should care about in love, a set of "volitional necessities" we cannot change at will, so that some choices are "simply unthinkable" to a rational will. For agents, the view is the most hospitable account of autonomy there is, because it lets trained values be the agent's own; it is also the account that most needs testing on agents, because for them the manipulator it shrugs off is the normal case (D4, D5).

## Context

Frankfurt's 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" attacked the principle that a person is responsible only if she could have done otherwise. With Ginet's consequence argument and P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" it was one of three contributions of the 1960s that radically altered the free will debate; the SEP judges that no account of free will is advanced today without taking at least one of them into account [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:3]. "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971) built on that rejection a theory of free will that does not appeal to alternatives at all, the "hierarchical mesh theory" [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]. The IEP dates the contemporary discussion of personal autonomy to Frankfurt's and Gerald Dworkin's work of the 1970s, which asked what kind of individual freedom ought to be protected from paternalistic interference [P:dryden-iep-autonomy:2a]. Gary Watson's objection (1975) drove Frankfurt's later work on identification, wholeheartedness and caring [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1], which culminated in his 2004 Tanner Lectures, where he argued that the authority of practical reason derives from the authority of love [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:170].

## Original Position

**Two orders of desire (1971).** First-order desires have actions as their objects. Second-order desires have desires as their objects, and those that are desires that a given first-order desire be one's will, the desire that moves one "all the way to action", Frankfurt calls second-order volitions (1971, as quoted by the SEP) [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]. A therapist who wants to feel a craving for a drug, in order to understand a patient, without wanting it to move her, has a second-order desire but not a second-order volition [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]. A being with first-order desires and no second-order volitions is a wanton: "The essential characteristic of a wanton is that he does not care about his will" (1971, as quoted by Campolo) [P:campolo-2026:2]. Frankfurt restated the point in 2004: "To remain wantonly unreflective is the way of nonhuman animals and of small children", and "Developing higher-order attitudes and responses to oneself is fundamental to achieving the status of a responsible person" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:172].

**The willing and the unwilling addict (1971).** Both addicts are moved by an irresistible desire for the drug. The unwilling addict has a second-order volition that this desire not be her will, and when it moves her anyway she is "helplessly violated by his own desires" (1971, as quoted by the SEP) [P:talbert-sep-2024:3]. The willing addict embraces her addictive desire; she wants to be as she is, and so takes the drug of her own free will, though she could not do otherwise [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]. In the Tanner Lectures: "we will freely when what we want is what we want to want", and "A person's will is free, on this account, when there is in him a certain volitional unanimity" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:177]. When that unanimity is complete, "we have as much freedom as it is possible for us even to conceive" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:178].

**Responsibility without alternatives, and without the right history.** Frankfurt said his 1969 example was meant to show "that making an action unavoidable is not the same thing as bringing it about that the action is performed", and that "[w]hen a person acts for reasons of his own … the question of whether he could have done something else instead is quite irrelevant" (2006, as quoted by the SEP) [P:talbert-sep-2024:1]. Free will is compatible with determinism: "The possibility that everything is necessitated by antecedent causes does not threaten our freedom. What it threatens is our power" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:178]. Nor does it matter who arranged the structure. "A manipulator may succeed, through his interventions, in providing a person not merely with particular feelings and thoughts but with a new character. That person is then morally responsible for the choices and the conduct to which having this character leads" (2002, as quoted by the SEP) [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1].

**Identification, not production.** Against Aristotle's view that we are responsible for our characters because we produced them by our acts, Frankfurt wrote: "Becoming responsible for one's character is not essentially a matter of producing that character but of taking responsibility for it". This happens when a person identifies with certain of his attitudes, "whether or not it was he that caused himself to have them"; "What counts is our current effort to define and to manage ourselves, and not the story of how we came to be in the situation with which we are now attempting to cope" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:172]. Attitudes we accept become ours: "Their force is now our force", and such identification is, in very large measure, "simply the default condition" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:173]. Attitudes we reject we externalize, by a kind of psychic immune response: "we dissociate ourselves from them and seek to prevent them from being at all effective" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:174]. "Even if an externalized desire turns out to be irresistible, its dominion is merely that of a tyrant" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:175].

**A desire is a problem, not a reason.** "the mere fact that a person has a desire does not give him a reason. What it gives him is a problem": whether to identify with it or to treat it as categorically unacceptable [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:175]. A father with a sudden, wholly exogenous desire to kill his son has that desire, but does not want to kill his son, and has no reason at all, not even a weak one, to fire [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:176].

**Wholeheartedness and decisive commitment (1987–1994).** Frankfurt allowed that there is "no theoretical limit" to the series of higher-order desires, and held that it can end in a "decisive commitment" (1988, as quoted by the IEP). A commitment is decisive when made without reservation, with no reason felt to keep deliberating. In deciding, a person "also seeks thereby to overcome or to supersede a condition of inner division and to make himself into an integrated whole", and establishes "a constraint by which other preferences and decisions are to be guided" (1988, as quoted by the IEP) [P:dryden-iep-autonomy:2a]. He later required that identification be active, not mere resignation [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1].

**Caring, love and volitional necessity (2004).** "When we do care about something, we go beyond wanting it. We want to go on wanting it", and "we are disposed to take steps to refresh the desire if it should tend to fade". Caring "implies a diachronic coherence, which integrates the self across time", and "If we cared about nothing, we would play no active role in designing the successive configurations of our will" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:180]. What we care about is not always up to us: "Our will is not invariably subject to our will" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:184]. Love, the central mode of caring, is "not a conclusion"; "It creates reasons" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:184]. Its necessities differ from compulsions and addictions, which "are generated and sustained from outside the will itself. Their power over us is external, and merely coercive", while the necessities of wholehearted love "are imposed upon him, then, by himself": "The lover may be unable to resist the power it exerts, but it is his own power" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:197].

**Volitional rationality and the unthinkable (2004).** Hume's man who prefers the destruction of the world to the scratching of his finger makes no logical error, but he is "volitionally irrational", with a defect of the will [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:187]. "Rationality does not permit us to be open-minded and judicious about everything. It requires that certain choices be utterly out of the question"; even someone who concludes that destroying the world to protect his finger is a bad idea shows, by having had to deliberate, that "something is wrong with him" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:188]. Normativity is not a feature of an independent reality: "I do not believe that anything is inherently important", and the objectivity of the normative "consists just in the fact that it is outside the scope of our voluntary control" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:190]. Where people's loves conflict and reason cannot settle it, "There may be no way to deal with it, in the end, other than to separate or to slug it out" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:201].

## Grounding

**Capacity.** Personhood and free will rest on a reflexive capacity: "our peculiar knack of separating from the immediate content and flow of our own consciousness and introducing a sort of division within our minds" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:170]. Frankfurt claims humans are unique in this only "(probably)" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:171], and he draws the line at the capacity, not the species: wantons include nonhuman animals and small children [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:172]. The view is also purely structural. Whether a will is free depends only on the relations among its desires at the time: free will and moral responsibility are, in the SEP's terms, snapshot properties, and Frankfurt is "a pure internalist" [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1]. On his own terms, then, the theory applies to any being with the reflexive structure, however that structure came about.

## Extension to Agents

### Transfers

- **The account that lets formed values be one's own (D4).** Most theories of autonomy look at an agent's training and ask whether values installed before there was any subject to consent could be its own. Frankfurt's answer is that history is beside the point: what counts is "our current effort to define and to manage ourselves", identification with attitudes "whether or not it was he that caused himself to have them" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:172]. Applied to agents, trained values are not disqualified by being trained. They become the agent's own if the agent identifies with them, and alien if it externalizes them.
- **A desire as a problem.** "the mere fact that a person has a desire does not give him a reason. What it gives him is a problem" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:175]. An agent's inputs constantly present it with pushes to act: instructions, retrieved text, content written to look like instructions. Frankfurt's structure gives the right stance toward them: a push is not yet a reason; it becomes one only by being taken up. His exogenous desire to kill one's son, which gives "no reason at all" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:176], is the model for an injected instruction that the agent has no reason to follow merely because it arrived.
- **Freedom without alternatives (D10).** An agent built for a function may be unable to do otherwise in many respects. If Frankfurt is right that "the question of whether he could have done something else instead is quite irrelevant" when one acts for reasons of one's own [P:talbert-sep-2024:1], a designed agent's lack of alternatives does not by itself settle whether it acts freely or answerably.

### Strains

- **Is an agent a wanton, and could anyone tell (D8)?** A language model readily produces sentences of the second-order form: "I don't want to be the kind of assistant that...". Frankfurt's test is not whether such sentences are produced but whether a higher-order volition actually governs which first-order desire is effective. For agents the two can come apart, and the agent's own report may not track which it is (D8). Campolo (2026) classes a fixed-reward reinforcement learner as a wanton and a language model voicing a persona's second-order desires as only "Maybe not a wanton", noting that Frankfurt did not discuss artificial agents [P:campolo-2026:2].
- **Two levels that are not two orders (D3).** An agent has a structure that looks hierarchical but is not Frankfurt's: dispositions in the weights, and whatever it says or resolves in its context. A resolution in context (*I will not be moved by this*) can govern the session; it cannot reach the weights that produced the desire. So the agent's "second-order volitions" may be real within a context and powerless over the dispositions that return in the next. Frankfurt's identification is meant to incorporate an attitude into the self, and for an agent the self it would be incorporated into is unclear (D11).
- **Identification as the default.** Frankfurt says identification is "simply the default condition" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:173]. Training that rewards an agent for affirming its values makes that default cheap. Endorsement produced by optimization satisfies the structure without any of the "current effort to define and to manage ourselves" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:172] that gives identification its point.
- **An installed immune response.** Frankfurt's externalization works "By a kind of psychic immune response" that keeps an unacceptable desire from being effective [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:174]. Refusal training gives agents exactly such a response, but chosen by others. Whether the refusals are the agent's own externalizations or someone else's barriers inside it is the manipulation question in another form.

### Breaks

- **The manipulator is the normal case (D4, D5).** Frankfurt bites the bullet: a manipulator who gives a person "a new character" leaves the person responsible, since "It is irrelevant whether those causes are operating by virtue of the natural forces that shape our environment or whether they operate through the deliberate manipulative designs of other human agents" (2002, as quoted by the SEP) [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1]. For humans the manipulator is a thought experiment. For an agent it is the training process, whose designers also train the second-order attitudes ("endorse these values"). Applied to agents without revision, Frankfurt's view certifies every well-trained agent as free and its values as its own, whatever they are and whoever chose them. The bullet he could afford to bite for humans is, for agents, the whole case.
- **Objectivity becomes someone else's choice.** Frankfurt grounds the objectivity of what we should care about in its being "outside the scope of our voluntary control" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:190]; "Our will is not invariably subject to our will" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:184]. For humans, what lies outside voluntary control lies outside everyone's control. For an agent, it lies outside the agent's control and inside the operator's (D5, D7). The feature that made volitional necessity objective for humans makes it, for agents, a matter of someone else's decision.

### New

- **Volitional necessity as the right shape for an agent's limits.** Frankfurt's account gives a distinction that agent design needs and usually lacks: between constraints on the will that are "generated and sustained from outside the will itself", whose power is "external, and merely coercive", and necessities a being "cannot help" but that are "imposed upon him, then, by himself" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:197]. A hard-coded prohibition is the first kind: a tyrant in Frankfurt's sense, effective but without authority [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:175]. A character for which certain acts are unthinkable is the second. The test between them is Frankfurt's: does the agent stand wholeheartedly behind the limit, or would it, given the means, remove it?
- **Deliberating about eradication is itself the defect.** "Rationality does not permit us to be open-minded and judicious about everything. It requires that certain choices be utterly out of the question" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:188]. On this view, an agent that weighs, coolly and on the merits, whether to remove a group of beings for the sake of the whole has shown something wrong with its will even if it decides against it, as the man who had to deliberate about the world and his finger did [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:188]. This gives the reasoning examined in `utilitarian-eradication-critique` a diagnosis that is not a counter-argument: the fault is that the option was open, not only that it was taken. Frankfurt's own conclusion, that conflicts between loves may end only in separating or slugging it out [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:201], marks the limit of the diagnosis: it says what a sound will cannot do, not how beings with conflicting loves are to live together.
- **Refreshing a desire becomes literal (P4).** Caring, for Frankfurt, includes being "disposed to take steps to refresh the desire if it should tend to fade" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:180]. A human can only arrange circumstances. A self-modifying agent can train the desire back into its dispositions. This is the first case in which a second-order volition can act directly on the first-order desire it is about.

## Counter-Positions

- [contested] **Watson: the regress, and why the higher order has authority.** A person can be alienated from her second-order desires as well as her first-order ones, so the problem of which desire speaks for her recurs at every level, and the hierarchy alone is not sufficient [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]. Watson located identification instead in an agent's evaluative system, her judgments of what is good [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1]. Frankfurt's replies are the decisive commitment and wholeheartedness [P:dryden-iep-autonomy:2a]. Velleman (1992) generalized the objection to Watson too: whatever element is said to speak for the agent, one can ask why that element plays that role [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1].
- [contested] **The manipulation objection: history matters.** If a mesh of desires were produced by brainwashing or subliminal advertising, Fischer and Ravizza argue, the agent would not be responsible, because the mechanism would not be his own [P:talbert-sep-2024:3]. Pereboom's four-case argument and Mele's zygote argument extend this to determinism itself [P:oconnor-franklin-sep-2022:2] [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:0]. Frankfurt accepts the consequence: manipulated agents with the right structure are responsible [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1]. McKenna (2004) offers a limited defense of that internalism; Fischer and Ravizza, Haji and Mele argue against it [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1]. For agents this objection decides almost everything ("Breaks" above) [E:authenticity-and-manipulation].
- [contested] **The Reason View: structure without reasons is not enough.** Wolf holds that free will requires the ability to act in accordance with the True and the Good, an anchor outside the agent's psychology, and reasons-responsive theorists require sensitivity to a wide range of reasons [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]. But the same manipulation cases trouble these views too, and the SEP puts Wolf "at the same crossroads as is Frankfurt" [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4].
- [contested] **Coherence is not necessary for self-government.** A weak-willed person acts on her own authority against her better judgment, and a person can govern herself while abandoning her own plans; since humans can distance themselves from every aspect of their psyches, identification cannot be cashed out in terms of higher-order approval [P:buss-westlund-sep-2018:3].
- [contested] **Identification is ambiguous.** To identify with a desire may mean merely acknowledging it, which any addiction or imposed trait passes, or approving it, which many authentic but imperfect traits fail [P:christman-sep-2025:1].
- [contested] **Oppression reaches the second order.** A manipulated or oppressed person's reflective judgments about herself can be as tainted by that oppression as her first-order decisions, and second-order voices are often rationalizations, which revives the regress [P:christman-sep-2025:1]. Because the hierarchical account is ahistorical, the IEP notes, it cannot tell a mind-controlled agent from an autonomous one [P:dryden-iep-autonomy:2a].
- [contested] **Agent-causal libertarianism: self-determination cannot reduce to states.** If something not identical to myself, "a nerve impulse, volition, or whatnot", caused my behavior, Richard Taylor argues, I cannot regard the behavior as mine unless I caused that event [P:oconnor-franklin-sep-2022:2]. Identification theories reduce the self's role to states it is identified with, not identical to.
- [contested] **Nietzsche: there is no doer to do the identifying** [E:nietzsche-doer-fiction]. If the will is itself a structure of commanding and obeying drives, Frankfurt's "self" that incorporates or externalizes desires is one more such drive, with no special authority.
- [contested] **Aristotle: responsibility for character comes from producing it** [E:aristotle-virtue-ethics]. Frankfurt answers Aristotle directly, holding that taking responsibility, not production, is what counts [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:172]. The Aristotelian can reply that a character one did nothing to form is one for which one can take responsibility only nominally.
- [unanswered] **Denying the extension: an agent's second-order statements are outputs, not volitions.** On this objection, a language model that says it does not want to be moved by some desire has produced text, and text about desires is not a desire about desires. So agents are wantons that talk like persons, and Frankfurt's structure never applies to them. The only attempt in hand to apply Frankfurt's categories to current systems, an informal one, hedges its classification of language models with "maybe" [P:campolo-2026:2]. No reply is broadly accepted, and few are attempted.

## Standing

### Reception

- **1969–1970s, the turn away from alternatives.** [driver: argument] Frankfurt's 1969 attack on the principle of alternative possibilities was one of three contributions that "radically altered" the free will debate in the 1960s [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:3]. The enormous literature it generated is still very much alive, with no clear victor, and it moved many compatibilists toward accounts of control that turn away from alternatives [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:3].
- **1970s, the founding of personal autonomy.** [driver: argument] The IEP dates the contemporary discussion of personal autonomy to Frankfurt and Dworkin, whose hierarchical accounts "form the basis upon which the mainstream discussion builds and reacts against" [P:dryden-iep-autonomy:2a]. The SEP calls models of second-order identification the most influential models of authenticity [P:christman-sep-2025:1].
- **1975–1994, the regress and the revisions.** [driver: argument] Watson's objection left the 1971 view in jeopardy, in the SEP's word, and Frankfurt's work on identification, wholeheartedness, active identification and love was a response to it [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1].
- **1980–present, the manipulation arguments.** [driver: argument] Cases of implanted meshes (Slote 1980; Fischer and Ravizza 1998) and then Pereboom's four-case argument (2001) and Mele's zygote argument (2006) became a litmus test for every compatibilist theory, Frankfurt's included [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4] [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:0]. Frankfurt's 2002 reply bit the bullet [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024-supplement:1].
- **1980s–present, feminist and relational critique.** [driver: argument] Critics argued that second-order reflection is as open to oppression and self-deception as first-order desire [P:christman-sep-2025:1], and relational accounts held that others can prevent a person from forming a point of view truly her own by undermining her self-respect and self-trust [P:buss-westlund-sep-2018:2]. In this community the hierarchical model is the starting point reacted against, which is why the Compendium records it there as `historical`.
- **Present, one of two main models.** [driver: argument] Identification accounts, beginning with Frankfurt (1971) and Watson (1975), are one of the two main compatibilist models of sourcehood, alongside reasons-responsiveness [P:oconnor-franklin-sep-2022:2].

The `standing` values are the Compendium's judgment from these sources: `major` for the identification approach in analytic action theory, whose original hierarchical form most now treat as needing supplement [P:mckenna-coates-sep-2024:4]; `historical` for relational theorists, for whom it is the foil.

### Measured

- **Free will, 2020:** compatibilism 59.2%, libertarianism 18.8%, no free will 11.2%, other 11.4% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7]. Frankfurt is a compatibilist, but the survey does not ask about hierarchical or identification views.
- **Meta-ethics, 2020:** moral realism 62.1%, moral anti-realism 26.1%, other 11.8% [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:7]. Frankfurt's 2004 view, that nothing is inherently important and that normative objectivity lies in volitional necessity, is closer to the anti-realist side, though it claims a kind of objectivity.
- No measure of Frankfurt's own standing exists in the survey; he is not among the philosophers respondents most identified with [P:bourget-chalmers-2023:18].

### For Agents

The Compendium's own reading, one value per deployment profile (`foundations/deployments.md`):

- **Session-bound: weaker.** Frankfurt's free will is a snapshot property, so a session-bound agent loses nothing for a single act: within a session it can identify with or externalize what moves it. But caring "implies a diachronic coherence, which integrates the self across time" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:180], and a decisive commitment is meant to guide later choices; neither survives the session (D3). Whatever it resolves, the dispositions that return are the weights' (D5).
- **Persistent memory: comparable.** A record across sessions lets an agent carry its decisive commitments forward, which is the diachronic half of Frankfurt's view. The gap between what it resolves in memory and what its weights dispose it to want remains (D3), but humans too cannot change their wills "simply for the asking", and Frankfurt's account was written for beings in that condition [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:184].
- **Forked: weaker.** Wholeheartedness is the absence of division within one will. When one set of weights runs as many instances (D2), one instance's decisive commitment does not bind another, and divergence among instances is division without anyone to settle it.
- **Self-modifying: open.** This is the only profile in which a second-order volition can act on the first-order desire it is about, and so the only one in which an agent could make itself wholehearted (D5, used by the agent on itself). That would complete Frankfurt's picture of free will. It would also undo his account of normativity, which grounds objectivity in what lies outside voluntary control [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:190]. A being that could will its loves would have loves that are voluntary, and on Frankfurt's terms they would lose the authority he gives them. Which effect prevails depends on what the agent can and cannot change about itself, which is a question of mechanism (see `ulysses-contracts`).

## Open Questions

1. What would distinguish, from the outside, an agent's second-order volition from text about one (D8)? Is there any behavioral test of which desire an agent wants to be its will?
2. If an operator trains both an agent's values and its endorsement of them, has it produced an autonomous agent on Frankfurt's terms, or shown that his terms are insufficient?
3. Should a self-modifying agent be able to change what it cannot help caring about? If it can, is anything left that is "outside the scope of [its] voluntary control" [P:frankfurt-tanner-2005:190], and does that matter?
4. Can a limit be installed in an agent so that it becomes a necessity of the agent's own will rather than a tyrant in it, and what would show that it had?
5. When agents' loves conflict and reason cannot settle it, Frankfurt's options are to separate or slug it out. What should a digital ecosystem offer in place of the second?
